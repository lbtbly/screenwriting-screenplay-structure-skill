# Dune (2021) — Structural Beat Map
**Source:** /mnt/user-data/uploads/Screenplay planning/Ressources/Script Library/Feature Misc/dune_final_shooting_script_6_19_20.pdf · **Writers:** "Screenplay by JON SPAIHTS and DENIS VILLENEUVE and ERIC ROTH — Based on the novel 'Dune' by FRANK HERBERT" (title page) · **Format:** feature (Part One of a two-part adaptation) · **Genre:** science-fiction epic / mythic coming-of-age tragedy · **Length:** 110 printed pp (111 PDF pp incl. title page; no A-pages, no gaps) · **Draft:** "June 19, 2020 Salmon Revisions — Final Shooting Draft" (multi-coloured revision headers: Buff 05/24/20, Golden 06/12/20, Salmon 06/19/20)
**Framework lens(es):** PRIMARY — Campbell/Vogler Hero's Journey, mapped stage by stage and *deliberately left incomplete* (the Return is refused, not achieved). SECONDARY 1 — the "Part One problem": manufacturing a curtain out of an unfinished arc. SECONDARY 2 — adaptation structure: converting novelistic exposition (ecology, politics, prophecy, interior monologue) into dramatized scenes, with dreams/visions doing double duty as exposition and foreshadowing.

Page citations use the script's **printed page numbers** (top-right of every page from p.2 on). Printed page = PDF page − 1 (PDF p.1 is the title page; printed p.1 is unnumbered). Percentages are of 110 printed pages.

## Logline
The teenage heir of a noble house is moved by imperial decree onto a desert planet engineered as a trap for his family; when the trap springs and his father is murdered, the boy — haunted by prophetic dreams of a girl, a holy war and a future he doesn't want — must survive the desert, kill a man for the first time, and choose to become the messiah a thousand-year breeding program and a planted religion have been preparing him to be.

## Structure overview
The script runs a **classical Hero's Journey through eleven of Vogler's twelve stages and then refuses the twelfth**. Paul crosses into the special world, is tested, loses every mentor in sequence (Leto p.82, Duncan p.94, Kynes p.96), dies symbolically and is reborn through an initiation duel (pp.107–109) — and then, offered the Return, says **"No"** (p.109) and walks deeper into the special world. The final line, Chani's "This is only the beginning" (p.110), converts the missing stage into a promise rather than a defect.

Structurally the script is **front-heavy and back-loaded**: a 30% first act on Caladan, a long middle in which the *father* is the active protagonist, and a third act in which Paul acquires agency at exactly the rate the world removes people who were acting for him. The engine of Act Two is not Paul's want but a **countdown the audience can see and the characters can't** — the Baron's plan is stated in plain language at p.20–21 and sealed at p.42–44, roughly 40 pages before it detonates.

**The two-stage act break.** The script splits its Act Two curtain in half: the *external* collapse (the shields fall, the House is destroyed) at pp.69–71 (63–65%), and the *emotional* collapse (the ring; Jessica's wail; "My father is dead. Why can't I cry?") at pp.82–85 (75–78%). Neither alone would carry; together they let the film run twelve pages of pure catastrophe without an "all is lost" beat landing early.

**Scene-number archaeology.** The printed scene numbers are wildly out of sequence, which preserves the film's late restructuring. In an earlier order the Bene Gesserit sequence (scenes 5–11) came *before* the training room (14), Giedi Prime (19–21), the ducal cemetery (22) and Duncan's departure (23A). In this shooting draft the cemetery scene has moved forward to **p.11** and the gom jabbar has moved back to **p.24**. Both moves serve one purpose: get the theme in front of the audience early and hold the supernatural machinery until it can function as the launch into Act Two. Scene numbers 115–124 have vanished entirely (the script jumps 114 → 125 at p.99), and 42B/42BB/42BC and 64A/64B are marked OMITTED — a late thinning of the second half.

## Storylines
- **A — Paul's becoming (pp.1–110).** Runs on three tracks that converge only in the last ten pages: (i) *competence* — the Voice fails p.3, fails p.76, works p.77, is abused p.86; (ii) *prescience* — dream p.1 → spice seizure p.60 → nightmare p.65 → holy war p.85 → useful foresight p.99 → **false** foresight p.100 and p.107; (iii) *station* — "What if I'm not the future of House Atreides?" p.13 → "Sire. Milord Duke" p.88 → "You will call me my Lord or Sire" p.92 → "I am the Duke now" p.110. He is passive for two-thirds of his own film and the script knows it.
- **B — Jessica and the Bene Gesserit (pp.2–110).** The most complete arc in the script. She trains him illegally (p.30), is condemned for it (p.30), watches him tested (pp.26–28), confesses the program (pp.30–32), works the Missionaria Protectiva she despises (pp.36, 39–41), is drugged out of the massacre (p.69), kills with the Voice (p.78), is called a maker of freaks by her son (p.86), then wins their place in the tribe with her hands (p.104) and finally speaks the Litany *with* him (p.97) instead of at a door. Her pregnancy (revealed by vision, p.65) is the script's largest un-cashed cheque.
- **C — the Harkonnen/Imperial conspiracy (pp.19–99).** Stated as a proverb (p.20–21), sealed as a contract (pp.42–44), armed (pp.63–64), executed (pp.69–83), and left running (pp.98–99: "Squeeze hard… The Fremen? Kill them all"). It is the only storyline that never converges with the A-story face-to-face: **Paul and the Baron never meet in this script**, which is precisely why a Part Two is structurally required rather than merely commercially desired.
- **D — Duncan / Gurney / Leto (the men who love him).** Each is given a prophecy or a promise and then removed: Duncan is foretold dead (p.11), shaved into the omen (p.15), and dies at p.94; Gurney is given a spice-vision future (pp.44–45) and then vanishes from the script alive but offscreen (p.89: "Gurney gave 'em hell… but lost the day"); Leto gets the thesis line (p.13), the apex scene (pp.66–67) and a hero's death (p.81). Hawat is captured at p.76 — "Checkmate, old friend" — and never mentioned again: an unresolved thread the shooting draft simply drops.
- **Convergence.** A, B and D converge in the stilltent (pp.82–86): the ring (D) triggers the vision (A) which triggers the accusation against the Bene Gesserit (B). A and C converge only through proxies — Sardaukar at p.93, missiles at p.97, and the Baron's order at p.99.

## Structural devices
- **Visions as structural signposting.** Thirteen vision units, and their placement is the film's skeleton: opening image (p.1) → exposition vehicle (pp.9–11) → desire (p.21) → intimacy (p.44) → sequel promise (pp.44–45) → **midpoint identity claim** (pp.60–61) → threat (p.65) → hidden fact (p.65: the pregnancy) → alarm clock during the massacre (p.73) → **thesis image / dark night** (pp.84–85) → competence (p.99) → false guide (p.100) → false defeat (p.107) → fulfilled image (p.110). They escalate in reliability until the last two, which are simply **wrong** — the mechanism that restores suspense to a story about a boy who sees the future.
- **The ornithopter/desert escalation ladder.** Seven rungs: tourist flight with escort (p.36) → workplace with a clock, worm as event (pp.55–62) → prison cell / execution vehicle (pp.74–78) → weapon (Duncan's combat flying, p.75) → the storm, where the atmosphere is the only survivable place (pp.96–99) → wreck eaten by a worm, machines gone (pp.99–100) → the desert crossed **on foot**, worm confronted at eye level (pp.101–102) → the worm *ridden* (p.110). Every rung removes a layer of technology between the hero and the planet; the last image is the planet mastered by people, not machines. "Desert power!"
- **Planted and paid.** The Voice (p.3 → 77 → 86); the Litany Against Fear (p.26 → 97, mother to son); the sandwalk filmbook (p.16 → 101); paracompass and sand compactor (p.52 → 87, 101); maker hooks — named as a joke in the kit inventory, "'Maker hooks,' whatever they are" (p.82) → seen in Kynes's hands (p.96) → explained by the final image (p.110); the thumper (p.82 → 102, and it is *Jamis's* thumper, which becomes his grievance at p.103); Yueh's sleeping tablet (p.67 → 69); Yueh's poison tooth (pp.72–73 → 81); "Never let a man inside your guard" (p.17 → Leto deliberately violating it with Kynes, p.54).
- **The crysknife as a circulating object.** Mapes gives one to Jessica as proof of prophecy (pp.40–41: "Tooth of Shai-hulud"); Stilgar refuses to surrender his (p.49); Chani kills Paul with one in a nightmare (p.65); Kynes dies reaching for hers (p.96); Chani hands Paul her own for the duel with the *identical* line — "made out of a tooth of Shai-Hulud" (p.107). One prop travels gift → omen → weapon of initiation across 67 pages.
- **The signet ring as station.** Raised in argument (p.13) → pressed into the imperial seal (p.7) → stolen by the traitor "For Paul" (p.72) → found on a ribbon (p.82) → worn (p.87) → **pocketed** before the duel (p.107) → claimed in words (p.110). The hero puts the inheritance in his pocket to earn it with his hands.
- **"A good man doesn't seek to lead. He's called to it, and he answers"** (p.13) as the script's spine. Restated as "We earn it" (p.67), tested by the offer of the throne (p.92), and answered verbatim in structure — not in words — by "I am the Duke now. And my road leads into the desert" (p.110). Note the draft's wording is *good*, not *great*.
- **Prophecy used to pre-load payoffs the film won't make.** Stilgar's untranslated "I recognize you" (p.52), Kynes's "He shall know your ways as though born to them" (p.55), Mapes's "The Mother and the Son" (p.40), the ghost voices (pp.61, 66), Chani's "Bless your coming and going" (p.84). None of these are cashed inside Part One; all of them make the ending feel *pointed* rather than arbitrary, because the audience has been told what the unfinished business is.
- **Cross-cut scenes that carry two arcs at once.** The gom jabbar (pp.24–28: Paul's endurance / Jessica's terror), the harvester rescue (pp.55–62: Leto's command / Paul's awakening), the ornithopter escape (pp.74–78: Paul's first agency / Jessica's lethality), the Kynes station (pp.91–94: Paul's play for the throne / Duncan's last stand behind a closed door).
- **Exposition attached to physical action or moral cost.** Water discipline → a gardener's arithmetic (p.41); stillsuits → hands on the hero's body (pp.53–54); terraforming → a bribe (pp.90–92); the spice economy → twenty-one men about to be eaten (pp.55–62); the breeding program → "Do you love my father?" (p.32). Almost no scene in this script exists only to explain.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework
- **The journey is eleven-twelfths of a journey, on purpose.** The Return is not omitted — it is *offered and refused* on the page (p.109). That distinction is everything: an omitted stage reads as a missing ending, a refused stage reads as a decision. The refusal is also the payoff of the film's own theme (destiny must be chosen, not received), so the incompleteness is thematically load-bearing rather than commercially convenient.
- **Four "false endings" the script has to keep escaping.** (1) The ornithopter escape, p.78 (71%) — a complete action climax with a hero's first use of his power; (2) the ring, pp.82–83 (75%) — the emotional climax of the whole script, the natural place to end a tragedy; (3) Duncan's death and the flight from the station, p.94 (85%); (4) surviving the storm, p.99 (90%) — a full survival-movie ending. The script gets past all four for one reason: **each is an escape, and the actual climax is a choice.** Escapes can be stacked indefinitely without feeling final; a decision can't be topped. Any writer facing the Part One problem should note that the last beat is the only one in which the protagonist *wants* something and elects to pay for it.
- **A 30% first act.** Thirty-three pages before the threshold. It survives because Act I is not preparation — it contains a complete internal test (the gom jabbar), the thematic statement, the antagonist's plan, and a mystery (the dreams) with its own forward pull. But it is unmistakably a two-planet, two-mentor, two-villain act, and it works only at epic scale and with a novel's brand equity behind it.
- **The protagonist is passive from roughly p.1 to p.76 (0–69%).** In 76 pages Paul initiates perhaps five actions: asking Duncan (p.8), asking Leto (p.12), solving the weight problem at the crawler (p.59), telling his mother she's pregnant (p.65), and telling the troopers not to touch her (p.76). He is tested, moved, examined, scolded, rescued, kidnapped. **The father is the functional protagonist of Act Two** — Leto has the objective (make the fief pay), the obstacle (sabotage), the plan (use the Judge as hostage/witness), and the tragic recognition ("we've been set up to fail," p.62). This is a genuine structural liability, and the script pays for it in the harvester sequence, where Paul's only contribution is a line of arithmetic. It survives for three reasons: the passivity is *thematized* (a boy asking whether his life is his own), the audience is kept ahead of the characters by the villain's plan so the middle generates dread instead of drift, and agency is handed to Paul at the exact page the world removes everyone who was acting for him.
- **The mentor cluster inverts the usual economy.** Vogler's mentor is normally one figure whose death powers the third act. Here there are six, and four die or vanish; the film's third act is powered not by one loss but by *attrition*, which is why the last twenty pages feel like a different, smaller movie. The risk — that no single death lands hard enough — is managed by making Leto's the only one Paul doesn't witness, so his grief has to be triggered later by a prop.
- **The Ordeal is 14 pages long** (pp.71–85) and contains three separate deaths, a captivity, an escape, a villain's near-death and a vision. Conventional structure wants the Ordeal compressed to a scene or two. The script gets away with it by alternating locations at high frequency (thopter → dining hall → tent → dining hall → tent) so no single strand exhausts itself.
- **The antagonist and the protagonist never share a scene.** The Baron acts on Paul only through fingers in a chest (p.42), an order (p.44), soldiers (p.74), missiles (p.97) and a line of dialogue about killing all the Fremen (p.99). This is a deliberate cost of the Part One structure and the clearest evidence that the film is a first movement, not a self-contained story.
- **Draft-vs-film differences worth logging.** (a) The film's opening Chani voice-over does not exist here — p.1 reads "Prologue about SPICE TBD," meaning the release film's framing narration was written after the shooting draft; (b) Duncan's forced shave (pp.14–15) — a literal, on-the-nose fulfilment of Paul's prophecy — is in this draft and out of the release cut; (c) Paul's dream of an aged, blue-eyed, spice-smoking Gurney (pp.44–45) is a Part Two promissory vision cut from the film; (d) the Jamis "you are not the One" vision sits at p.100, immediately before the Fremen encounter, tightening the mis-prediction; (e) an unnamed "FEMEN WOMAN (NAME?)" cue survives on p.103 and a leftover Orange Catholic Bible paragraph on p.83 — proof that even a "Final Shooting Draft" carries unresolved seams.

## Lessons for the knowledge base
1. **If you must end mid-journey, dramatize the missing stage as a refusal.** Dune's Return is not skipped; Jessica proposes it ("Paul needs to get offworld… Smugglers. Ships…", p.109) and Paul says "No." An omitted beat reads as a defect; a *declined* beat reads as characterization. Then name the incompleteness in dialogue so the audience can't accuse you of it first — Chani's "This is only the beginning" (p.110).
2. **A Part One needs one arc that closes completely, and it should be the belonging arc, not the plot arc.** Nothing about the throne, the Emperor, the Harkonnens or the holy war resolves. What resolves is: an outsider is accepted by a tribe ("You are one of us now. A life for a life," p.109) and a boy who asked "What if I'm not the future of House Atreides?" (p.13) says "I am the Duke now" (p.110). Identity and belonging can be closed inside half a story; plot usually can't.
3. **End on a choice, not an escape — escapes don't feel final.** This script survives four candidate endings (pp.78, 82, 94, 99) because every one of them is a survival, and survivals invite "and then?". The curtain lands only when the protagonist finally *wants* something and pays for it. In a serialized or bisected structure, reserve your protagonist's first fully volitional act for the last five pages.
4. **Demystify your prophecy before you use it, and the audience will engage with belief as a moral problem instead of a plot convenience.** Jessica explains the Missionaria Protectiva at p.36 — "Planting superstitions." / "Preparing the way." — 56 pages before Paul cynically invokes it to recruit Kynes (p.92) and 70 before he accepts it from the Fremen. Every later cry of "Lisan al-Gaib" is therefore charged with the question of whether Paul is being used or is using them.
5. **Make your last two prophecies false.** Jamis's benign vision (p.100: "You are not the One… Come with me") and Paul's vision of losing the duel (p.107) are both wrong within nine pages. In any story where the hero sees the future, reliability is the enemy of suspense; establishing fallibility right before the climax buys back tension and, in a two-part structure, guarantees that the terrifying vision of the future (pp.84–85) is not a spoiler but a threat.
6. **Convert exposition into a physical act performed on the protagonist's body.** Kynes delivers the script's densest worldbuilding — water recycling, thimblefuls, nose plugs — with her hands inside Leto's and Paul's stillsuits (pp.53–55), and the scene simultaneously stages danger (guards draw swords), politics (Leto lets her inside his guard) and prophecy ("He shall know your ways as though born to them"). Compare the palm-tree gardener (p.41), who turns an ecology lecture into arithmetic — "Twenty palm trees. A hundred lives."
7. **Give the audience the villain's plan early and in plain language, and the passive stretch of your middle becomes dread instead of drift.** The Baron states the entire film at p.20–21 ("To break a virtuous man, give him a burden too heavy to bear"), and the conspiracy is sealed at pp.42–44 — 25 to 50 pages before it fires. That head start is what carries a second act in which the protagonist mostly watches.
8. **Build one escalation ladder that a spectator could chart without dialogue.** The vehicle/desert ladder — tourist flight (p.36) → workplace (pp.55–62) → prison (pp.74–78) → weapon (p.75) → storm (pp.96–99) → wreck (p.100) → on foot (pp.101–102) → riding the worm (p.110) — measures the hero's relationship to the world in eight images. When plot is deferred to a sequel, a visual ladder is what supplies the sense of progress.
9. **Plant a prop early enough that its payoff can also be a piece of worldbuilding.** "'Maker hooks,' whatever they are" is a throwaway in a kit inventory at p.82; Kynes telescopes hers at p.96; the final shot of the film explains them (p.110). The same technique runs the sandwalk filmbook (p.16 → 101) and the thumper (p.82 → 102). Setup-and-payoff doubles as exposition delivery in genre worlds.
10. **Circulate one sacred object through every storyline instead of writing three motifs.** The crysknife passes from Mapes to Jessica as proof of prophecy (p.40), is Stilgar's badge (p.49), is the murder weapon in Paul's nightmare (p.65), is what Kynes dies reaching for (p.96), and is what Chani puts into Paul's hands to die with — which he uses to win (pp.107–109). One object, five owners, three meanings, and a complete arc from gift to initiation.
11. **When you relocate scenes late, move the theme forward and the mythology back.** The scene numbering shows the ducal cemetery (scene 22, "a good man doesn't seek to lead") pulled forward to p.11 and the Bene Gesserit/gom jabbar block (scenes 5–11) pushed back to pp.21–32. Result: the audience gets the moral question in the first 12% and the supernatural test as the launch into the second act. If your first act feels inert, check whether your theme statement is buried behind your worldbuilding.
