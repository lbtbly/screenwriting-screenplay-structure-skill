## Logline
A middle-aged laundromat owner drowning in tax receipts, an estranged daughter, a father she cannot please and a husband quietly filing for divorce is told in an IRS elevator that she is the only version of herself in the entire multiverse who can stop an omniversal being from collapsing all of existence into a bagel — and discovers that the being is her daughter, that the weapon is not kung fu but kindness, and that the only universe worth saving is the worst one she could have ended up in.

## Structure overview
The script's coherence problem is stated by its own antagonist on p.98 (78%): "A lifetime of fractured moments, contradictions, and confusion... With only a few specks of time where anything actually makes sense." The screenplay solves it with four disciplines applied ruthlessly.

**One, it front-loads eleven pages of pure kitchen-sink realism.** Nothing supernatural happens until p.16 (13%). Every asset the multiverse later spends — the receipt frozen between two piles (p.1), the googly eyes (p.9), the divorce papers (p.4), "you're getting fat" (p.14), Waymond's cookies for the auditor (p.10), the karaoke machine (pp.1, 21), Debbie the Dog Mom and Rick the creepy regular (pp.8–9), the old musical on the laundromat TV (p.11) — is planted before the premise arrives. The fantasy has nothing of its own to introduce; it only re-uses.

**Two, it teaches exactly one new rule every five to eight pages** and never repeats a lesson. The audience is always being taught rather than waiting, which is what lets "Fun and Games" run for 47 pages without stalling.

**Three, it makes absurdity and sincerity inversely proportional.** The more ridiculous the vessel, the more sincere the content: the two most emotionally naked sequences in the script are two rocks in a field (pp.99–101) and a woman with hot dogs for fingers wiping a lover's tears with her toes (pp.107–109).

**Four, it changes the *ordering logic* of the universes at the exact structural hinge.** In Part 1 universes are retrieved by tactical need — the Alphaverse literally runs a targeting console ("She jumped somewhere: brute force. Sumo wrestler? Body builder?" / "Counter with someone agile," p.41). From p.85 onward universes are cut together by emotional rhyme, and the home reality is demoted from "ORIGINAL UNIVERSE" (its slug on pp.50–63) to just another labelled world, "ACTION UNIVERSE," co-equal with "TAXES UNIVERSE" (the universe where she finished her taxes). By the last three pages the slugs drop away entirely and we are back to bare "INT. APARTMENT — MORNING" (p.123). The slugline system is itself the structure: label proliferation is the chaos, label removal is the cure.

Note how badly balanced the parts are on purpose: 56% / 30% / 1.6%. They are not acts, they are **modes** — accumulate, superimpose, integrate — and the third mode needs only two pages because the whole film has been arguing that integration is small and quiet. The film's own title card arrives dead last, on p.125 after CUT TO: BLACK.

Key turns: **catalyst p.16 (13%)**, **break into two p.29 (23%)**, **midpoint pp.63–64 (50–51%)** (the bagel revealed: "Nothing matters"), **all is lost pp.82–83 (66%)** (Evelyn dies; "THE END"), **dark night pp.99–101 (79–81%)** (the rocks), **break into three pp.102–105 (82–84%)** (Waymond's "Be kind"), **finale pp.106–123 (85–98%)**, **final image pp.124–125 (99–100%)**.

**Overall fit verdict:** the fit is close to astonishing. Eleven of fifteen beats land within three percentage points of Snyder's prescription and three land exactly (Break into Two at 23%, Midpoint at 50%, Final Image at 100%). Only two beats deviate materially — Fun and Games runs 14 points long and Break into Three arrives 6 points late — and both deviations serve the same purpose, which is to buy the maximalist middle enough room to teach its own rules. The famous formlessness is a *surface* property: the content of each beat is unrecognisable (the All Is Lost is an actual death, the Dark Night is a rock, the climax is a hug), but the load-bearing geometry underneath is orthodox to the page. That gap between radical content and conventional placement is the single most useful thing this script has to teach.

## Storylines
- **A — Evelyn and Joy / Jobu Tupaki (the spine).** Opens as a comic irritation (pp.4–14: the meal for three, the "good friend," "you're getting fat"), becomes the sci-fi plot at p.35 (Jobu is Joy) and is revealed as the *cause* of the sci-fi plot at pp.52–53 ("You saw her potential, so you pushed her... beyond her limit"). Its true wound is withheld until p.92 — the parked car on the highway where Joy came out and Evelyn said nothing. It resolves in three movements: refusing to kill her (p.70), refusing to repeat her own father ("I am no longer willing to do to my daughter, what you did to me," p.116), and choosing her with full knowledge of every alternative (pp.120–121). Terminal image: a head on a shoulder in a parking lot (p.122).
- **B — Evelyn and Waymond (the marriage).** Runs almost entirely in objects and interruptions: the divorce papers revealed to the audience at p.4 and to Evelyn at p.30, the trip she refuses (p.16), the instructions written on the back of the papers (p.17), the Taxes-universe conversation she keeps being yanked out of (pp.45–47), the Movie Star reunion (pp.54–55), the kiss that never lands as Alpha Waymond dies (p.81), the papers signed at the party (p.96), the stabbing (p.98). It is the B story that supplies the film's solution — "This is how I fight" (p.104) — which is the classic Snyder function executed at full strength. Resolves silently (the papers torn, p.123) and physically (the awkward kiss at the elevator, p.125).
- **C — Gong Gong, the laundromat and the audit.** The pressure system: the party, the lien, the 6pm deadline (p.27), the seizure (p.96). Deirdre is its antagonist and Gong Gong its judge; Alpha Gong Gong is the same judgement given a wheelchair and a gun (pp.64–71, 114). Note that this plot is never *won*: the extension is granted because Waymond was kind to Deirdre (p.96, "I don't know, I just talked to her"), Gong Gong's acceptance is two words ("Hao ba," p.119), and the last scene is a fresh list of infractions (p.126).
- **Convergences.** Four, all late and all deliberate: **p.96** (C solved by B's method — kindness gets the extension); **pp.107–109** (C's antagonist converted by A's method — Evelyn loves Deirdre in three universes at once); **p.116** (A and C fuse in one sentence: "Ba, this is Becky. This is Joy's girlfriend," which is also what physically frees Evelyn from Alpha Gong Gong's grip); **pp.122–125** (all three resolve in adjacent beats — Joy on the shoulder, the papers torn, the audit resumed).

## Structural devices
- **Verse-jumping as a comic escalation ladder.** The jumping pads are the script's metronome, and they climb in four graded registers. *Mildly odd* — shoes on the wrong feet (p.20), imagining a closet (p.21). *Physically unpleasant* — eating a chapstick whole (p.31), four paper cuts between the fingers (p.41), hand sanitiser in the eyes (p.72), swallowing a frog paperweight (p.77), two litres of orange soda in two seconds (p.78). *Socially humiliating* — professing love to your attacker (pp.44–48), wetting yourself (p.61), bird calls (p.72), eating a man's snot (p.72). *Transgressive* — a vibrating feather up the nose (p.76), caressing your father's leg with your foot (p.79), and the Auditor of the Month trophy escalating to Auditor of the YEAR (p.79). Then, crucially, the ladder is **abandoned**: after the bagel montage Evelyn stops using pads entirely (pp.109–113) and simply POPs objects into kindnesses. The removal of the mechanic is how the script signals that she has surpassed the system that recruited her.
- **The rule-teaching schedule.** One new rule per sequence and never a repeat: the jump (p.21) → consciousness only, bodies stay (p.22) → infinite branching bubbles (p.36) → you borrow skills, memories *and emotions* (p.37) → jumps need improbable pads (p.41) → distance costs mental energy; the Stochastic Path Algorithm (p.44) → the clay pot cracks and leaks (pp.51–52) → an overloaded mind fractures into Jobu (pp.52–53) → the worst life has the most branches (p.66) → deliberate overload as strategy (p.71) → *retraction*: there was never a villain (pp.90–91) → every Joy is Jobu (p.92) → the final rule, invented by the protagonist and stated by a rock: "There are no rules!" (p.110). The audience is never idle, and the last rule cancels the first ten.
- **The everything bagel as image-of-theme.** Seeded three times before it means anything: a sharpie circle on Deirdre's forehead (p.26), a stapled post-it (p.40), and a catering bagel with cream cheese eaten in ecstasy by Alpha Waymond (p.52). Revealed at the exact midpoint (pp.63–64) as an object made by putting *everything* on one thing until it collapses — a black hole shaped like a hole. Its argument is stated as relief, not menace: "If nothing matters, then all of the pain and guilt that you have for making nothing of your life, it goes away too." Its final meaning is a suicide note (p.101). The title of Part 1 is literally the recipe for the bomb.
- **The googly eye as the counter-image.** A circle with something inside it, opposed to a circle with a hole. Introduced as an irritation (p.9, "No more google eyes!"), returned as a smile on her own weapon at the moment of Waymond's thesis (p.104: "There are two googly eyes stuck to it. It's smiling at her"), weaponised at the climax when bullets POP into googly eyes and she wears one as a third eye (p.110), and finally attached to a rock in a dead universe (p.110). It is the only object in the script that appears in the prologue, the theme statement, the climax and the abstract universe.
- **The rocks scene as engineered stillness.** Placed at 79–81%, at maximum narrative pressure, the script stops entirely: two static objects, no cutting, no jokes for a page, and the theme in plain speech ("We're all small and stupid"). Because the rocks cannot move or emote, the sequence is pure text — and because it is absurd, the sincerity cannot curdle. It is then re-entered four more times (pp.110, 117, 119–120, 122), so a scene of total stasis becomes the film's most reliable emotional index: first Evelyn wiggles, then a chase, then a cliff, then contact.
- **The hot-dog universe as emotional rhyme.** Generated by a child's drawing of "a man with wiggly worm fingers" on Evelyn's wall (p.61), landed as failure and disgust (p.62: "It's wrong. SO WRONG!"), used to stage the old laundromat musical from p.11 in grotesque form (p.62), and finally made the vessel of the film's most tender act (pp.107–109) and one of its curtain calls (p.122). The film's argument — that meaning is chosen, not found — is proven structurally on its dumbest joke.
- **Absurdity calibrated against sincerity, as a rule.** Every peak of silliness is immediately adjacent to a peak of sincerity, in strict alternation: the dildo/luchador rampage (p.58) → "Her name is Joy. A beautiful name" (p.59); the butt-plug trophies (p.79) → Alpha Waymond's death (pp.80–81); Spaghetti Baby Noodle Boy screaming (p.85) → "I did it" (p.85); Raccacoonie under Chad's hat (p.111) → "We are all useless alone" (p.113). Inversely, the two scenes carrying the most sincerity — the rocks and the hot-dog hug — are set in the two most ridiculous universes. Nothing is played straight and sincere at the same time.
- **Split screen used, then discarded.** Split screen appears only in Part 1 (pp.22–26, 45–46, 76), where its job is to teach the audience that a person can be in two places and that the two scenes comment on each other. Once that literacy exists, the device is dropped and the same effect is achieved by cutting alone (pp.87–88, 94–99, 116–118). A teaching device with a scheduled retirement.
- **The receipt, the mirror, the musical and the song.** Four objects carry the whole arc without exposition: the receipt frozen between two piles (p.1 → splits into two hands at p.86 → picked up off the IRS floor at p.95); the round mirror that frames the family (p.1) and Evelyn's distorted reflection in the elevator door before the punch (p.29); the old musical on the laundromat TV whose dancing lovers give Evelyn "a flash of longing" (p.11) and return with hot dog fingers (p.62); and "Barbie Girl" (p.1 → p.123), whose closing lyric — "Life is your creation" — is the film's thesis sung by a Chinese church choir in harmony.
- **Sub-scene numbering as a formal instrument.** The script's own numbering registers the structure: single scenes in Part 1 (12, 13, 14), fragment-clusters at the midpoint (14-1 through 14-19), and by the bagel montage a single scene number carrying twenty-five fragments (93-1 … 93-25) and then eleven interleaved combat fragments (118-1a … 118-1k). The page furniture visibly disintegrates in step with the protagonist.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework
- **The running time.** 125 pages, of which Fun and Games occupies 47 (38% of the script). This is the deviation that would kill most scripts. Three things pay for it: the *curriculum* (a new rule every 5–8 pages), the *comic contract* (each new universe must be funnier than the last, and the pads escalate on a visible ladder), and the fact that the section is doing double duty — every combat universe introduced in pp.72–80 is a relationship that will be paid off in pp.107–113. Nothing in the long middle is only spectacle.
- **The three parts are not acts and are grotesquely unequal.** Part 1 runs pp.15–85 (56%), Part 2 pp.86–123 (30%), Part 3 pp.124–125 (1.6%). Read as acts this is a failure; read as *modes* it is precise. Everything = accumulation (one privileged reality, universes fetched as tools). Everywhere = simultaneity (no privileged reality, cutting on emotional rhyme, sluglines multiplying). All at Once = integration (no labels, one room, two pages) — and the joke of the third card is that "all at once" turns out to mean *sitting still in a single tax meeting*. The title cards also refuse to mark the act breaks: Part 1 lands at the end of the Set-Up (12%), Part 2 at the start of the Dark Night approach (69%), Part 3 *after* the climax and resolution (99%).
- **Five endings.** (1) p.82–83: Evelyn dies; "CUT TO: BLACK / THE END" at 66%. (2) p.99: she surrenders and becomes a rock, 79%. (3) pp.119–120: Joy walks away and Evelyn says "Okay," 95%. (4) pp.121–123: eight universes fade to black in sequence, then the karaoke finale, 97–98%. (5) pp.124–125: the coda. **Do they work?** Yes, and for a reason worth stealing: each false ending is the ending of a *different genre*, and each is followed by an escalation of intimacy rather than an escalation of plot. The action movie's tragic death gives way to an arthouse dissolution; the arthouse dissolution gives way to a family drama's estrangement; the estrangement gives way to a musical's finale; the musical gives way to a comedy's shrug. Nothing is repeated, so nothing feels like a stall. The risk is real — ending (4)'s eight fade-outs are the point at which an unsympathetic reader checks the page count — but it is mitigated by giving each fade-out to a *different* payoff, so the sequence reads as a curtain call rather than a delay.
- **The villain is not defeated and the climax has no violence in it.** Jobu is a suicide (p.101), the mission was a lie (p.90), and the final action is Evelyn reaching into the bagel and pulling her daughter out (p.122). The Alphaverse's entire apparatus — the tribunal, the jumpers, the algorithm — is simply abandoned rather than beaten. This leaves an orthodox problem unsolved on the page: there is no antagonist left to lose in the last twenty pages, so the script has to manufacture the "high tower surprise" out of a character choice (Joy walking away at 95%) instead of an opposing force. It works, but it works because the A story was seeded from page 4.
- **The protagonist's arc runs through her own villainy.** From p.71 ("Just like her...") to p.99 ("The transformation is complete. She is finally everywhere"), Evelyn is pursuing the antagonist's method, and at pp.94–99 she does everything the villain would do — insults her auditor, abandons the party, signs the divorce papers, stabs her husband. Most frameworks locate the protagonist's worst act at the All Is Lost; here it comes 30 pages *after* her death, in the Dark Night, and it is not a mistake but the completion of her stated plan.
- **The document itself.** This is a locked, post-release shooting script (PDF title "EEAO Shooting Script 11_2_2022"), so it tracks the finished film very closely rather than showing a developmental draft. Its traces of production are still legible and worth noting: scenes 68, 104 and 105 are omitted from the numbering while A91 and A107 are inserted; the description slips into calling the character "Michelle" — the actor's name — twice in the finale (pp.112, 114) at the two moments where Evelyn is most physically heroic; and the googly eyes are spelled "google eyes" in the prologue (p.9) and "googly" everywhere after. There is no useful draft-vs-release divergence to report here, which is itself the finding: what looks improvised on screen is fully specified on the page, down to "It's an orgy of stupid" (p.76) and "Everything is suddenly a lot cuter" (p.110).

## Lessons for the knowledge base
1. **Buy your maximalism with a realist prologue.** Fourteen pages (11%) with zero fantasy in them fund 110 pages of chaos, because every later spectacle is built from an object already loved: googly eyes (p.9 → p.110), a receipt (p.1 → p.86), "you're getting fat" (p.14 → p.120), a karaoke machine (p.1 → p.123), a dog in a pram (p.8 → p.112). The rule: **in a maximalist structure, introduce no new nouns after the premise arrives.**
2. **Teach one rule per sequence, and schedule the retraction.** The script delivers eleven distinct rules between pp.21 and 92 at a steady 5–8 page interval, which is what lets a 47-page Fun and Games hold. More importantly it plans the moment (pp.90–91) when the mentor revokes the mission — "there is no evil villain really" — so the third act cannot be won by the tools of the first. **If your world has rules, decide on page one which of them is a lie.**
3. **Make the accidental universes the important ones.** Evelyn aims for a fighter and lands in her own kitchen (p.45); she aims for anything and lands off the map with hot dogs for fingers (p.62). Those two mistakes become the co-lead reality of Part 2 and the vehicle of the climax's central act of grace. In a story about infinite options, **the branches the protagonist chooses are plot; the branches she falls into are theme.**
4. **Calibrate absurdity and sincerity inversely, and never run both at once.** The dildo fight is followed by "Her name is Joy. A beautiful name" (pp.58–59); the butt-plug trophies are followed by the mentor's death (pp.79–81); and the two most sincere scenes in the script are set in the two silliest universes (two rocks, pp.99–101; hot-dog fingers, pp.107–109). **The more ridiculous the vessel, the plainer the language should be inside it** — sincerity delivered through absurdity is protected from sentiment, while sincerity delivered straight after absurdity reads as sentiment.
5. **Build an escalation ladder with visible rungs, then retire it.** The jumping pads climb odd → painful → humiliating → transgressive across 60 pages, so the audience can feel the altitude. Then Evelyn stops needing them entirely (pp.109–113). **Removing a mechanic is a stronger act-three signal than upgrading it**, because it tells the audience the protagonist has outgrown the system that recruited her.
6. **Give each supporting character one satellite universe and pay them off in a round.** Deirdre owns hot dogs (p.107), Chad owns the raccoon (p.113), Debbie owns her son (p.112), the Master owns the cookie (p.111), Gong Gong owns the opera (p.122), Waymond owns the movie star (p.102). Three pages of finale close five relationships because each was pre-assigned a world. **In a sprawling cast, allocate territory early so the ending can be a sequence of returns rather than a sequence of explanations.**
7. **Let the formatting carry the structure.** The prime reality goes unnamed (pp.1–49), then "ORIGINAL UNIVERSE" (pp.50–63), then merely "ACTION UNIVERSE" alongside "TAXES UNIVERSE" (from p.85), then unlabelled again (p.123). Scene numbers fragment from `12` to `93-1…93-25` to `118-1a…1k` and then resolve back to `123`, `124`, `125`. **The reader's experience of the page can enact the protagonist's mental state without a line of description.**
8. **Site your stillest scene at your loudest moment.** The rocks (pp.99–101, 79–81%) contain no cutting, no jokes for a full page, and the theme in plain speech — placed precisely where the plot is most frantic. Then re-enter it four times so stasis becomes a measurable emotional index. **A maximalist structure needs one location where nothing can happen, and it should arrive at the Dark Night.**
9. **Make the false endings genre-changes, not repetitions.** Five terminal beats (pp.83, 99, 120, 123, 125) survive because each closes a different film — action tragedy, arthouse dissolution, family estrangement, musical finale, comic shrug — and each is followed by an escalation of intimacy rather than of plot. **A false ending stalls only when the next beat raises the same stakes; it lands when the next beat lowers them.**
10. **Radical content, orthodox placement.** Eleven of fifteen Save the Cat beats land within three points of prescription, three land exactly, and the two large deviations both buy room for rule-teaching. The film reads as formless because its beats are *unrecognisable* (the All Is Lost is a literal death and an end card, the climax is a hug, the Final Image is a tax meeting), not because they are misplaced. **If you want to break the surface of a form, keep its skeleton exact — the audience's sense of shape is what makes the strangeness legible as meaning rather than noise.**
