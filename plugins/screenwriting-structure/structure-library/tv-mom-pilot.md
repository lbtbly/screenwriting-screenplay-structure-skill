# Mom 1x01 "Pilot" (2013) — Structural Beat Map
**Source:** /mnt/user-data/uploads/Screenplay planning/Ressources/Script Library/TV Pilots/Comedy/mom-101-pilot-2013.pdf · **Writers:** Chuck Lorre, Eddie Gorodetsky & Gemma Baker (title page: "Written by") · **Format:** multi-camera network sitcom pilot (CBS) — cold open + two acts + tag · **Genre:** multi-cam dramedy (addiction/recovery family comedy) · **Length:** 47 printed pages (48 PDF pages incl. title page); multi-cam spacing — double-spaced dialogue, single-spaced ALL-CAPS action, underlined sluglines, cast list under each slug; **WRITERS DRAFT, November 27, 2012**
**Framework lens(es):** PRIMARY — multi-camera sitcom anatomy (cold open, lettered scenes, act-outs, tag; printed structure mapped exactly). SECONDARY 1 — the dramedy hybrid inside a multi-cam frame: the tonal switching mechanism, hold-length, and release rules that let joke-per-page format carry addiction, teen pregnancy and absent fathers. SECONDARY 2 — the mother-daughter engine: mutual resentment plus mutual need as a renewable weekly conflict, and how the pilot proves it is an engine and not a premise.

Page citations use the script's **printed page numbers, 1–47**, shown top-right with the act/scene code in parentheses directly beneath (e.g. `24.` over `(I/J)`); PDF page = printed page + 1. No A-pages, no revision colours, no asterisks — a clean writers' draft, so the numbering is continuous and unrevised. Percentages are of 47 printed pages.

## Logline
A waitress 118 days sober in Napa Valley — the wine capital of the universe — discovers that getting sober means having to feel everything she drank to avoid: a teenage daughter repeating her exact mistakes, two deadbeat fathers, a married boss she sleeps with, and the newly clean, relentlessly cheerful mother she blames for all of it, who has just walked into her AA meeting and will not go away.

## Structure overview
The printed architecture is the classic Chuck Lorre multi-cam shape: **COLD OPEN → MAIN TITLES → ACT ONE → ACT TWO → TAG → END OF SHOW.** Scenes are lettered continuously across the show (not restarted per act), and the letter is carried in the page header as an act/scene address — `(CO)`, `(I/A)`, `(II/K)`, `(TAG)` — so any page in the script announces its own structural position. Ten lettered scenes plus the cold open and the tag = **12 scene units**.

The two acts are **exactly equal at 17 pages each** — but they are built in opposite ways, and that asymmetry is the pilot's central architectural move:

- **Act One = 7 scenes averaging 2.4 pages.** Breadth. A rapid tour that installs every relationship in Christy's life — daughter, son, lover/boss, job, program — one short scene each.
- **Act Two = 3 scenes averaging 5.7 pages,** including one **8-page two-hander**. Depth. Everything installed in Act One collides in three long confrontations.

Establish, then collide. Act One is a montage of obligations; Act Two is the bill coming due.

The day/night designations do real structural work. **NIGHT 1** covers the cold open through Scene D (pp.1–19). **DAY 2** then runs unbroken from Scene E to the end of Act Two (pp.20–43) — **24 consecutive script pages, more than half the episode, in a single catastrophic day**, with each scene handing off "MOMENTS LATER" / "SHORT TIME LATER" / "LATER THAT SAME DAY." The Tag jumps to **NIGHT 3**, which is the point: the new status quo is shown on a *different* night, so it reads as permanent rather than as the tail of one bad day.

Scene letters run **A B C D E H J K L M** — F, G and I are skipped. Skipping `I` is standard multi-cam practice (confusable with the numeral 1); the F/G gap is the format's other habit — letters are production addresses on a call sheet, not a continuous outline, and gaps are left or retired rather than re-lettered so that scheduling never shifts under a rewrite.

**Transition grammar.** `RESET TO:` (pp.3, 9, 23, 44) is the multi-cam-specific move — the audience stays put while the stage area is redressed within one scene unit; `CUT TO:` links contiguous scenes (A→B, E→H, H→J); `DISSOLVE TO:` marks the three real time gaps (D→E, K→L, L→M). `FADE OUT...` closes Act One, `FADE OUT.` closes Act Two and the show.

| 46–47 | Tea and truce | "Is there anything you wanna say to me?" / "I love you with all my heart." / "And?" / "I appreciate your apology." | **Never released.** The show ends with the wound open | Only the doorbell rescues the scene — and the final button ("Married. What a whore.") is aimed away from the wound, not at it |

**The four rules the pivots obey.**
1. **The joke comes after the pain, never on top of it.** In almost every pivot, the painful line gets its own complete speech; the release arrives in the *next* speech. Pain and punchline in the same breath would read as flinching.
2. **The release usually comes from the listener, not the sufferer.** Roscoe's "I'm not good anyway" (p.17) is released by Christy; Christy's "Is that all I am?" (p.6) is released by Gabriel; Christy's "empty house" (p.33) is released by Bonnie's topper. The exceptions — the cold open and the AA share — are the two places where self-deprecation is being characterised *as Christy's defence mechanism*, which is a choice, not a flinch.
3. **The hold is capped at one speech unless a device buys more.** The three longest holds all sit inside licensed containers: the AA podium (p.25, ~15 lines), the staged group look (p.42), and the Tag's final exchange (pp.46–47) where the show is ending anyway and has nothing left to protect.
4. **The joke almost never undercuts the pain — it re-aims it.** Test case: "While other mothers were cooking dinner, you were cooking meth" (p.33) is funny *and* it makes the accusation worse, because Bonnie's topper ("Otherwise known as working") confirms it. The one genuine undercut is the phone-call forgiveness (pp.42–43), where a real act is performed insincerely to win an argument — and the script immediately marks it: "You are so lame."

## Pilot audit
**CHRISTY** (intro p.1: "A PRETTY, THIRTY-SOMETHING WAITRESS"). Waitress in a Napa fine-dining room (p.1); in visible collapse (pp.1–6); in debt to credit cards, a loan shark and a bookie (pp.4–5); failed at school, marriage and "being a lesbian" (p.7); made soft-core porn films that live forever online (p.7); two children by two fathers (p.7, p.41); sleeping with her married boss (pp.18–19, p.35); got pregnant with Violet as a teenager (p.14); **118 days sober** (p.24); has a mug shot, taken in her mother's hoodie (p.25); has a younger brother who is thriving (pp.25, 32); estranged from her mother for a couple of years (p.27). **Want:** to be more than "a very good waitress" (p.6). **Need:** to stop blaming her mother (p.9) long enough to be forgiven by her daughter (p.42). **Flaw made visible:** she is her mother, and says so out loud at p.25 without changing anything.

**BONNIE** (intro p.26: "AN ATTRACTIVE, MIDDLE-AGED WOMAN"). Characterised in absentia at pp.7–8 before she appears. First line is a dig (p.26). Two years clean; steady job, exercise, book club, church, azaleas, pays taxes (p.30). Priors: hashish smuggled in a diaper, blackouts with lit cigarettes, a motorcycle stolen from a coke dealer (p.8), cocaine off a shag carpet (p.31), cooking meth (p.33). Predatory toward young waiters (pp.29, 34, 47). Forgives Christy unasked (p.33) and will not apologise (p.47). **The key design choice:** she is in the same recovery program (p.30) and already Facebook friends with Violet (p.32) — she cannot be excluded.

**VIOLET** (intro p.12: "BEAUTIFUL, SIXTEEN-YEAR-OLD DAUGHTER"). Sexually active with Luke (pp.11, 17, 40); a fluent liar (pp.12–13); does her homework and parents her little brother (pp.12, 44); in love and says so (p.41); knows her mother was absent even when present ("You only started noticing when you stopped drinking," p.41); **states the show's thesis** (p.42); allied with her grandmother, who she calls "Bonnie" (pp.32, 45). She is Christy at sixteen, and the script never says so — it just shows her repeating the behaviour.

**ROSCOE** (intro p.16, nine years old). Sleeps in his mother's bed (p.16); waited up for her (p.16); informant on his sister (p.17); gets the talent-show date wrong and shrugs, "It's okay. I'm not good anyway" (p.17); already fluent in his father's absence (p.43). The show's conscience and its most reliable source of unearned pain.

**GABRIEL** (intro p.3: "RESTAURANT MANAGER, A GOOD-LOOKING MAN IN HIS THIRTIES"). Her boss (p.3), her confessor (pp.3–9), her lover (pp.18–19), and married to Claudia, whose father owns the restaurant (pp.35, 40) — so the affair is also her job security and the reason she cannot quit. Two functions in one body: exposition valve and trap.

**BAXTER** (intro p.37: "GOOD-LOOKING, SCRUFFY, THIRTY-SOMETHING DAD"). Roscoe's father; six months behind on child support (p.37); wants $2,000 to buy two kilos of "Sonoma kush" (p.39); propositions his ex ("I was hopin' for sex with the ex," p.38). Establishes the second absent father — and by omission, the third: Violet's father is never named, referenced only as "the one in the bedroom sleeping with this idiot is not even your kid" (p.41), and Christy's own father is never mentioned at all. **Three generations of absent men, two of them established purely by silence.**

**GREG** (intro p.27). The waiter. Structurally the most interesting minor character in the pilot: an oblivious third party who interrupts the 8-page mother-daughter fight roughly every 1.5 pages (pp.27, 28–29, 29, 30–31, 31, 34), then pays off in the Tag as Bonnie's date (p.47) — a comic device promoted to plot.

**THE SERIES ENGINE.** Sobriety is the problem, not the solution. Christy got sober and now has to feel it all: a daughter repeating her mistakes, a son she keeps failing, two useless fathers, a job she can only keep by sleeping with her boss, and a mother she cannot forgive and cannot escape. The renewable weekly conflict is engineered into the machinery rather than left to character choice — mother and daughter are **in the same program** (p.30), so they will keep meeting in church basements whether or not they reconcile; and the grandmother already has an independent line to the granddaughter (p.32) and, by p.44, a place at the sink. Christy's "I don't want you in my life" (p.34) is structurally unenforceable, which is exactly what a series needs.

## Storylines
- **A — Christy vs. Bonnie (the mother-daughter engine).** pp.7–8 (built in absentia), 14 (the inherited sea-turtle speech), 24–26 (AA reveal / act-out), 27–34 (the eight-page fight), 36 ("this is my mother's fault"), 42–43 (performative forgiveness), 44–47 (Tag: installed, then the withheld apology). **~9 beats over roughly 24 of 47 pages.** Owns both act-outs and the final button.
- **B — Christy vs. Violet (the same story one generation down).** pp.7 (reported), 10–11 (Luke out the window), 12–15 (the confrontation that becomes the motif), 17 (Roscoe's report), 32–33 (Violet has been talking to Bonnie), 40–43 (the confrontation and the thesis line), 44–45 (Tag: Violet has defected). **~7 beats.** Locked to the A-story by a single line at p.42.
- **C — Christy & Gabriel (the affair / the job).** pp.3–9 (established as confidant), 18–19 (established as lover), 35–36 (the wife), 40–41 (his excuse: "Her father owns the restaurant"). **~4 beats, ~13 pages.** The runner that keeps her economically trapped.
- **Runner 1 — Christy & Roscoe (the guilt engine).** pp.16–17, 20–23, 43. **3 beats.** Generates the Act One turn ("Oh god, I need a drink," p.23).
- **Runner 2 — Baxter (the deadbeat father).** pp.37–40, 43. **2 beats.** Exists to prove the men are not coming back.
- **Runner 3 — the self-help CD.** p.10 planted, p.21 paid. **2 beats.** The pilot's only conventional joke runner.

## Structural devices
- **The crying-in-the-restaurant cold open (pp.1–9).** A "malfunctioning professional" opening: the character performs a demanding job flawlessly while visibly disintegrating, and the comedy lives in the gap between the two registers. It does four jobs at once — states the premise as an image, runs three escalating table gags, dumps the entire backstory through a confidant's interrogation, and ends on the false thesis that the episode will test. It is **nine pages, 19% of the script**, and its button is a pure character joke rather than a story turn, because MAIN TITLES supplies the structural break.
- **The AA meeting as confession device (pp.24–26).** The format's answer to a problem it cannot otherwise solve: multi-cam cannot flash back, so trauma has to be *spoken*. The podium converts exposition into a scene with an audience, a status game and a punchline structure. Critically, **the laughter is written into the script** — `THE GROUP APPLAUDS` / `THE GROUP LAUGHS` (pp.24, 25, 26) — so the on-stage group models the studio audience's permission to laugh at addiction, and the writers can hold a serious speech far longer than anywhere else in the episode.
- **The sober-time counter.** "118 days" (p.24), repeated in the same breath as "the worst 118 days of my life"; Bonnie's "two years clean and sober myself" (p.30). The counter functions as a clock (early sobriety = maximum fragility), a status hierarchy between mother and daughter, and a plot mechanism — Violet's "You only started noticing when you stopped drinking" (p.41) makes the counter the reason the story is happening now.
- **The generational-repetition motif.** Traced in six plants across the script: the mother's curse (p.7) → the sea-turtle speech repeated verbatim to Violet (pp.13–15) → "I drank like her, I used men like her…" (p.25) → "She's sexually active, drinking, smoking grass. Sound familiar?" (p.33) → "Why should I forgive you when you can't even forgive your own mother?" (p.42) → "It is a family tradition" (p.45). The motif is what turns two separate mother-daughter fights into one nested, self-renewing structure.
- **The oblivious third party as pressure valve.** Greg interrupts the coffee-shop confrontation five times across eight pages (pp.27–34). Each entrance resets the register, which is what allows the accusations to escalate all the way to meth-cooking without the scene curdling — and it lets an 8-page two-hander, structurally closer to a one-act play than a sitcom scene, survive inside a multi-cam.
- **Act-out buttons that are jokes carrying reveals.** p.9 ("Don't lie to me, Ethel") — character; p.26 ("Uch. Hi, Mom.") — the reveal, the cliffhanger and the premise in four words; p.43 (Roscoe's hookers / "A little less each time you visit") — the biggest laugh in the act, deployed to lift a curtain off a real beat; p.47 ("Married. What a whore.") — a joke aimed deliberately *away* from the wound the scene just opened.
- **`RESET TO:` and the swing-set economy.** Four resets (pp.3, 9, 23, 44) move within a scene unit rather than cutting between them. Standing sets — Christy's living room/kitchen (A, B, D, M, TAG), the bedrooms (C), the restaurant and its kitchen (CO, L) — carry seven of twelve units; the swings (car, school, church basement, coffee shop) carry five. **The pilot's two most emotionally violent scenes are staged in public places** (a church basement and a coffee shop), which forces the characters to perform their pain in front of onlookers — the set economy manufacturing the tonal permission.
- **Day/night designations as story architecture.** NIGHT 1 (pp.1–19) / DAY 2 (pp.20–43) / NIGHT 3 (pp.44–47). The 24-page single-day block is the spine; the Tag's jump to a different night is what converts "one bad day" into "this is the show now."

## Deviations & tensions with the framework
**Where the multi-cam frame strains under the dramatic material:**
- **Page count is not screen time.** 47 pages for a 21–22 minute episode, because double-spaced dialogue inflates the count roughly 35–40%. A single-cam half-hour of the same duration runs ~30–35 pages. Percentages inside this map are internally valid but must never be compared like-for-like against a single-cam or feature beat map.
- **There is no B-story in the cross-cut sense.** Act Two is three scenes; nothing advances in parallel while the A-story rests. There is no structural redundancy — if the 8-page coffee-shop scene fails, the act fails. Compare a single-cam dramedy, which would intercut the Bonnie fight with the Violet situation to relieve pressure.
- **The act-out must be a joke even when the beat wants silence.** Act Two's curtain has to travel from Violet's "You are so lame" (p.43) to a nine-year-old's joke about beating up video-game prostitutes inside half a page. The laugh is bigger than the beat before it, and some of the emotional residue of the forgiveness scene is spent buying it.
- **All trauma is reported, none is dramatized.** The fire extinguisher, the diaper, the mug shot, the meth lab, the teen pregnancy — every one of them exists only as dialogue. The format converts backstory into stand-up, which is why the two biggest chunks of history arrive as a rule-of-three aria (p.8) and a confession speech (p.25). It works, but it means the audience never *sees* the childhood the entire series is about.
- **The script has to write its own laughter down** (pp.24–26) to make a scene about alcoholism playable. That is the frame openly admitting it needs permission — and it is the only scene in the script where the writers felt the need to supply it.
- **Scene K is a play, not a sitcom scene.** Eight pages of escalating accusation between two people is dramatic-play architecture wearing multi-cam clothes. It survives only because of a waiter.

**Where the frame succeeds — and does something single-cam could not:**
- **Joke density buys content that would be unbearable straight.** "You slept with a fire extinguisher instead of a teddy bear" (p.8) is child endangerment. As the second item of three in an escalating comic list, it is survivable, memorable, and lands harder than a scene of it would.
- **The live-audience contract and the AA-group contract are the same contract.** A room of strangers laughing at a woman's worst material *is the scene*. No other format has that alignment available.
- **The everyone-in-one-room convergence (Scene M, pp.37–43)** puts six people — five of them a different failure of Christy's life — in one standing set, and gets a farce climax and a dramatic pile-up out of a single staging.
- **The Tag, a unit single-cam has largely abandoned,** is where the series gets proven. Four pages after the story is over, on a different night, the show demonstrates the new normal and withholds the one thing that would end it.
- **The 17/17 act symmetry with inverted scene architecture** (seven short scenes vs. three long ones) is a genuinely elegant use of the two-act form: the same page budget spent on breadth, then on depth.

## Lessons for the knowledge base
1. **Put the pain in the first line, not the first act.** The waitress is already crying in the opening image (p.1) and her first joke is her first confession ("Not so much for me"). A tonal hybrid never has to *turn* dark if it was never light — the contract is signed before the main titles, and the audience spends the rest of the episode calibrated.
2. **Give the joke to the listener, and give the pain its own speech.** p.6→7 (Christy's "Is that all I am?" released by Gabriel's "you're not really that good a waitress"); p.17 (Roscoe's "I'm not good anyway" released by Christy, then topped by Roscoe). The painful line completes; the release arrives in the *next* speech, usually from the other character. Pain and punchline in one breath reads as the writer flinching.
3. **Cap the hold at one speech — and buy longer holds with a device.** The longest straight-serious passage in the pilot (p.25, ~15 lines) is only possible because it sits on an AA podium, where monologue is the native form and laughter is diegetically licensed (`THE GROUP LAUGHS`, pp.24–26). If you need a two-page serious beat in a comedy, build the container first.
4. **A confession device is how a joke-per-page format carries a backstory it cannot show.** Multi-cam cannot flash back, so Mom builds a podium (pp.24–26). The device supplies an in-scene audience, a status game (118 days vs. two years, p.30), a punchline structure, and permission — four problems solved by one location.
5. **Make the horror the third item in a rule of three.** p.8: hashish in the diaper / fire extinguisher instead of a teddy bear / the motorcycle stolen from a coke dealer. Escalating comic form carries escalating trauma; the structure does the emotional work the format won't let you stage.
6. **An oblivious third party lets a two-hander go much darker than it otherwise could.** Greg interrupts every ~1.5 pages across pp.27–34, resetting the register so the fight can climb to meth-cooking and abandonment without curdling — then pays off as plot in the Tag (p.47). Comic buffers should be promoted to story before the episode ends.
7. **Build the weekly conflict into the machinery, not into anyone's willingness to show up.** Bonnie is in the same recovery program (p.30) and already Facebook friends with the granddaughter (p.32), so Christy's "I don't want you in my life" (p.34) is unenforceable. An engine that depends on characters *choosing* to keep meeting will die in episode six.
8. **Withhold the object that would end the show — on the last page, explicitly.** "Is there anything you wanna say to me?" / "I love you with all my heart." / "And?" / "I appreciate your apology." (pp.46–47). The episode resolves; the series refuses. Name the withheld object clearly enough that the audience can want it for six seasons.
9. **Nest the conflict across three generations so that fixing one requires fixing the other.** Violet's line at p.42 — "Why should I forgive you when you can't even forgive your own mother?" — mechanically welds the A and B stories together, and the sea-turtle speech (pp.13–15) shows Christy delivering her mother's exact material without noticing. That nesting is what turns two relationships into one self-renewing engine.
10. **Spend equal act budgets in opposite architectures.** Act One = 7 scenes averaging 2.4 pages (install every relationship); Act Two = 3 scenes averaging 5.7 pages (collide them). Same 17 pages, opposite strategies — breadth, then depth. It is a reusable template for any pilot that has a large ensemble and one central wound.
11. **Use the Tag to prove the series, not to add a joke.** The Tag jumps to NIGHT 3 (p.44) and shows the antagonist doing the dishes while the daughter calls her by her first name (p.45). Demonstrating the *new permanent situation* on a different day is what distinguishes a series engine from an episode premise.
