# Desperate Housewives 1x01 "Pilot" (2004) — Structural Beat Map

**Source:** /mnt/user-data/uploads/Screenplay planning/Ressources/Script Library/TV Pilots/Desperate_Housewives_1x01_-_Pilot.pdf · **Writer:** Marc Cherry (title page: "DESPERATE HOUSEWIVES / BY / MARC CHERRY") · **Format:** TV pilot, network (ABC) hour — Teaser + four acts · **Genre:** suburban soap / domestic comedy with a serialized suicide mystery ("camp and menace") · **Length:** 59 printed pp (60 PDF pp incl. title page); title page reads "WRITER'S FINAL DRAFT / AUGUST 12, 2003"; copy stamp 082216. No text layer in the PDF — the whole script was rendered at 300dpi and OCR'd, with key pages verified visually.
**Framework lens(es):** PRIMARY — network hour soap structure with a dead narrator: printed act architecture, act-outs, and the Mary Alice V.O. as a load-bearing structural device. SECONDARY 1 — multi-lead ensemble braiding (four, arguably five, co-leads with separate A-stories). SECONDARY 2 — the dual engine: a serialized mystery running underneath an episodic domestic-comedy engine.

Page citations are the script's **printed** page numbers (top-right of each page). Offset: **PDF page N = printed page N−1** (PDF p.1 is the title page). Percentages are of 59 printed pages. The act structure is **printed on the page**: `TEASER` … `END OF TEASER` … `ACT ONE` … `END OF ACT ONE`, through to `ACT FOUR` … `THE END`, each act opening on `FADE IN:` and closing on `FADE OUT.` **There is no Tag** — Act Four runs straight into the series hook and stops.

## Logline
Six days after a seemingly perfect suburban wife shoots herself in her immaculate hallway, her four closest friends on Wisteria Lane go on with their small, secret catastrophes — a burnt-out mother, a bored trophy wife sleeping with the teenage gardener, a divorcée chasing the new plumber, and a homemaker whose husband wants out — until they open a blackmail note in the dead woman's coat pocket and realise none of them knew her at all.

## Structure overview

The pilot is a **four-act broadcast hour with a suicide for a teaser**, and its central bet is that a dead woman's voice can do the work that four separate first acts would otherwise require. Marc Cherry front-loads the architecture: the Teaser and Act One together are **21 of 59 pages (36%)** and contain **37 of the script's 70 narration cues (53%)**. In that stretch the script kills its narrator, establishes the neighbourhood's moral economy, and installs four co-leads with complete backstories — using a single repeated sentence shape ("*Lynette Scavo, who lives on the corner, brought fried chicken*", p.5; "*Gabrielle Solis, who lives three doors down, brought chilled gazpacho*", p.8; "*Bree Van de Kamp, who lives down the block, brought baskets of muffins*", p.10; "*Susan Meyer, who lives across the street, brought macaroni and cheese*", p.11).

The **wake is the delivery system**. One location, one afternoon, four leads, zero transitions. Every introduction is a triptych: a V.O. formula line, a flashback montage (six blocks, fourteen flashback slug lines in the script), and one present-tense action that reverses the flashback. The dish each woman carries *is* her character — Lynette's "family recipe" fried chicken is fast food; Gabrielle's gazpacho is "not quite as chilled as Gabrielle's current relationship with her husband"; Bree wants her baskets back; Susan's macaroni is inedible and becomes her meet-cute.

Act Two is the **pure comedy act** — the only act with no mystery content and the only act whose act-out belongs to a domestic storyline (Rex's "*Everybody just be quiet so I can eat this slop in peace*", p.31). Act Three is the **longest run of unnarrated drama** in the script: four narration cues in twelve pages, because by then all four storylines are self-propelling. Act Four is the **convergence night** — one overheard line in a Starbucks queue (p.44) turns four separate stories into a single Friday, and the arson set-piece pulls all four women onto the same lawn.

Three of four act-outs belong to the serialized engine, and each costs less than a page. The mystery takes the **doorways**; the comedy takes the **rooms**.

## The narrator ledger

**70 narration cue-lines** in 59 pages (69 printed `MARY ALICE (V.O.)`, plus one printed simply `MARY ALICE` on p.56 — a typo, since she is dead and speaking over Mike's living room). Two of the 70 are `(continuing)` continuations. She also appears **on camera with four live dialogue cues** in the pp.14–16 flashback, and there is exactly **one non-Mary-Alice V.O.** in the script: `BREE (V.O.) "Susan!"` (p.16), used as the sound-bridge out of that flashback.

Distribution: **Teaser 13 · Act One 24 · Act Two 15 · Act Three 4 · Act Four 14.** Density collapses from 3.3 cues/page in the teaser to 0.33 in Act Three, then rises again to 0.9 in Act Four.

**The rule the narrator obeys, and never breaks: Mary Alice narrates women; the camera narrates men.** Every interiority cue in the script belongs to a woman — Mrs. Huber (pp.3–4), Lynette (pp.6, 34), Gabrielle (p.8), Bree (pp.10, 41, 51), Susan (pp.13, 53), Edie (p.27). Carlos is narrated only externally ("*who worked in mergers and acquisitions, proposed on their third date*", p.8), and the joke is about Gabrielle's disillusion, not his mind. Paul, Rex, Mike, Tom and John are never narrated at all. When the script needs to accuse Paul, it uses an action line instead — "*The malignant look in his eyes suggests he may know the answer to that question*" (p.17), "*This is a man with a SECRET*" (p.21). That single discipline is what lets an omniscient narrator coexist with a serialized mystery: her omniscience is real, and it simply stops at the property line of the men.

**What the device solves for a four-lead ensemble**
1. **The introduction problem.** Four co-leads with full backstories in nine pages (pp.5–13), using one repeatable sentence shape and six flashback blocks. A dramatized version of the same information is 25+ pages — an entire act the pilot does not have.
2. **The transition problem.** With a voice above the street, the script can cut from Gabrielle's bed to Susan's kitchen with no connective scene. p.22 re-opens all four storylines in a single sentence.
3. **The interiority problem.** These are four women defined by what they will not say — Lynette's lie (p.34), Bree's refusal (p.41), Susan's shame (p.53). The narrator delivers the confession while the scene keeps the mask on, which means no lead ever has to become articulate about herself and thereby lose her comedy.
4. **The tone problem.** The narrator's amused, forgiving register is the show's tonal governor. It licenses jokes about a suicide (p.4), an arson (p.54) and a near-murder, because the person laughing has the most right to be bitter and isn't.
5. **The unity problem.** Four A-stories with no shared plot need something to make them one show. The V.O. is the only element present in all four; it is the series' *voice* in the literal sense.
6. **The renewability problem.** A narrator who is dead, who knows everything, and who is withholding exactly one fact about herself is a permanent engine: every week she can narrate anyone and still owe the audience her own answer.

**How four A-stories stay A-stories rather than four B-stories**
- **Each runs a different genre.** Susan = romantic comedy with a rival. Lynette = domestic farce shading into tragedy of wasted competence. Bree = marital drama with a black-comic detonator. Gabrielle = adultery thriller played as caper. Four genres in one hour read as variety; four instances of the same genre would read as one story and three echoes.
- **Each owns a dedicated antagonist physically present in the world.** Edie, the children/Tom, Rex, Carlos. No storyline borrows its stakes from another, so none is structurally dependent — the definition of an A-story.
- **Each gets at least one act-out-worthy set-piece.** Lynette in the pool (pp.19–21), Bree's salad (pp.39–43), Gabrielle's ballgown mow (pp.47–51), Susan's arson (pp.52–56). Any of the four could have closed an act; the pilot's choice to spend three of four act-outs on the mystery is a luxury purchased by that surplus.
- **Each reverses her own introduction.** The executive wades into a pool in her clothes; the perfect wife nearly kills her husband; the woman who has everything mows a lawn in couture; the nice one commits arson. The pilot proves each character by breaking the image it just sold.
- **Ensemble scenes are rare and load-bearing.** Only four in 59 pages (pp.13–17, 32–33, 54–56, 57–59 — roughly 7 pages, 12%). Each does three jobs at once: refresh the friendship premise, let each woman comment on another's plot, and advance or reset the mystery.
- **There is still a first among equals.** Susan takes ~31% of the pages and both the emotional opening (the meet-cute) and the closing line. A "true" four-way split would leave no one to follow; the pilot braids unevenly on purpose.
- **The V.O. is the connective tissue.** Because a narrator can move between houses, the script never needs a scene whose only job is to get from one woman to another — the structural cost that normally forces ensembles into A/B/C hierarchy.

## Dual engine analysis

**The split: ~15% serialized, ~85% episodic.** Mystery content occupies roughly **8.8 of 59 printed pages** — pp.1–4 (the teaser, 4pp), p.17 (Paul eavesdropping, a paragraph), p.21 (the pool act-out, a third of a page), p.43 (the pick-axe, ~1pp), pp.56–57 (Mike's gun, ~0.7pp) and pp.57–59 (the letter, ~2.5pp). Everything else is domestic comedy and marital drama.

**The mystery advances on exactly seven occasions**, spaced 11–13 pages apart, and outside the teaser and the closing sequence it never occupies more than one page:

| Page(s) | ~% | Advance | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | 2–7% | The suicide itself; the street's reaction; Mrs. Huber's petty larceny | 4 pp (the whole teaser) |
| 17 | 29% | The four friends did not know her; Paul is listening and "*may know the answer*" | ~0.2 pp |
| 21 | 36% | Paul + the pool; menace made visual | ~0.3 pp |
| 36 | 61% | *Thematic* advance only — Gabrielle quotes the method ("*blow my brains out*") | ~0.1 pp |
| 43 | 73% | Paul is destroying something under the pool; he silences his own son | ~1 pp |
| 57 | 97% | Mike is armed and reporting to someone: "*No, nothing yet… I'm getting close*" | ~0.5 pp |
| 57–59 | 97–100% | The blackmail note, postmarked the day she died; the women now hold the mystery | ~2.5 pp |

**What the pilot spends:** the death and its method; the fact that the street's etiquette survives it (the blender); the fact that four intimate friends knew nothing; the fact that the husband knows and is physically destroying evidence; the fact that a third party knew and threatened her; and the fact that the new neighbour is armed and working for someone.

**What it banks:** *what she did*; who wrote the note; what is under the pool; who Mike works for and who is on the phone; what Zach knows or saw; and Mrs. Huber's real function — planted three times as a thief and a gossip (pp.4, 41, 55) with no payoff at all. Six open questions against one answered one.

**How it proves the episodic engine is sustainable.** All four domestic storylines end the pilot **changed but unresolved**, and each ends holding a renewable weekly problem: Susan has Mike back and a rival she has now materially wronged (the fender-bender and the arson are both unpaid debts); Lynette has punched her husband and is still alone with four children; Bree's marriage is over *and* she nearly killed him *and* nobody outside the family knows; Gabrielle got away with it, which guarantees she'll do it again. None of these require the mystery to generate story. Act Two proves the point by containing **no mystery content whatsoever** and still building to a functional act-out (p.31) — a deliberate ten-page demonstration that the show works without the corpse.

**How it proves the serialized engine is sustainable.** Three moves. First, **escalation by return**: the pool is an act-out image at p.21 and the *same* image, drained and under a pick-axe, at p.43 — the mystery grows without new information. Second, **a second front**: Mike's gun (p.57) opens an entirely separate serialized thread in half a page, so the season is not hostage to one secret. Third, and decisively, **the transfer of ownership**: for 56 pages the mystery belongs to Paul, whom the leads barely know. The letter moves it to the women — found by Bree, opened by Gabrielle, read by four, and the last spoken line of the script is Susan's "*Oh, Mary Alice. What did you do?*" (p.59). A serialized question the *protagonists* are now asking is a weekly engine; a serialized question only the audience is asking is a countdown.

**The two engines are welded at exactly one point.** The thesis. Mary Alice, alive, to a crying Susan on p.16: "*there will be days you won't feel like getting out of bed. But this, too, shall pass… at the end of the day, what other freaking choice do you have?*" Bree, watching a house burn on p.55: "*Besides, what other choice does she have?*" The serial engine is the woman who ran out of choices; the episodic engine is four women still making them. That is why the comedy and the corpse belong to the same show.

## Storylines

- **A1 — Susan & Mike & Edie (pp.11–13, 17–20, 22–23, 26–29, 33, 36–39, 44, 52–56).** Divorcée meets plumber over inedible macaroni; a predatory neighbour beats her to him; escalating self-sabotage (lard down the drain, freeway pursuit, restaurant stakeout, break-in) culminating in accidental arson and a reprieve when Mike turns out to have been at the movies. The pilot's spine and its comic climax.
- **A2 — Lynette vs. her own life (pp.5–8, 19–21, 22, 32–35, 45–47).** The executive who quit for four children. Three public defeats (the wake pool, the supermarket cart, the peaches) and one private one — the lie to Natalie Wellman (p.34) — ending in a punch her husband never sees coming (p.47).
- **A3 — Bree & Rex (pp.9–11, 29–31, 39–43, 51–52).** Perfection as a hostage situation. The family dinner (pp.29–31), the divorce request over a salad bar (pp.39–43), the accidental near-poisoning, and the hospital verdict — "*this cold, perfect thing you've become*" — answered with twenty minutes of muffled sobbing and a reassembled face (pp.51–52).
- **A4 — Gabrielle, Carlos & John (pp.8–9, 23–26, 35–36, 47–51).** A trophy wife having sex with her sixteen-year-old gardener to keep from wanting to die (p.36), running a two-scene caper (bribed waiter, midnight mow) to protect the affair, and winning (p.51).
- **B — Paul & Zach Scott (pp.11, 17, 21, 43, 57–59).** Five appearances, roughly 2 pages total, all of them observational: the widower who receives condolences, eavesdrops, stares into a pool, drains it, hacks at its floor, and silences his son.
- **B — Mike Delfino's other business (pp.17–20, 26–29, 37–39, 56–57).** Introduced as a romantic object for two women; revealed in half a page as an armed man reporting to an unseen employer.
- **C — Mrs. Edith Huber (pp.3–4, 7, 19, 41, 55).** Discovers the body, steals the blender, polices Lynette's children, interrupts Bree at the salad bar, and delivers the gossip at the fire. All plant, no payoff — a character built for later.
- **Thematic spine — the friendship & the thesis (pp.13–17, 32–33, 54–56, 57–59).** Four ensemble scenes. The wine-glass flashback states the thesis, the supermarket refreshes the group, the fire reprises the thesis, and the packing detonates the mystery in the group's hands.

## Structural devices

- **The suicide cold open, narrated by the suicide.** The premise is spent in the first two pages: the narrator introduces herself, performs six chores, and shoots herself in the head, all in the same conversational register. The gap between the mildness of the delivery and the violence of the content is the show's entire tonal proposition, established before the title card.
- **Blood to tomato sauce (pp.2–3).** A woman's hand wiping the spreading blood becomes Mrs. Huber wiping spilled sauce off her counter. The pilot's thesis as an edit: horror and housework are the same gesture.
- **The "brought X" introduction formula (pp.5, 8, 10, 11, 26).** One sentence shape, five times: name, address, dish. Each dish is a character diagnosis, each is followed by a flashback block, and each ends on a swerve. The formula's repetition is what makes the pilot feel authored rather than crowded.
- **The flashback as a compression device (six blocks, fourteen flashback slugs).** Lynette's career (pp.5–6), Gabrielle's courtship (p.8), Bree's competences (p.10), Susan's marriage in three casseroles (p.12), Edie's three conquests with three dropped props (p.27), and the wine-glass scene (pp.14–16). Note that five of six are montage under V.O.; only one is a *scene* — and that one exists to make the narrator a person.
- **Transitions that do the narrator's job when she is silent.** The wine-glass dissolve (p.14: wine pours backwards into two years earlier), the push *through* the Van de Kamp window (p.29), and the pass-by braid at Cowboy Bob's (p.39: Susan peels out of the lot as Bree's family pulls in). The script proves it can move between storylines without V.O. before it starts relying on V.O. less.
- **The note in the coat pocket (pp.57–59).** A physical object that (a) confirms a secret, (b) names no fact, (c) carries a postmark that dates it to the day of the death, and (d) is discovered by the four leads rather than delivered to the audience. Four functions, one prop, three pages.
- **The pool as an escalating image (pp.19–21 → p.43).** Introduced as the site of Lynette's comic humiliation, taken over by Paul in the same scene's final paragraph, and returned to at the next act-out drained and under a pick-axe. The serial engine escalates by revisiting rather than by adding.
- **Camp welded to menace.** Bree's "*I will need the baskets back*" (p.11) and Paul's underwater face (p.21) are eleven pages apart in the same act. Gabrielle's "*take care of the bush*" joke (p.26) and her "*blow my brains out*" (p.36) are ten pages apart in the same storyline. The script never modulates between the registers — it cuts between them, and the narrator's voice is the only thing holding both.
- **The cul-de-sac as a bounded world.** Everyone lives within sightlines: Huber jumps at a fence (p.3), Susan watches Mike's driveway from her kitchen window (p.37), Gabrielle mows in a gown while a neighbour in a bathrobe watches (p.49), and the whole street assembles in bathrobes at the fire (p.54). Gabrielle states the rule out loud: "*We all live fifty feet away, for God's sake*" (p.17). A bounded world is what makes four unconnected storylines legible as one place — and what makes a blackmailer plausible.
- **The closing pull-back (p.59).** From four frozen women to a jogger, girls jumping rope, a man mowing, cars passing — "*A beautiful day in SUBURBIA. And nobody seems to be DESPERATE.*" The title spoken as the last three words of the script, by the action line rather than the narrator, as a lie the widening frame has just disproved.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework

- **The tonal tightrope is a genuine risk, and the script knows it.** The teaser button is a woman stealing a blender off a suicide (p.4). Read wrong, that is not satire but contempt, and the series dies in its first four pages. Cherry's insurance is structural: the joke is aimed at a *minor* character, and the pilot then spends pp.14–17 making Mary Alice warm, funny and generous on camera before any of the leads become ridiculous. The audience is given a person to mourn before it is asked to laugh at the neighbourhood.
- **The ensemble load is not evenly distributed, and one lead nearly disappears.** Susan takes roughly 31% of the pages; Lynette and Gabrielle about 17% each. **Bree has no scene of her own between p.11 and p.29** — an 18-page gap patched by a single image of her cleaning an oven in a hairnet (p.22) and her presence in the wake kitchen. Gabrielle likewise vanishes from p.36 to p.47. The pilot survives this because the V.O. montage on p.22 keeps every woman visible while she is dramatically dormant; a script without a narrator would have to pay for those re-entries with scenes.
- **The narrator's omniscience has hard, unexplained limits.** She reads Mrs. Huber's private hesitation (p.3), Lynette's internal arithmetic (p.34) and Bree's unspoken confession (p.41) — but never Paul's, Rex's, Mike's or Tom's. She also narrates events days after her own death from vantages she could not occupy. She is not a ghost with a plausible viewpoint; she is a formal device, and the script wisely never offers an explanation. The framework tension: an omniscient narrator inside a mystery is normally a cheat, because she must be withholding. The pilot solves it by making the withheld thing **her own secret** — refusing to confess is psychologically legible in a way that refusing to report someone else's business is not. The one place she brushes against it (p.58, "*something I tried so desperately to keep secret*") converts the withhold into characterisation, and the apology on p.59 ("*I'm so sorry, girls*") buys goodwill with information that isn't there.
- **The suicide has no motive within the pilot, so the narrator's warmth is doing work the plot has not earned.** "*This, too, shall pass… what other freaking choice do you have?*" (p.16) is the advice of a woman who shortly afterwards chooses otherwise. The audience must accept that contradiction as intrigue rather than incoherence for 22 more episodes — a large ask made on the strength of a single scene.
- **Act One is 29% of the script and is almost entirely installation.** Seventeen pages, four introductions, one flashback scene and one mystery beat. That is affordable only because every installation beat is also a joke or a humiliation — the baskets, the Santa threat, the pool — but it leaves the pilot's *first* real story turn (the affair, p.25) at nearly the halfway mark.
- **Draft-only inconsistencies (this Writer's Final Draft, 12 Aug 2003).** Bree explicitly sets the closet-packing for **Friday** (p.17), but Act Four opens on Friday (p.44), spends Friday night on the fire, and then places the packing "*the next day*" (p.57). Andrew Van de Kamp is 16 on p.10 and 15 on p.29. Paul is cued "MR. SCOTT" on pp.10–11 and "PAUL" from p.43. One narration cue is printed without its (V.O.) tag (p.56). Two scene headings are printed "EAT." for "EXT."
- **Differences from the aired episode.** The family name is **Scott**, not Young (Mary Alice Scott, Paul Scott, Zach Scott). Susan is **Meyer**, not Mayer. Her daughter is **Jenna, 12** — the aired series renames and ages her (Julie). The neighbour is **Edith Huber**, not Martha. Lynette has **four** children including an 18-month-old daughter, **Patsy**, whom she breast-feeds through the wake (pp.13, 19) — a whole strand of business the aired pilot does not carry — and the boys are named Peter, Porter and Preston rather than the aired Preston, Porter and Parker. Ages skew older than the eventual casting throughout: Gabrielle, Bree and Lynette are all "late 30's," Susan "mid-30's," Mary Alice "early 30's," John 16. The note's wording in this draft is "**AND I'M GONNA TELL**." Structurally, however, the draft is essentially the shooting architecture: the acts, the act-outs, the pool, the gun and the letter are all in place.

## Lessons for the knowledge base

1. **A narrator is how a network hour installs four co-leads without a four-act prologue.** Pages 5–13 give four women a formula line, a flashback montage, a defining irony and a present-tense action — nine pages for what would otherwise cost twenty-five. Use one repeatable sentence shape ("*X, who lives Y, brought Z*") so the repetition reads as authorship rather than as a crutch, and make the introduced object (the dish) carry the diagnosis, so the setup and the character note are the same beat.

2. **Give the narrator one rule and never break it.** In this pilot the rule is *she narrates women's interiority and never a man's*. Paul's menace and Mike's gun are delivered by action line ("*This is a man with a SECRET*", p.21) and by staging, never by voice. That single discipline is what allows total omniscience and a watertight mystery to coexist; break it once and the audience starts asking why she isn't telling them everything.

3. **Let narration density collapse as the storylines start running.** 3.3 cues per page in the teaser, 1.4 in Act One, **0.33 across the whole of Act Three**, then back to 0.9 for the convergence act. V.O. is an installation and interiority tool, not a running commentary. When four storylines are self-propelling, twelve pages need four lines.

4. **Put the serialized engine on the act-outs and nowhere else.** The mystery occupies ~15% of the pages but takes three of four act-outs, each for under a page (p.21 is one paragraph). The comedy owns the rooms; the thriller owns the doorways. This is the cheapest sustainable ratio in a dual-engine pilot — and it means a bad week of serial plotting can never starve the episodic show.

5. **Prove the episodic engine can carry an act break alone.** Act Two (pp.22–31) contains zero mystery content and still ends on a functional out — Rex's "*slop*" line. That ten-page demonstration is the pilot telling the network the series is not hostage to its corpse.

6. **Four A-stories stay A-stories when each runs a different genre against a different antagonist.** Rom-com with a rival (Susan/Edie), domestic farce (Lynette/her children), marital drama with a black-comic detonator (Bree/Rex), adultery caper (Gabrielle/Carlos). Four genres read as variety; four instances of one genre read as one story with three echoes. And no storyline may borrow its stakes from another — the moment it does, it is a B-story.

7. **Keep ensemble scenes rare and make each do three jobs.** Four group scenes in 59 pages (pp.13–17, 32–33, 54–56, 57–59 — about 12% of the script). Each simultaneously refreshes the friendship premise, lets each woman comment on another's plot, and advances or resets the mystery. More than that and the braid becomes a talk show; fewer and the "friends" premise is a claim rather than a fact.

8. **State your thesis inside a flashback and return it from another mouth at the end.** Mary Alice, alive, p.16: "*at the end of the day, what other freaking choice do you have?*" Bree, over a burning house, p.55: "*Besides, what other choice does she have?*" The theme comes back from the woman it will destroy, about a rival, over a fire one of them started. Plant the thesis in the voice of the character who has already failed it.

9. **Reverse every lead's introduction before the pilot ends.** The executive wades into a pool in her clothes (p.21); the perfect wife nearly kills her husband with a salad (p.43); the woman who has everything mows a lawn in an evening gown (p.49); the nice one commits arson (p.54). A pilot earns its characters by breaking the image it just sold — and the reversal, not the introduction, is what makes a viewer come back.

10. **End a serialized pilot by transferring the question from the audience to the leads.** For 56 pages the secret belongs to Paul, whom nobody knows. The letter — found by Bree, opened by Gabrielle, read by four — moves it to the protagonists, and the last spoken line is one of them asking it out loud: "*Oh, Mary Alice. What did you do?*" (p.59). Then pull back to a jogger, a skipping rope and a man mowing his lawn, and let the action line say the title: "*And nobody seems to be DESPERATE.*" Answer one question, bank six, and hand the asking to the people the audience has just spent an hour learning to like.
