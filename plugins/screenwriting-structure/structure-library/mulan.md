**Framework lens(es):** PRIMARY — Hero's Journey (Campbell/Vogler). SECONDARY 1 — Murdock's *Heroine's Journey* and Hudson's *Virgin's Promise* run against the same beats, and argued out in a dedicated comparison pass. SECONDARY 2 — Disney animated-musical beat architecture (I Want placement, transformation montage, villain function, sidekick relief rhythm, song-for-dialogue compression).

Page citations use the script's **printed page numbers** (header format `MULAN EIGHTH DRAFT 12/21/96 p. N.`); printed page N = PDF page N+2. Percentages are of 101 printed pages. **This is a genuine production screenplay, not a transcript** — it carries revision-date slugs, change asterisks in the margins, animation sequence numbers in the headers, and text that differs from the finished film (e.g. Mushu's baldly self-serving motive on p.29, Shang's flat "you fight good" on p.97) — so the page numbers are real timing evidence, not reverse-engineered from a cut.

## Logline
When the Emperor conscripts one man from every family to repel a Hun invasion, a Chinese girl who has just publicly failed the matchmaker cuts off her hair, steals her crippled father's armour and takes his place in the army — where she becomes the soldier who saves China, is unmasked as a woman at the height of her triumph, and has to win the war a second time as herself.

## Structure overview
The script runs a clean four-block architecture whose act turns land almost exactly on model: **threshold at p.21 (21%)**, **transformation/midpoint at pp.47–53 (47–52%)**, **exposure at p.76 (75%)**, **final climax pp.86–94 (85–93%)**. What makes it unusual is that it stages its climax **twice** — once at 65–71% (the avalanche, won as "Ping") and once at 85–93% (the palace, won as Mulan) — with the exposure beat between them converting the first victory into a crime.

Two antagonists split the work: **Shan-Yu** carries the plot clock (six interstitial "meanwhile" panels: pp.1–3, 30–31, 48–49, 65–72, 80–81, 84–94) and never speaks to Mulan until p.90; **Chi Fu** carries the theme (pp.18, 42, 54, 76–77, 84, 94–96) and is the one who actually enforces the rule the film is about. The film "defeats" both — Shan-Yu by rocket (p.94), Chi Fu by the Emperor handing Mulan his job (p.96).

Sequence numbers in the headers run **out of order** relative to page order — 1.0, 1.9A/B/C, 3.9, 4, 5, 6, 7, **8.6, 7.5**, 8, 8.7, 8.8, 9.0, **7.3**, 8.3, 8.4, 9.6, 9.5, 9.8, 9.9, 10, **15, 17.5, 16**, 19, 20, 21, 22 — with sequences 2, 3, 11–14 and 18 gone entirely. That's the fingerprint of animation development: sequences are numbered when assigned to units and then cut and re-ordered, so the numbering preserves an earlier structure underneath this one.

| Fa Zhou puts the trophies aside: "having you for a daughter" (98) | **Return with the Elixir** — and the elixir is refused as an object | **Healing the Father/Daughter split** — Murdock's framework predicts *Mother*/daughter | **The Kingdom is Brighter** |

**Verdict: Hudson's Virgin's Promise describes this script most accurately — thirteen beats for thirteen, in order, at the right percentages. Vogler describes the *other* protagonist.**

The Virgin's Promise fit is not approximate; it is nearly verbatim, including Hudson's beat *names*. **Dependent World** (pp.3–6, a family whose standing depends on her marrying well). **Price of Conformity** stated as the film's own lyric — "if I were truly to be myself / I would break my fam'ly's heart" (p.16). **Dresses the Part** performed literally with scissors and armour at p.21. A **Secret World** occupying 21–75% of the running time. **Caught Shining** at p.76 (75%) — Hudson's definition is *the secret is discovered at the moment of the Virgin's greatest success*, which is precisely what a wound sustained while saving the captain produces. **Gives Up What Kept Her Stuck** at p.79, where she abandons the alibi that she did it for her father. **Kingdom in Chaos** at pp.84–85. **Wanders in the Wilderness** at pp.78–84, ending on "That's 'cause you're a girl now." **Chooses Her Light** at p.85 ("Hey guys, I've got an idea!"), executed as herself, in her own name, in a dress. **Re-ordering** at p.95, when the Emperor bows and Chi Fu is fired. **The Kingdom is Brighter** at pp.98–101, where the change is measured in the *community's* behaviour — the father who once said "you dishonor me" now says "the greatest gift and honor is having you for a daughter."

Vogler fits the **plot** and misfits the **meaning**. Every external stage is present and well-placed — Call p.18, Threshold p.21, Ordeal pp.63–72, Reward p.72, Road Back p.80, Resurrection pp.86–94, Return p.98 — but three things break. (1) **The mentor arrives after the threshold and is a fraud**: Mushu appears at p.23 (23%), 2 pages after the irreversible act, and confesses at p.79 that "the Ancestors never sent me." (2) **The Reward is confiscated four pages after it's granted** — Shang says "you have my trust" on p.73 and withdraws it on p.77 — and Vogler has no stage for *the hero is punished for succeeding*. (3) **The Return with the Elixir is repudiated on the page**: the sword of Shan-Yu and the Emperor's crest, the two objects the entire Hero's Journey exists to produce, are handed over on p.98 and immediately "put aside" by Fa Zhou. A framework whose terminal beat is the boon-delivered cannot account for a script that stages the boon's delivery in order to reject it.

Murdock is the most useful **diagnostic** and the least accurate **map**. Her *Separation from the Feminine* (p.21), *Identification with the Masculine* (pp.34–48), *Illusory Boon of Success* (pp.72–75) and *Beyond Duality* (pp.90–93) are all sharper descriptions than anything in Vogler — "illusory boon" is the exact right name for a triumph awarded to a person who does not exist. But her framework then requires a **Descent to the Goddess**, an **urgent yearning to reconnect with the feminine**, and a **healing of the mother/daughter split**, and this script has none of the three. The descent (pp.78–80) is to a wisecracking male dragon. Fa Li speaks eleven lines in the whole script and none of them after p.22; she does not appear in the reconciliation on p.98, and Grandmother Fa's role at pp.99–100 is a marriage joke. The split that heals is **father/daughter** — which is why the script's last serious line belongs to Fa Zhou, not Fa Li.

The most productive reading is that **two frameworks run on the same timeline, and they are the film's two storylines.** Vogler's Hero's Journey belongs to *Ping* — called, thresholded, tested, rewarded with a sword. Hudson's Virgin's Promise belongs to *Mulan* — conforming, disguising, caught, wandering, choosing. **p.76 is the hinge where they separate:** Ping's Hero's Journey ends there (he is exposed and effectively killed off — "A life for a life. My debt is repaid"), and Mulan's Virgin's Promise turns there (Caught Shining). Everything after p.76 is the second framework finishing a story the first one cannot end. This also explains the double climax: the avalanche is the Hero's Journey's climax at 67%, and the palace is the Virgin's Promise's climax at 85–93%, and the film needs both because it has two protagonists in one body.

**Structural notes on the musical form.** There is **no villain song** — Shan-Yu doesn't sing, doesn't monologue, and speaks under 30 lines in the entire script. There is **no love duet and no I-Want reprise**; the emotional recap that a reprise would normally carry is instead delivered as prose on p.79 ("so when I looked in the mirror I'd see someone worthwhile"). All four songs land in the first 61% — the last 40 pages are score-only, which is why the third act reads faster than its page count. Two of the four songs are sung *about* Mulan by people who have her wrong (pp.7–11, 47–48) and one is sung *around* her by men fantasising about the kind of girl she is standing next to them failing to be (pp.60–62): the score's recurring joke is that Mulan is almost never the subject of her own musical numbers.

**Comic-sidekick relief rhythm.** Mushu enters at p.23 (23%) — late for a Disney sidekick — and thereafter his beats are deliberately placed *on top of* emotional peaks to prevent the film curdling: the Great Stone Dragon crumbles (p.27–28) immediately after the family's grief; "help me pick my victory pose" (p.75) immediately before Mulan collapses bleeding; spitting on and polishing her helmet (p.79) inside her lowest line in the script; "Now that's what I call a Mongolian barbecue" (p.94) two beats after the villain's death; and the film's final image is his joke, not her face (p.101). Cri-Kee is a pure running gag — "luck" — that is converted to plot at p.91 ("Do you feel lucky?") and p.93 (he lights the rocket).

**Villain function.** Shan-Yu is a **clock, not a character**: six interstitial panels (pp.1–3, 30–31, 48–49, 65–72, 80–81, 84–94) placed at intervals of roughly 20 pages, each one advancing the invasion while the A-story does comedy. He shares no dialogue with Mulan until p.90, and the only line he addresses to her — "It looks like you're out of ideas" (p.92) — exists to be refuted by the fan. The film's *thematic* antagonist is **Chi Fu**, who appears at every one of Mulan's humiliations (pp.18, 42, 54, 76–77, 84, 94) and delivers the sentence the story is built to overturn: "She's a woman! She'll never be worth anything!" (p.94).

## Storylines
- **A: Mulan's identity (the spine).** From "quiet and demure ... graceful ... polite" cribbed on her arm (p.3) to "My name is Mulan" (p.77) to putting her hair up in front of Shan-Yu while wearing a dress (p.90). Measured throughout by reflections: mirror (p.10), water trough (p.15), rain puddle (p.20), sword blade (p.66), helmet (p.79). Resolved not by victory but by her father setting the trophies aside (p.98).
- **B: The war / Shan-Yu (the clock).** Opens the film before the heroine exists (p.1), runs in interstitial panels, intersects the A-story exactly twice — the avalanche (pp.66–68) and the roof (pp.90–94). Its function is to supply a deadline and a public arena in which the A-story's question can be answered in front of witnesses.
- **C: Shang (the witness).** Installed with his own father-son mirror (pp.37–39), gives the trust (p.73), withdraws it (pp.77–78), refuses to re-extend it (p.82), silently joins her plan on the roof (p.86), defends her publicly (p.94), fumbles the private moment (p.97), and arrives at the moon gate (p.99). He is structurally the *audience surrogate for the community's verdict* far more than a love interest — his three-page romance payoff is the least-developed strand in the script by design.
- **D: The ancestors / Mushu (the comic and thematic underlayer).** Mushu wants his pedestal back (p.29); he forges the orders that cause the third act (pp.56–58); he confesses his fraud in the same breath she confesses hers (p.79); he is restored in his own scene after hers (p.100). This is the only storyline with a *stated* selfish want, which is why it can carry both the comedy and the film's second thesis — that honour claimed for oneself and honour earned for someone else are different things.
- **Convergence points.** pp.66–68 (A and B collide for the first time — Mulan's plan, Mushu's fire, Shan-Yu's army). pp.76–78 (A, B and C collide: the wound from B exposes A and destroys C). pp.86–94 (all four converge — Mulan leads, Shang follows, Mushu supplies the rocket, Cri-Kee supplies the luck, Shan-Yu dies). p.98–101 (A closes with the father, C with the moon gate, D with the pedestal).

## Structural devices
- **The matchmaker sequence as thesis-in-advance (pp.11–15).** The Matchmaker names the required virtues — *dignity*, *refinement*, *poised*, *"and silent!!"* — and Mulan fails each one on camera. The climax then re-runs the same test with the terms inverted: she is loud (p.90, "Haven't enough lives been lost?"), unposed (she punches Shang, p.91), and disobedient ("But I'll stop him my way"), and it is *that* set of failures that saves China. Even the props recur: the tea, the fan, the cricket, and the incense-fire that ends the matchmaker's scene rhymes with the fireworks that end Shan-Yu's.
- **The mirror/reflection motif as a five-beat arc.** Prettified reflection accepted with one act of rebellion (p.10, the curled strand); reflection rejected (p.15, "Why is my reflection someone I don't know"); reflection consulted before the decision (p.20, the puddle); reflection *used as a plan* (p.66, "the mountain reflecting in it" — she looks at the blade, looks at the mountain, sheathes the sword); reflection emptied (p.79, "I see nothing"). The motif never resolves in a mirror — it resolves in other people's faces when the plaza bows (p.95).
- **The fan.** Planted as an incompetence at p.9 ("Fa Li tries to teach Mulan how to hold a fan gracefully, only it flies out of Mulan's hands"), mis-deployed at p.14 (she fans the Matchmaker's burning rear and makes it worse), used as concealment at p.87 ("Mulan holds her fan in front of her face"), and at p.92–93 it disarms Shan-Yu: "MULAN SNAPS the fan shut on the blade, TORQUES it from his hand and CATCHES the sword. / Not quite." Planted at 9%, paid at 92%, and the payoff *is* the theme — the tool of enforced femininity taken as a weapon.
- **Planted and paid, a partial inventory.** The Emperor's "a single grain of rice can tip the scale ... one man may be the difference" (pp.2–3) → the rice punishment (p.42) → one girl and one cannon (p.67). The stone discs and the pole (pp.46–47) → the palace pillars, with the script explicitly writing "the way Mulan climbed the pole during training" (p.86). Cri-Kee's luck (pp.6, 11, 14, 80) → "Do you feel lucky?" (p.91) → he lights the rocket (p.93). Chien Po's "relax and chant with me" (p.36) → p.97. Yao's "King of the Rock" (p.52) → "You're king of the mountain!" (p.73). Shan-Yu's doll (pp.48–49) → found by Mulan in the ashes and laid on the General's helmet (pp.62–63). Shang's father's sword (p.38) → thrown into the snow instead of executing her (p.77) → returned to him by Mulan (p.82) → blocks Shan-Yu's killing blow (p.89). The Great Stone Dragon (p.20, p.27) → rebuilt "badly put together" (p.101).
- **The double climax.** The two climaxes are deliberately the *same shape*: aim a projectile at Shan-Yu, use the environment as the real weapon, and end holding Shang. The avalanche (pp.66–72, 65–71%) is executed by "Ping," believed, celebrated and then criminalised. The palace (pp.86–94, 85–93%) is executed by Mulan, in a dress, in her own name, and is believed only because it worked. The film's argument is carried by the *repetition*: identical competence, read twice.
- **The exposure as the true act break.** Placed at p.76 = 75.2%, exactly where a conventional Act Two curtain sits — but it is a *disclosure*, not a defeat. Everything the heroine wants is granted (trust, honour, the sword) between pp.72 and 75, and revoked between pp.76 and 78. Compressing grant and revocation into six pages is what makes the reversal feel like a trapdoor rather than a setback.
- **Interstitial villain panels as pacing valves.** Shan-Yu's six scenes are all short (1–2 pages except the battle), all cross-cut into comedy sequences, and all end on a forward-pointing image (the arrow drawn, the doll sniffed, the smile toward the Imperial City). They keep a war present during forty pages of army-camp farce without requiring the A-story to be tense.
- **Comedy placed on grief, deliberately.** The script repeatedly follows its heaviest beats with a joke inside the same scene rather than after it — Fa Zhou wailing in the rain (p.22) is followed within one page by the ancestors bickering about acupuncturists (p.25); Mulan's "I see nothing" (p.79) is answered by Mushu spitting on her helmet; the villain's death (p.94) is followed by "Mongolian barbecue." This is the animated-feature tonal contract made structural.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework
- **The mid-film exposure has no Vogler stage.** At p.76 the hero is unmasked, stripped of the reward granted three pages earlier, and abandoned by the mentor-adjacent ally who owes her his life. It is not the Ordeal (that was the avalanche), not the Road Back (that's p.80, when Shan-Yu resurfaces), and not the Resurrection. Vogler's model assumes the hero's *identity* is stable and only the hero's *circumstances* are at risk. Hudson's **Caught Shining** names it exactly; Murdock's **Death** covers its aftermath. This single beat is the strongest evidence that the Hero's Journey is the wrong primary lens for this script.
- **The Refusal of the Call is inverted.** Mulan never hesitates. The refusal is administered *to* her by Chi Fu — "Silence! ... teach your daughter to hold her tongue in a man's presence" (p.18) — and by her father — "I know my place! It is time you learned yours" (p.20). In a conformity story, the obstacle is the community, so the Refusal has to be spoken by the community.
- **The Mentor arrives after the threshold, and is an impostor.** Mushu is woken at p.23 and reaches Mulan at p.32 — 11 pages *after* the haircut. He is sent by nobody ("The Ancestors never sent me, they don't even like me," p.79), his stated goal is his own promotion (p.29), and his one decisive act — forging the orders (pp.56–58) — is what puts the army in Shan-Yu's path.
- **The Reward is a fiction's property.** "You are the craziest man I've ever met ... From now on, you have my trust" (p.73) is addressed to a person who does not exist. Murdock's *illusory boon* is precise here in a way Vogler's *Seizing the Sword* is not.
- **The elixir is refused twice.** Mulan turns down a seat on the Emperor's council (p.96) and her father sets the sword and crest aside without examining them (p.98). A Hero's Journey that ends by discarding the boon is arguably not a Hero's Journey.
- **Murdock breaks at the mother.** The Heroine's Journey requires a *Descent to the Goddess*, a reconnection with the feminine, and a healed mother/daughter split. Fa Li has no dialogue after p.22 and no scene with Mulan after p.15; Grandmother Fa's final function is a marriage gag (p.99). The reconciliation the script actually builds toward is with **Fa Zhou** — set up at p.5 (the teacups), broken at p.18 ("you dishonor me"), and paid at p.98.
- **The romance is vestigial by design.** Shang and Mulan-as-Mulan share four scenes: the exposure (p.76, he doesn't speak to her), the city gate (p.82, "You don't belong here"), the roof (p.91, she knocks him out), and the plaza (p.97, "you fight good"). His arrival at the gate (p.99) resolves it in eleven lines. The genre expectation of a love-story climax is deliberately starved so the father can hold the emotional ending.
- **Musical-form deviations.** No villain song, no reprise, no duet, no song after 61%. The comic song is aborted mid-word (p.62). Two of the four numbers are sung by characters who have the heroine's identity wrong.
- **Two antagonists, two defeats, two mechanisms.** Shan-Yu is killed by a rocket he never sees (p.94); Chi Fu is destroyed by a promotion offered to someone else (p.96) and exits by fainting down a staircase with his ink spilling (p.96). The plot villain gets an action climax; the theme villain gets a bureaucratic one.
- **Animation-specific artefact.** The out-of-order sequence numbering (SEQ 8.6 at p.30 before SEQ 7.5 at p.31; SEQ 7.3 at p.48; SEQ 17.5 at p.78 before SEQ 16 at p.80) and the missing sequences 2, 3, 11–14 and 18 are evidence that this structure is a *revision* of an earlier one, and that the burned-village and rock-shelter sequences were moved. Any timing lesson drawn from this script should be read as the result of restructuring, not first-draft instinct.

## Lessons for the knowledge base
1. **When your protagonist's problem is conformity rather than a monster, use the Virgin's Promise as the primary map and let the Hero's Journey run the subplot.** Mulan's Hero's Journey stages are all present and all belong to *Ping*, who is exposed and discarded at p.76 (75%); the story that finishes the film is Mulan's — Secret World (21–75%), Caught Shining (p.76), Gives Up What Kept Her Stuck (p.79), Chooses Her Light (p.85). If your act-three problem is "my hero already won at 67% and I still have 30 pages," check whether you have two protagonists in one body.
2. **Site the I-Want song (or scene) *after* the humiliation, not before it.** "Reflection" lands at pp.15–16 (15%), immediately after "you will never bring your family honor" and the villagers' "what a shame." Placing the want after the cost has been demonstrated turns a statement of desire into a statement of price — "if I were truly to be myself / I would break my fam'ly's heart" — and buys the audience's permission for everything that follows.
3. **Grant the reward in full and confiscate it within five pages.** Shang says "From now on, you have my trust" (p.73) and offers her the honour of presenting Shan-Yu's sword to the Emperor (p.74); by p.77 she is on the ground being sentenced. The compression is the effect. A reversal that takes twenty pages reads as a downturn; one that takes four reads as a trapdoor.
4. **Make the wound that exposes the hero a direct consequence of the heroism.** Mulan is unmasked because a medic has to treat the injury she took saving the captain (pp.73–76). Exposure by accident is a coincidence; exposure caused by the virtue itself is structure, and it retroactively makes the whole second act feel inevitable.
5. **Plant the object of the protagonist's failure and make it the weapon of her victory.** The fan flies out of her hands at p.9 and torques the sword out of Shan-Yu's at p.92. Same object, same protagonist, opposite competence — and the reversal argues the theme without a line of dialogue. Do this with the specific thing your character was humiliated for being bad at.
6. **Stage the climax twice under two identities.** The avalanche (pp.66–68) and the palace roof (pp.90–94) are the same manoeuvre — aim a projectile at the villain, let the environment do the killing, end holding Shang. The first is credited to a man and punished when the truth comes out; the second is credited to her. Repetition-with-changed-attribution is how a script argues that the problem was never the competence.
7. **Split the antagonist into a clock and a thesis.** Shan-Yu supplies deadline and spectacle in six short interstitial panels and never engages the heroine until 89%; Chi Fu supplies the actual opposition and is present at every humiliation (pp.18, 42, 76, 84, 94). This lets you run forty pages of comedy without losing tension, and it lets your climax kill the plot villain while your *resolution* dismantles the thematic one (p.96, the Emperor gives Mulan his job).
8. **Give the comic sidekick a stated selfish want, then let his want cause the third act.** Mushu says it flat at p.29 — "I'll make Mulan a hero and they'll have to give me my pedestal back!" — forges the orders that march the troops into Shan-Yu's path (pp.56–58), and confesses in the same scene where the heroine confesses hers (p.79). The comic layer becomes load-bearing, and the sidekick's own restoration (p.100) earns its own beat.
9. **Use the song (or the sequence) to compress the phase, not to decorate it.** "I'll Make a Man Out of You" does the entire Tests-Allies-Enemies phase in two pages at the exact midpoint (pp.47–48); "Honor to Us All" does a whole ideology in four (pp.7–11). And when you need to end a comic sequence hard, **cut the song off mid-word**: "A GIRL WORTH FIGHTING —" (p.62) is severed by black smoke, and the burned village that follows needs no dialogue at all.
10. **Let the protagonist refuse the trophy, and let someone else set it down.** She declines the council seat at p.96 ("I think I've been away from home long enough"); she brings home the sword and the crest at p.98 and her father *puts them aside* to hold her instead — "The greatest gift and honor, is having you for a daughter." Two refusals, one from her and one from the person whose approval started the plot, are what convert an adventure ending into a thematic one.
