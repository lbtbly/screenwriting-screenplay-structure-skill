# The Father (2020) — Structural Beat Map
**Source:** /mnt/user-data/uploads/Screenplay planning/Ressources/Script Library/Feature Misc/thefather-screenplay.pdf · **Writers:** Florian Zeller and Christopher Hampton (title page: "a film by FLORIAN ZELLER / screenplay by FLORIAN ZELLER and CHRISTOPHER HAMPTON"; from Zeller's play *Le Père*) · **Format:** feature, FINAL SCRIPT (production draft — scene numbers locked, nine slugs retained as DELETED: 40C, 41A, 41Ba, 41D, 41E, 46, 57A, 57B, 63F) · **Genre:** psychological drama / subjective chamber film · **Length:** 87 pp printed, continuous 1–87 (89 PDF pages; one unnumbered title page + one "ii." design note)
**Framework lens(es):** PRIMARY — subjective / unreliable structure, where the form *enacts* dementia rather than depicting it. SECONDARY — puzzle-film architecture (a ledger of continuity violations as escalation steps); and the play-to-film adaptation problem (space, POV, and the final location reveal).

Page citations use the script's **printed** page numbers (top-right of each page, "THE FATHER - FINAL n"). Printed page = PDF page − 2. Percentages are of 87 printed pages.

## Logline
An eighty-year-old man in London keeps losing his watch, his flat, and his daughter's face — and the film, told entirely from inside his failing perception, keeps losing them with him, until the room he has been fighting to stay in turns out to be a care-home bedroom he was moved into months ago.

## Structure overview
The script has no act structure in the conventional sense. Its architecture is **the set itself**, and the design note on page ii states the machine outright: the whole film is one studio space whose décor mutates through five numbered states — "In every case, the space is identical. The décor is the only indication that we might be in a different place. The intended aim is to create uncertainty and the impression of being simultaneously in the same location and somewhere different - ultimately, a hospital."

So the "acts" are **flat states**, and the sluglines carry the numbering. Each state strips furniture and adds contradictions; the audience's job (and Anthony's) is to work out which room they are in, and the answer arrives only at p.79.

| 2 | "2" — BEDROOM 2, LIVING ROOM 2… | 17–47 | 21–57 | 24–66% | Anne and Paul's flat, where he is a lodger | That the flat was always Anne's and the first act was his distortion |

| 5 | "5" — BEDROOM 5 | 63A | 76–79 | 87–91% | "Clearly a different bedroom" | The reveal begins: Anne is wearing a coat |
| — | HOSPITAL | 63B–66A | 79–87 | 91–100% | A care home; Catherine and Bill are staff | Everything preceding was replay, recast and reshuffle |

Four **stationary empty-room shots** punctuate the stages — p.23 ("the CAMERA CONTEMPLATES the empty room"), p.57, p.71, p.79 — the same framing four times. Three times it reads as a chapter card. The fourth time ("Same stationary empty SHOT. We are now clearly in a hospital") it *is* the reveal. The device that organised the film turns out to have been the answer all along.

The only causally-forward storyline is **Anne's**, and it is told almost silently in the handful of scenes Anthony is not present for: the doctor's card pocketed (p.46), the card turned over and over and the number dialled (p.57), the coat (p.76), the taxi pulling away (p.79).

**Shape of the ratchet:** the violation *type* never de-escalates. Fact (p.11) → space (p.16) → identity (p.18) → eyewitness (p.19) → causal order (p.23) → narrator (p.34) → scene repetition (pp.49/55) → prop migration (p.58) → mid-scene substitution (p.58) → violence (p.67) → ally defection (p.75) → location (p.79). Twenty violations arrive before the halfway mark and twenty after, but the second half's are categorically worse. That monotonic ordering is what allows a film with no plot to feel like it is accelerating.

**Facts that survive both readings:** Anne exists and loves him. Lucy is gone. Paris is real. The watch matters. There is a park outside the window. Everything else the film asserts is revoked at least once.

## Storylines
- **A — Anthony's decline (the surface).** The only storyline the audience thinks it is watching. Its milestones are losses of competence, not events: the watch accusation (pp.2–5), the boast of "two watches… one in my head" (p.28), the tap-dancer confabulation (p.30), the cruelty aria (pp.32–33), forgetting why he came into the kitchen (p.54), pyjamas at seven in the evening (p.69), "Who exactly am I?" (p.84), "I want my mummy" (p.85), "losing all my leaves" (p.86). It has no goal and no obstacle — it is a subtraction sequence, and the script substitutes the *rate* of subtraction for rising action.
- **B — Anne's decision (the hidden spine).** The only causally-connected storyline in the film, and the only one with a protagonist who acts: "We have to talk, Dad" (p.5) → the announcement of Paris (pp.7–9) → hiring Laura (pp.24–33) → the cracked cup and the minute of crying (p.34) → the strangling fantasy (pp.34–35) → the doctor's card pocketed (p.46) → Paul's pressure, twice (pp.49–50, 55–56) → the card turned over and the number dialled (p.57) → the coat in the doorway (p.76) → "I think you might be better off here" (p.77) → the taxi and the last look back (p.79) → Paris, months ago (p.82). It is almost entirely wordless at its decisive moments, and it is visible only in the scenes Anthony is absent from — the scenes the film has trained us not to trust.
- **C — Lucy (the absence that powers everything).** Told exclusively by omission: the painting Anne looks at when she needs help (p.6); "she was always my favorite" (p.29); Laura's resemblance (pp.31, 62); "she still used to call me 'little daddy'" (p.43); the painting gone from the wall and denied (pp.57–58); "About her accident." — "What accident?" (p.62); the hospital dream where she is lying on a bed (p.70); "I do too, Dad, I miss her too. We all miss her" (p.79). C is the film's true grief; A and B are its displacement. Anthony's terror of being abandoned by Anne is the survivable version of a loss he cannot hold.
- **Convergence.** All three lines meet on pp.78–79, in the care-home bedroom, inside nine lines of dialogue: Anne states the decision (B), Anthony registers it as abandonment (A), and asks after his sister — "If you knew how much I missed her" (C). His response to hearing that Lucy is gone is to comfort *Anne*: "he rediscovers his role as a father: he strokes her cheek." The convergence is then formally sealed at p.86, where the leaves speech fuses all three — the decline, the leaving, and the dead daughter — into one image, which the final shot (p.87) makes literal.
- **D — the staff (structural, not dramatic).** The MAN and the WOMAN have no arc; they have a *function*. They appear at pp.11 and 18 as violations, recur at pp.65 and 75 as escalations, and are named at pp.83–84 as Bill and Catherine, a shift handover. The film's most frightening characters turn out to be its most ordinary.

## Structural devices
- **The watch.** The film's one countable object, and the only question the audience can always answer even when it cannot answer "where are we?". Accused stolen (pp.2–5) → found in the cupboard (p.6) → missing before Laura arrives (p.27) → found (p.27) → the boast: "I have two watches. I've always had two. One on my wrist and the other in my head" (p.28) → missing, and he covets Paul's (pp.38–42) → found by Anne, with the fork (p.42) → "Is it because of your watch? I found it, look!" (p.69) → gone from the hospital bedside table (p.80) → confirmed absent from his wrist (p.82) → "I know where my watch is. On my wrist. That I do know. For the journey" (p.86). The internal watch is the one that has stopped; the external one is a prosthesis for time, and the film ends with him certain he is wearing it and wrong.
- **The flat as shifting stage.** Formalised in the design note (p.ii) and executed through the sluglines: unnumbered → 2 (p.21) → 3 (p.57) → 4 (p.71) → 5 (p.76) → HOSPITAL (p.79). Furniture is subtracted at each stage; the four stationary empty-room shots (pp.23, 57, 71, 79) are the transitions. The audience can *see* the film counting down without knowing what it is counting to.
- **Recasting as a plot device rather than a stunt.** Five substitutions, each at a higher level of intrusion: a new Paul between scenes (p.11), a new Anne between scenes (p.18), the same substitution reversed (p.35: "Unlike the MAN, there seems nothing hostile about him"), a substitution *inside* a scene (p.58: Anne's voice, Laura's body), and finally a substitution that the sane character insists on (p.75: "Say hello to Laura"). The device carries plot because the swaps also carry dialogue: Paul's threat line (p.44) migrates to the MAN verbatim (pp.66–67), and Lucy's name for her father, "little daddy" (p.43), migrates to Anne (p.69).
- **The repeated chicken dinner.** The film's metronome. Anne buys a chicken (p.13) → it is handed over and then denied ("What chicken? … A CHICKEN!", pp.19–21) → it cooks and she burns herself (p.36) → she buys it again (p.47) → prepares it again (p.49) → the dinner is eaten, with the institution conversation, twice (pp.50–54, 55–56) → "Let's eat our chicken. Shall we? You like chicken, don't you?" (p.69). A domestic ritual is the perfect vehicle for repetition-with-variation: it is plausible that it recurs, so the audience notices the *differences* rather than the recurrence.
- **The bag and the leaves.** A child outside plays with a plastic bag; Anthony watches "as fascinated as if he were trying to uncover some mystery," smiles, "But then his smile brutally disappears" (p.57). It is the film's only image of pure external contingency — an object with no will being moved by wind — and it is planted 29 pages before "I feel as if I'm losing all my leaves, one after another. The branches! And the wind…" (p.86) and 30 pages before the final shot of wind in the trees (p.87). (The script specifies only "a plastic bag"; the colour is the film's addition.)
- **The fork, the bags, the ghost rectangle, the postcard.** Four pieces of physical evidence that let the audience adjudicate a scene it has been told to disbelieve. The fork is picked up at p.10, pocketed at p.13 and found in the cupboard at p.42 — proof the "intruder" sequence happened. The shopping bags land "in the exact position ANTHONY found them" (p.23 vs p.10) — proof that the film is running out of order. The lighter rectangle on the wall (p.58) proves the painting existed after Anne denies it. And the postcard whose reverse is the kitchen poster (p.83 vs p.10) proves the whole flat was assembled from objects within reach of a hospital bed.
- **The nurse, the window, and the curtain.** The film's opening action is Anne opening curtains he had closed (p.1); the reveal's key gesture is Anne opening curtains onto a park (p.77); the last camera move is a PAN to a window (p.87), out of the room and out of his head entirely. Catherine's closing speech (pp.86–87) replaces the film's structure with a domestic loop — dressed, park, lunch, siesta, another walk — which is exactly what a life reduced to a day looks like; and she delivers it while rocking him "like a child in its mother's arms," a role reversal completed one page after he asked for his mummy.

**What the stage could not do (the adaptation problem).** Zeller's play already had the mutating single set and the recasting; the screenplay's additions are all about *point of view* and *elsewhere*.
1. **A subjective sound rule the stage has no access to.** The film's opening music turns out to be coming through Anthony's headphones (p.1). A theatre audience shares the auditorium's sound; a film audience can be told, in one cut, that everything they hear belongs to one man.
2. **Exteriors, all of which belong to Anne.** The street and tube (p.1), the Indian shop (pp.13, 47), the lift and landing (p.44), the surgery (pp.44–46), the taxis (pp.46, 79), the hospital park (pp.79, 87). Anthony never has an exterior of his own until the camera abandons him for one at p.87. The film partitions the world so that the daughter has a life and the father has a room — a division a single-set play cannot make.
3. **The window as the set's alibi.** At p.21 the décor has changed but the view is identical to scene 1, "So as far as he's concerned, he's obviously still in his flat." Film can put contradiction and proof in the same frame; on stage the audience simply accepts a new set.
4. **Cross-cutting, which lets a character be alone.** Anne alone: the shattered cup (p.34), the strangling fantasy (pp.34–35), the cigarette at the window (p.42), her father's suits and polished shoes (p.47), the doctor's card and the crystal pyramid (p.57), the taxi (p.79). These scenes are the entire B-story, and they exist only because the camera can leave the room while the other character waits. They also carry the film's most important structural trick — the audience adopts them as objective, and p.35 takes that away.
5. **The empty-room shot as a chapter card.** Four identical stationary compositions (pp.23, 57, 71, 79) is a purely cinematic form of punctuation, and it converts the reveal from information into recognition: the audience has been looking at the answer three times already.
6. **A sound bridge that asserts causality.** Anne's hold muzak carries over the empty, packing-case-filled flat and resolves into "Doctor Odgers speaking… Hello? Hello?" (p.57). Film can say "this phone call is what emptied this room" without a word of dialogue.
7. **The reveal as a cut, not an announcement.** The care home is disclosed by a costume detail (Anne's coat, p.76), a line of small talk ("That's what all the residents say," p.77), and a change of slugline (p.79) — three tiny, non-verbal moves. On stage the same reveal has to be carried by set change and exposition.
8. **Faces.** Recasting on stage reads as convention; on screen it reads as an assault on identity, because film audiences take a face as a fact. The device is simply more violent in the medium the adaptation moved to — which is why the script can afford to escalate it five times.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework
**What it abandons.**
- **A protagonist with a goal.** Anthony wants only to stay where he is and to know what time it is. There is no plan, no pursuit, no escalating attempt.
- **An antagonist.** Paul is a tired man who wants his life back (pp.52–56); the MAN is Paul refracted through fear; Anne is doing the only sane thing available. Nobody in the film wants anything cruel.
- **The midpoint reversal.** At ~50% (pp.44–46) the film goes to a doctor's surgery — an apparently objective scene — and gains no new information the audience can use; the "reversal" is that even the outside world is made of the same rooms.
- **Dramatic irony in its usual direction.** Conventionally the audience knows more than the character. Here the audience knows *less* than every character except Anthony — Anne, Paul, Laura, Catherine and Bill all know where they are. The film's suspense comes from the audience being the second-most-confused person in the room.
- **Time.** No scene is reliably dated; there are no cards, no dissolves marking gaps, no seasons. The script deletes the ordinary tools (40C, 41A, 41D, 41E, 46, 57A, 57B, 63F are all cut) and refuses to replace them.
- **The act break as decision.** The only real decision in the film (p.57) is made silently by a supporting character, and the film does not treat it as a turn.

**What it substitutes as forward momentum.**
- **A monotonic escalation ladder.** The disorientation ledger above never de-escalates by category. The audience cannot predict the next violation but can feel that it will be worse, which functions exactly as rising stakes.
- **A puzzle contract.** From p.11 the audience is set the task of reconciling contradictions. The engine is the viewer's own inference, refreshed every few pages by a new incompatible fact.
- **An emptying set as a visible clock.** Furniture leaves at pp.23, 57, 71, 79. The audience can literally see how much time is left without knowing what the deadline is.
- **A silent causal spine.** The B-story is a straight line through a scrambled film. Even when the audience cannot date a scene, it can tell that Anne is moving toward something.
- **Repetition with one variable.** The reprise at pp.55–56 differs from pp.49–50 in exactly one action (he refuses to sit down); Laura's arrival at p.75 differs from p.24 in one word and one body. The changed element carries the story beat that a new scene would normally carry.
- **A withheld sentence.** "If you refuse to have a carer, I'm going to have to…" (pp.9–10), asked three times and never answered, is the film's true dramatic question. It is finally completed 68 pages later, by a room: "I think you might be better off here" (p.77).

**The residual tension.** The method costs the film a second act in any usual sense — pp.35–56 are its weakest stretch precisely because they are its most conventional, a stretch of coherent domestic drama where the machine idles. The script's answer is to spend that stretch stockpiling material (the threat line, the chicken, the doctor's card, Paul's contempt, the strangling blocking) that the last third will recycle at speed. Everything that feels like padding at 45% is ammunition at 75%.

## Lessons for the knowledge base
1. **Give the audience a false anchor, then revoke it at ~40%.** The Anne-only scenes (pp.23–24, 33–34) are engineered to read as objective relief, so that the strangling (pp.34–35) — presented straight, in long shot, held "for some time," then annulled by a single line — can prove that no track in this film is safe. Do the revocation *early enough that the audience must live under the new rule*, and *late enough that they have already committed to the anchor*.
2. **Order violations by category and never de-escalate.** Fact (p.11) → space (p.16) → identity (p.18) → eyewitness (p.19) → causal order (p.23) → narrator (p.34) → repeated scene (p.55) → migrating props (p.58) → mid-scene substitution (p.58) → violence (p.67) → the sane character's endorsement (p.75) → location (p.79). Randomised confusion reads as sloppiness; a ratchet reads as an accelerating story.
3. **Hand the disoriented viewer one countable object.** The watch lets the audience keep score across 87 pages when it cannot keep place. Pick something small, portable, checkable, and thematically loaded — then let the final assertion about it be false (p.86 vs p.82) so the ending lands without a line of explanation.
4. **Plant physical evidence that lets a discredited scene be adjudicated later.** The fork (pp.10, 13 → 42) proves the "hallucinated" sequence happened; the bags "in the exact position" (p.10 → 23) prove the film is out of order; the ghost rectangle (p.58) proves the painting was real; the postcard that is the poster (p.10 → 83) proves the whole set was built from one room's contents. Unreliable structure needs a small number of hard facts or it becomes weightless.
5. **Repeat a scene and change exactly one variable; the variable is the beat.** pp.49–50 and pp.55–56 are identical dialogue — the script even writes "A reprise" — and the only difference is that the second time he will not sit down. pp.24 and 75 differ by one word ("early"/"late") and one actress. Repetition is not filler; it is the cheapest available escalation once the audience has learned the first version.
6. **Put the reveal in a subordinate clause, in a minor character's mouth.** "That's what all the residents say" (p.77) does what a confession scene would do clumsily. Support it with a costume detail placed one page earlier (Anne's coat, p.76) and a slugline change one page later (p.79) — three small moves instead of one big one.
7. **Show the ending early, disguised as the least reliable thing in the film.** The hospital corridor and Lucy on a bed (p.70, at 80%) are presented as a nightmare and are in fact the only accurate location in the first four-fifths. The reveal at p.79 then reads as recognition rather than as a trick played on the audience — the difference between a twist that earns a rewatch and one that earns resentment.
8. **Let a formal device carry the act breaks, then let the device be the answer.** The stationary empty-room shot appears at pp.23, 57, 71 as punctuation and at p.79 as the reveal. If the shape of the film is the subject of the film, its transitions can do plot work.
9. **Give the causal storyline to the character who is not the POV, and tell it silently.** Anne's decision — the card pocketed (p.46), turned over and dialled (p.57), the coat (p.76), the taxi (p.79) — is the only chain of cause and effect in the script, and it contains almost no dialogue. A subjective film still needs a spine; it just cannot be the subjective character's.
10. **End by leaving the head you were trapped in.** "I feel as if I'm losing all my leaves, one after another. The branches! And the wind…" (p.86) is followed by a PAN to a window and a cut to wind in real trees (p.87) — the film's only image that is nobody's perception, and the only one the protagonist is not in. The metaphor becomes fact at the exact moment the perceiver stops being able to supply it.
