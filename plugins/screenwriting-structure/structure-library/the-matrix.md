# The Matrix (1999) — Structural Beat Map
**Source:** /mnt/user-data/uploads/Screenplay planning/Ressources/Script Library/Feature Misc/the_matrix.pdf · **Writers:** Larry and Andy Wachowski (title page) · **Format:** feature · **Genre:** science fiction / action (messianic quest) · **Length:** numbering runs 1–126 with six A-pages (64A, 75A, 80A, 88A, 101A, 125A) = 132 physical script pages; 133 PDF pages incl. title page · **Draft:** "NUMBERED SHOOTING SCRIPT, March 29, 1998"; revision slugs dated 3/9/98 (50 pp), 3/22/98 (6 pp), 3/29/98 (1 p)
**Framework lens(es):** PRIMARY — Syd Field three-act paradigm (setup / confrontation / resolution; inciting incident, Plot Point I, midpoint, Plot Point II, climax). SECONDARY 1 — Vogler's 12-stage Hero's Journey, mapped stage by stage. SECONDARY 2 — the eight-sequence method (Frank Daniel / Gulino).

Page citations are the script's **printed** page numbers (top-right of every page after p.1). PDF page = printed page + 1 through p.125, +2 from p.125A on. Percentages are of 126 printed pages.

## Logline
A hacker who suspects something is wrong with the world is shown that it is a simulation grown over the corpse of the twenty-first century — and is told he is the prophesied man who can break it; when the prophecy is revoked and his mentor is captured, he has to choose to believe in himself without any evidence, die for it, and come back.

## Structure overview
The Matrix is the cleanest three-act machine in the modern canon: **Plot Point I lands at p.29–30 (23–24%), the midpoint at p.60–63 (48–50%), Plot Point II at p.95 (75%), the climax at pp.113–123 (90–98%)**. What makes it worth studying is not that it hits the marks but *how it distributes information*: the film front-loads a rulebook, then spends its entire third act cashing rules planted in Act Two, and it splits its midpoint into two halves — an **audience** midpoint (Cypher's betrayal, p.60–61, which the hero never learns about) and a **hero** midpoint (the Oracle's "you're not the One," p.73, deliberately displaced to 58%).

**Where the frameworks disagree.** Field's Plot Point I and Vogler's Crossing the Threshold are usually treated as the same beat; here they are five pages apart and the difference matters. The **red pill (p.30)** is Field's turn — it is a decision that spins the story irreversibly, and the script frames it as a choice between two *narratives* ("the story ends"). The **pod (pp.33–34)** is Vogler's crossing — the physical departure from the Ordinary World, and Neo is unconscious for the end of it. My reading: the script is unusual in giving the hero agency for the turn and none for the crossing, which is exactly right for a story about a man who chooses to be told the truth and then has to survive it. Treat p.30 as Plot Point I and pp.30–34 as an extended threshold.

The second disagreement is the **midpoint**. Field's midpoint should be a turn for the protagonist; the beat that sits at 48–50% (Cypher's steak, then "I'm taking Neo to see her") turns the *plot* and the *audience's knowledge*, not the hero. The hero's reversal — the Oracle's "you're not the One" — sits at 58%. Both readings are defensible, but the script is better described as having deliberately split the function: irony at centre, reversal at 58%, so that the fall from p.73 runs downhill without interruption into the trap at p.76.

| 6 | 64–83 | 51–66% | *What will the Oracle say?* → *can they get out?* | "You're not the One" plus a scheduled sacrifice; then déjà vu, the bricked windows, Mouse's death, the wet-wall. Resolves with the worst available outcome: Morpheus captured, prophecy dead |
| 7 | 83–98 | 66–78% | *Do they save Morpheus or kill him?* | Cypher's massacre, Trinity's "...yes," Smith's virus speech, Tank at the plug. Resolves with Neo choosing to go in *without* the prophecy — "Guns. Lots of guns." |
| 8 | 98–126 | 78–100% | *Can Neo save Morpheus — and can he become what he no longer claims to be?* | Lobby, elevator, roof, helicopter, rescue, subway duel, chase, death, kiss, bullets stopped, EMP, coda |

Sequence 8 runs 28 pages (22%) — nearly double a standard sequence, and honestly it hinges at **p.109–112**, where Morpheus is extracted and Neo is left alone. A defensible nine-movement reading is: 8a "the rescue" (98–109, 78–87%, question: *can they get him out?* — answered yes) and 8b "the resurrection" (109–126, 87–100%, question: *will Neo run or turn?*). I keep the eight-sequence count because 8a's resolution (Morpheus saved) immediately generates 8b's question with no reset, but a writer studying pacing should treat this as two movements: the film's third act is structurally two sequences, and the second one belongs entirely to the hero.

## Storylines
- **A: Neo vs. the Matrix / Neo vs. his own disbelief.** The external plot (free Morpheus, survive the Agents) is a delivery system for the internal one. Trackable as a single-word spine — "believe" — passed hand to hand: Trinity/Cypher (p.2) → Morpheus (p.23) → Cypher's doubt (pp.59–60) → the Oracle's revocation (p.73) → Trinity's "…yes" (p.89) → Neo's "I believe I can bring him back" (p.96) → Tank (p.108) → Morpheus's "He's beginning to believe" (p.112) → "He is the one!" (p.123).
- **B: Neo & Trinity (the love story that is also the mechanism).** Planted p.12 (she finds him), p.52 (the dinner tray, the inhaled scent), p.65 (the sentence she cannot finish), p.72 (the Oracle: "I see why she likes you"), p.97 ("I'm coming with you"), p.110 (interrupted by a train), p.121 (spoken over a corpse), p.124 (the kiss on the ship). It converges with the A-story at p.72 via one line — "Being the One is just like being in love. Nobody can tell you you're in love. You just know it" — and *becomes* the A-story at p.122, where love is literally the resurrection technology.
- **C: Cypher's betrayal.** Seeded p.53 ("why doesn't he take him to the Oracle?"), deepened pp.58–60 (whiskey, "five before you"), consummated pp.60–61 (steak), signalled visually p.64A (the phone in the trash), executed pp.86–90, ended p.90. Cypher is the film's control group: the man who took the same red pill and reached the opposite conclusion.
- **D: Agent Smith's own escape story.** He is set up as a machine (pp.3–4, 20–22), starts leaking affect (p.99: "Agent Smith hides his knotting fist... It is something that isn't supposed to happen to Agents"), removes his earpiece and confesses to a prisoner (pp.99–100: "I hate this place. This zoo. This prison... I must get out of here, I must get free"), and dies screaming with "his calm machine-like expression shredding with pure rage" (p.123). He wants exactly what Neo wants — out — which is why the film's last physical act is Neo going *inside* him.
- **E: Zion / the war (offscreen).** Introduced p.45, weaponised p.93 (the codes), never dramatized. The global stakes are entirely verbal; the dramatized stakes are one man and one relationship.

## Structural devices
- **Rules taught on a schedule, paid on a schedule.** Every third-act physical event is licensed by a specific earlier line: bullets → p.55 ("when you're ready, you won't have to") paid half at p.103 and fully at p.122; death → p.52 ("the body cannot live without the mind") paid at p.120; EMP → p.57 paid at p.124; "There is no spoon" → p.70 paid at p.102 and p.122; déjà vu → p.77 paid within the same page. The film never invents a rule in Act Three.
- **The phone as spine.** The story opens inside a trace program and a phone call (pp.1–8), moves through a FedEx cellphone (p.15), a tapped landline (p.22), a hotel handset wired to headphones (p.30), Tank's operator line, a phone dropped in a garbage can (p.64A), a receiver shot out of the air (p.111), a phone stolen from a businessman mid-sprint (p.116), and closes by reversing the opening move — coming back *out* through the mouthpiece holes (p.125). Scene 219 is slugged "as in the opening."
- **Falling and leaping, four times.** Trinity's impossible rooftop jump (p.6) sets the standard; Neo fails it (p.51); Neo hurls himself into "the wide blue empty space" to catch Morpheus (p.106); Neo flies (p.126). One image, four values.
- **Mirrors and reflections.** The cracked mirror that heals and swallows him (pp.30–32); "residual self image" (p.39); the spoon in which "the entire room is reflected" (p.70); a bullet that shatters his reflection in a truck mirror (p.117); the bullet whose "distorted reflection morphs, becoming the 'real' image" (p.122).
- **"Welcome to the real world," three times, degrading.** Morpheus, sincerely (p.34); Cypher, sarcastically to Neo (p.75); Cypher, while murdering Apoc (p.88A).
- **Naming as arc.** Smith says "Mr. Anderson" at pp.20, 111, 114, 121; Neo answers with "My name is Neo" at p.114, and Smith's second "Good-bye, Mr. Anderson" (p.121) is the last time anyone calls him that.
- **The circular location.** Room 303, Heart O' The City Hotel — Trinity's trap in scene 2 becomes Neo's tomb in scene 203. The script gives Smith a beat of *recognising the hotel* (scene 197, p.119) so the audience gets the rhyme before the payoff.
- **Watching-on-screens as an emotional relay.** The crew watch the dojo (pp.48–49), the jump (pp.50–51), the trap, the duel, the flatline, the code dissolving into colour (p.123). Whenever Neo cannot narrate his own experience, the script cuts to people watching a monitor and telling us what it means — an exposition device that doubles as reaction shots.
- **Draft artefacts as structural evidence.** Fourteen A/B-numbered inserts (A10, A23, A40, **A71**, A72/B72, A99, A105/A106, A124, A144, A195/B195, A201) and eight OMITTED scenes (83, 98, 119, 136, 160, 166, 189, 191). The inserts cluster on the midpoint betrayal and the final chase; the omissions cluster on the trap and the chase. Late-draft work on this script was: add the irony, cut the connective tissue.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework
- **The paradigm fits almost too well — and the interest is in the exceptions.** PP I at 23–24%, midpoint 48–50%, PP II at 75%, climax 90–98%: these are textbook. The instructive deviations are (a) the split midpoint, (b) the extended five-page threshold, (c) a third act that is structurally two sequences.
- **The midpoint belongs to the audience, not the hero.** Cypher's steak scene (pp.60–61) is at dead centre and the protagonist neither appears in it nor ever learns of it. Field's model has no slot for this. It works because the film's second half is *about* the gap between what is true and what the characters believe — installing an irony at the centre makes every subsequent scene double-tracked.
- **The prophecy is revoked, not fulfilled, at the hero's midpoint.** "Sorry, kid... you're waiting for something" (p.73) removes the protagonist's mandate at 58%, and the script never restores it before the climax — Neo enters Act Three explicitly saying "I'm not the One" (p.96). This is the opposite of the standard chosen-one shape, and it is the reason the film survives its own mythology: the hero acts on faith while the audience holds a prophecy he has rejected.
- **Vogler's Ordeal and Resurrection are each doubled.** Ordeal = the Oracle (symbolic, p.73) + Morpheus's capture (proxy death, p.83). Resurrection = self-naming (p.114) + literal death and revival (pp.120–122). The doubling is what lets a 126-page script sustain an eleven-page climax without a sag.
- **The Reward is not an object.** Vogler's "seizing the sword" here is a decision (p.96) rather than a possession, and the acquisition scene that *looks* like the sword beat — "Guns. Lots of guns." (p.98) — is deliberately placed *after* the belief has been paid for. Order matters: conviction, then armament.
- **Act One withholds the goal.** For 30 pages the protagonist has no objective except curiosity. Field's setup normally establishes a want; here the want is a *question*, stated at p.13 and answered at p.42, and the actual goal (save Morpheus) does not exist until p.95. The script gets away with it because the antagonists supply pressure from p.15 onward — pursuit substitutes for objective.
- **The global stakes are never dramatized.** Zion is described twice (pp.45, 93) and never seen. The script quietly demonstrates that a world-ending clock does not need a location — it needs a character (Morpheus) whose mind is the door.
- **Draft vs. release cut — the ending is materially different.** The script's closing V.O. (p.125) is a negotiation addressed to the machines and a statement of interior liberation: "I believe deep down, we both want this world to change... the Matrix can remain our cage or it can become our chrysalis... to be free, you cannot change your cage. You have to change yourself." The finished film replaces it with an outward threat aimed at the audience inside the Matrix ("I'm going to show these people what you don't want them to see"). The rewrite trades philosophy for promise and closes on spectacle rather than doctrine — a legible late-stage choice about what a final image is *for*. Related: Morpheus's explicit line "As in Baudrillard's vision, your whole life has been spent inside the map, not the territory" (p.39) is in this draft and out of the film; the book survives only as a prop (p.10).

## Lessons for the knowledge base
1. **Teach the physics before you explain the premise.** Eight pages of Trinity outrunning Agent Brown (pp.2–8) establish superhuman speed, impossible jumps, phone-booth exits and the antagonists' parity *without one word of exposition* — so that Morpheus's four-page Construct monologue at pp.38–42 explains a world the audience has already felt. Demonstration buys permission for exposition.
2. **Put the biggest exposition block immediately after Plot Point I.** The history of the machine war (pp.38–42, 30–33%) lands at the one moment the audience is desperate for it: the hero has just crossed and knows nothing. Curiosity is a resource with a short half-life; spend it in the first ten pages of Act Two, and end the block on an object (the coppertop battery) rather than a sentence.
3. **Plant every third-act rule in Act Two, on a wound or in a joke.** "The body cannot live without the mind" (p.52) is delivered while Neo stares at his own blood, 68 pages before the flatline. "When you're ready, you won't have to [dodge bullets]" (p.55) is delivered as a wisecrack. Rules planted in the middle of an emotional beat are absorbed and not remembered — which is exactly what a setup should be.
4. **Split the midpoint: irony for the audience, reversal for the hero.** The steak scene (pp.60–61, 48%) gives the audience knowledge the protagonist lacks; the Oracle (p.73, 58%) takes the protagonist's identity away. Two functions, two positions, ten pages apart. If your midpoint is doing too much work, this is the split to try.
5. **Have the hero fail the climactic skill at ~40%, in public.** Neo misses the jump at p.51, in front of the whole crew, and the script has Cypher say "Everyone falls the first time." The same physical action succeeds at p.106 (catching Morpheus) and is transcended at p.126 (flight). One image, tested three times, is worth three different set-pieces.
6. **Revoke the prophecy before the third act, and make the hero act anyway.** "I'm not the One, Trinity" (p.96) followed one line later by "Because I believe in something" is the whole design: the script strips the protagonist of every external justification so that the choice at Plot Point II is uncontaminated by destiny. If your protagonist has a mandate, take it away at 58% and see whether they still go.
7. **Withhold one sentence for 57 pages and let it do the climax.** Trinity's "she told me…" is interrupted at p.65, interrupted again by a train at p.110, and finally completed over Neo's corpse at p.121 — where it functions as the mechanism of resurrection, not merely its emotion. A deliberately unfinished line is a stronger setup than a stated one, because the audience holds the gap.
8. **Rhyme the climax's location with the opening's.** Room 303, Heart O' The City Hotel, is Trinity's trap in scene 2 and Neo's death in scene 203, and the script writes Smith a beat of *recognising the neon* (p.119) so the rhyme registers before the payoff. Choosing your first location as your last is free structure.
9. **Let allies attack the mentor so the villain doesn't have to.** Cypher's whiskey scene (pp.58–60) does more damage to Morpheus's authority than Agent Smith manages in the whole film, and it costs nothing in plot — it is one conversation. Doubt planted by a friend is cheaper and more corrosive than doubt planted by an enemy.
10. **Fuse your two climaxes onto one action.** At p.124 Neo dives for the phone on the same cut as Morpheus turns the EMP key: the Matrix plot and the hovercraft plot resolve in a single beat, and the kiss that ends the love story is the same gesture that resurrected the hero two pages earlier. When parallel worlds each have a clock, land them on one hand movement.
