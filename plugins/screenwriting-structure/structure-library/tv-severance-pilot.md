# Severance 1x01 "Good News About Hell" (2022) — Structural Beat Map

**Framework lens(es):** PRIMARY — streaming hour-drama structure, tested against the page (this draft carries **explicit printed act breaks**, so the "continuous run" model does not apply). SECONDARY 1 — the mystery-box engine: what the script raises vs. what it answers, and why the ration is sustainable. SECONDARY 2 — the split-protagonist device: Mark innie and Mark outie as two characters sharing one body, and one's ignorance as the other's engine.

Page citations are the script's **printed** page numbers (top-right header; printed p.1 is unnumbered). Offset: **printed p.N = PDF p.N+1** (PDF p.1 is the title page). ## Logline
A divorced, unemployed man at the bottom of his life accepts an offer to have his memory surgically split between work and home — and wakes on a boardroom table as a brand-new person with no past, no name and no way out, who spends his first eight hours in existence trying to find out what his other half has done to him.

## Structure overview
The pilot is built as **two half-episodes of one man**, welded at the midpoint. **Prologue + Acts 1–2 (pp.1–27, 51%) are entirely innie.** They run in real time across a single workday and raise almost every question the series will live on. **Acts 3–4 (pp.28–52, 53%–98%) are entirely outie**, a flashback opened by a card — `SCREEN TEXT: FIVE DAYS EARLIER` (p.28) — that answers the mechanical questions by *dramatising the other half of the same man* rather than by explaining anything. The two blocks meet on p.51, when the flashback's last scene turns out to be the videotape the innie is watching in the present.

That weld is the pilot's whole design. The audience is the only entity in the story that holds both halves at once; every character on the page is missing one of them.

Structural oddities worth flagging up front: **Act 2 is 16 pages — 30% of the script**, effectively two acts fused (office induction pp.12–22, break-in pp.23–27). And **three of the five act-outs are stillness, not jeopardy** (p.4 a door opening, p.27 a silent room, p.52 two people waiting). The hook is epistemic throughout: the audience is held by wanting to know, not by fearing what happens next.

**Counts and pacing.** 32 substantive questions raised; **12 answered in full, 4 answered in part, 16 left entirely open** — a raise-to-full-answer ratio of about **8:3**. But the distribution matters more than the ratio:

- **pp.1–27 (the innie half, 51% of the script): 19 questions raised, 3 answered** (#8 sunburn p.10, #14 training p.19, #16 the delayed video p.21).
- **pp.28–53 (the outie half): 13 raised, 13 answered or part-answered.**

So the pilot front-loads questions and back-loads answers — but it never pays for the answers with a speech. **It pays for them by changing protagonist.** The Act 3 card arrives at the exact moment the innie block has become unbearable (a man has just seen an eye in a box and heard a woman scream for her baby), and the next 25 pages are one long answer delivered as a completely different, funnier, sadder story.

**Why this is sustainable rather than merely withholding.** Four reasons, all visible on the page:

1. **It answers the mechanical questions and withholds only the moral ones.** How the amnesia works (p.42), who signed (p.50), why he can type (p.19), why the exit loops (p.9–10), how they knew about the bridge (p.40) — all answered, most of them cheaply. What is withheld is *meaning*: what the work is (#5), who the woman in the box is (#18), what happened to Petey (#10), what one half of a man wanted to say to the other (#29). A show that answers mechanism and withholds meaning can run for years; a show that withholds mechanism runs out of patience in four episodes.
2. **Answers are constructed to raise larger questions.** The rat demonstration (p.42) answers "how does severance work" and in the same breath asks "then what is being done to people who cannot remember it?" The vanished house (p.46–47) answers "how powerful is this company" by making the question worse. No answer in this pilot closes a door without opening a bigger one.
3. **The withholding is dramatised as policy, not authorial choice.** Helly states the ration ("Exactly four percent," p.21) and the reason for the delay ("once they've broken you some," p.21–22). Cobel refuses on camera (p.51) and in person ("You'll never know," p.52). Every unanswered question has a character standing next to it, refusing — so the audience's frustration has a target inside the fiction.
4. **The audience is given a privileged position it can feel.** Because we hold both halves (#9, #30), we are ahead of both protagonists at all times. Mystery-box fatigue comes from feeling behind; this pilot keeps you ahead of two characters while remaining behind the institution.

## The split protagonist
Mark I and Mark O share a body and share almost nothing else. The draft differentiates them with unusual discipline.

**By grammar.** Mark I speaks almost exclusively in **questions**: "Hello?" / "Where the hell am I!?" / "Who the hell are you?" / "What is this place?" / "Orientation for what?" / "Again, entrance to what?" / "Who are these—" / "What's the thing with the letters?" / "Is there any way out of here?" / "Do I have a family?" (pp.1–52). Mark O speaks almost exclusively in **deflections**: "Your foot smells like pregnancy" (p.28), "I'm not interviewing to be President of Russia" (p.29), "Actually, things have been really great since you put your hand on my shoulder three seconds ago" (p.29), and — asked point-blank about his weaknesses — "No." (p.33). The innie's line is an interrogative; the outie's line is a joke standing where an answer should be.

**By want.** Mark I wants **to know** and **to leave** — and pursues both physically, immediately, repeatedly (kicks the door p.2, sprints the office p.7–9, climbs a shaft p.24, breaks into a locked archive p.26). Mark O wants **not to feel** — and pursues it by driving away from a cat he has just hit (p.32). Cobel names it: "how many things in your life are you putting off feeling horrible about?" (p.38). This is the weld that makes the device more than a gimmick: **severance is the literal, purchasable form of the outie's character flaw.** He didn't buy a job; he bought his own avoidance, industrialised.

**By what each is denied.** The innie can act but cannot know. The outie can know but will not act. Each is the other's missing faculty, which is precisely why neither can be a whole protagonist and why the episode needs both.

**By the antagonist's rhetoric.** The clearest proof that Erickson is writing two characters, not one: **Cobel tells each Mark a story about her mother, and the mothers are theologically opposite.** To the innie, at his lowest: "my mother was Catholic. Not a very good Catholic… your angels just OD'd on heroin… Let's go find some new angels" (pp.10–11) — comfort, community, replacement. To the outie, five days earlier: "my mother was an atheist… there's good news and bad news about Hell… what human beings can imagine, they can create" (p.43) — philosophy, ambition, a product. Same salesperson, same anecdotal form, two entirely different pitches, because she is selling to two entirely different men.

**Structural rules governing the transitions.** The draft is strict about this, and the rules are worth naming:

1. **The two worlds never share a scene.** The only contact between them is (a) recorded media — the VHS tape (pp.50–51) — and (b) Cobel and Milchick, who exist in both because they are **unsevered**. The unsevered character is the only continuity, and therefore the only one who can lie to both halves.
2. **A transition is always marked by a threshold object.** The lobby exit door (p.9), the archive door (p.51), the lobby glass doors (p.53). The severance switch is never depicted; a doorway is.
3. **In-episode switches are elisions, not events.** The `JUMP TO:` on p.9 removes the outie's afternoon entirely. Elapsed time is then **evidenced on the body**: sunburnt hands, a black suit, a calmer nervous system, a guard with an ice pack (p.10). This is the single most transferable craft move in the script — you can render an unrepresentable transition by cutting it out and letting the *residue* carry it.
4. **The big switch is a card, not a cut.** `BLACKNESS. SCREEN TEXT: FIVE DAYS EARLIER.` (p.28) at the act break; `FOUR DAYS LATER` (p.49) inside the flashback. Where the innie/outie boundary is *dramatic*, it uses a threshold; where it is merely *chronological*, it uses text. The script never confuses the two.
5. **The return is a match, not a jump.** The flashback does not end and cut back; its final scene *becomes* the tape the innie is watching (pp.50–51). The audience's knowledge and the innie's knowledge briefly touch — and then diverge again, because he has seen four percent of what we have.
6. **The innie's day ends in blackout, not in a transition.** p.53: he stares at his reflection, pushes the glass door, and the script writes one word: "Darkness." The innie doesn't travel home; he stops existing. Rendering the character's death as a fade is the reason the tag lands.

**One character's ignorance as the other's engine.** The pilot runs on a closed loop: the outie's decision (p.49, "Yes") creates the innie; the innie's ignorance (pp.1–27) is the outie's purchase; and the innie's only possible act of rebellion — finding the tape — returns him to the outie's face and his outie's request to speak to him, denied (p.51). The engine is that **neither half can free itself without the other's consent** (p.48: "Both halves of you would need to request it"), and the two can never meet. That is a series, not an episode.

## Storylines
- **A — Mark I's first day (pp.1–27, 51–52).** Wake, refuse, comply, escape, fail, be inducted, be befriended, break the rules, find the tape, learn 4% and be denied the rest. A complete arc inside a single working day. Ends where it began: in a locked building, with a locked door, in the dark.
- **B — Mark O's recruitment (pp.28–52).** Five days earlier: unemployment, dependency, a dead cat, a moral failure, a return, a drugging, a kidnapping, a pitch, a refusal, an erasure, a yes, a consent tape. This is the "premise pilot" of a conventional series, relocated into the back half of a mystery.
- **C — the Culling Office crew and the missing Petey (pp.12–23, 24, 26).** Helly, Dylan and Irving arrive as a functioning micro-society with its own manners, rituals and grief. Helly is the active agent (map, warnings, the four-percent rule); Irving supplies the pathos; Dylan supplies the enforcement. Petey is the absence they organise around.
- **D — management (pp.5–7, 15, 23–24, 26, 51–53).** Cobel and Milchick's B-plot is a workplace comedy about a botched onboarding — the pants, the apology, "The real problem's my personality," "How did that feel to you?" "Bad." "It was very bad." It is played straight and runs directly into the tag, where Cobel is revealed to be someone else's Milchick.
- **E — the outside conspiracy (pp.44–49).** Officers Irons and Green, Kaia's angels, the vanished house, the Port-o-Potty speaker. Nine pages establishing that the company's reach extends past its walls and that the world outside will not help. Almost entirely unresolved by design.

## Series engine
1. **Two irreconcilable points of view on one life, structurally incapable of meeting.** Every episode can be split between them; every discovery by one is a secret kept from the other. The pilot proves the format works by doing it once.
2. **A locked exit with a stated key.** "Both halves of you would need to request it" (p.48) is a finish line the audience can see and the characters cannot reach — the ideal long-form goal, because progress toward it is measurable and blockable.
3. **Work whose purpose is unknown to the workers.** "It's called data culling. And I have no idea." (p.21) The job is a permanently renewable mystery that costs nothing to maintain and can be revealed at any point across five seasons.
4. **A building with vertical secrets.** The infinite storage shaft, the black upper hall of whispering doors, the sealed cube with a person in it (pp.23–25). The map itself is a season structure: each floor is an act.
5. **A predecessor who got out.** Petey (p.12), I'M TAKING ME WITH ME (p.20), "like the other before him" (p.53). The series has a precedent, a method and a warning already planted.
6. **A hierarchy that goes up past the antagonist.** The bald man at the high window (p.8) and the raspy voice in the dark (p.53) mean Cobel can be defeated without ending the show.
7. **An outside world that is complicit or insane.** The police laugh (p.47); the only person who believes in hidden powers is a woman the police call "bonkers" (pp.45–46). The outie has nowhere to take the truth even if he finds it.
8. **An antagonist with a personal stake in the product she won't use.** "Have you had it?" — "No." (p.44) plus a daughter who names her pets (p.41) — a whole private life waiting to be a season.

## Structural devices
- **The disorienting opening — but it is Mark on the table, not Helly.** In this draft the naked, memory-less body waking in the boardroom is **Mark** (p.1), and the voice on the intercom is Milchick. (In the aired episode this is inverted: Helly R. wakes on the table and *Mark's* voice runs the questionnaire — see "differences" below.) The device is identical either way and is the pilot's masterstroke: the audience is severed too. We are born on page 1 with exactly the protagonist's knowledge, which is none, and the show's central experience — not knowing who you are inside a room that knows — is delivered before a single line of exposition.
- **The six-question survey as a published information ration.** "Unknown, Unknown, Delaware, Unknown, Unknown and Unknown" (p.4). One data point in six, and the one data point is nonsense. Helly restates the ration at the midpoint — "Exactly four percent" (p.21) — and the pilot then delivers roughly that: the innie ends the episode knowing he consented and nothing else.
- **Corporate liturgy as tone control.** Two distinct registers. **Milchick's** is HR-speak applied to abduction: "on behalf of all our staff, at all levels of seniority, and of all races and sexual genders" (p.6); "That's- a great opinion, and boldly expressed" (p.6); "this pair of empty black pants, along with a sincere apology… which I assure you is anything but empty" (p.7); "Let's make some rad magic" (p.50). **Cobel's** is aphoristic and religious — two mothers, two theologies, angels, Hell, "It's home" (p.15), "It violates no law, and it's brought solace to many of our workers" (p.43). Between them, everything monstrous in this pilot is said politely.
- **Office geography as a map of ignorance.** The corridor with "sharp twists, preventing Mark from seeing more than a few feet ahead" (p.5); the cubicle maze where he can see the exit sign but "no path through the cubicles" (p.8); glass lobby doors backed by a concrete wall (p.9); a storage closet that "appears to stretch upward forever" (p.23); a black hallway where "distant whispers echo from hidden rooms" (p.24–25). Sightlines *are* the exposition policy. And the one instruction on Helly's map is a direction, not a destination: **UP** (p.23). To learn anything, the protagonist must climb.
- **The threshold, not the elevator, as the transition device.** This draft has no severance elevator: elevators appear twice as ordinary staging (pp.9, 11). The switch is instead carried by (a) **the lobby exit door plus `JUMP TO:`** (p.9) — he runs out naked, walks back in suited, and the outie's afternoon is simply gone; and (b) **the lobby glass doors at the end** (p.53) — he looks at his reflection, exits, "Darkness." The residue technique (sunburn, suit, ice pack, calm) does the work the aired series later assigns to a descending lift.
- **Recorded media as the only channel between the halves.** The alphabetised VHS archive (p.27) is the pilot's Chekhov's cabinet, and the tape is the only object in the story that both Marks touch. The denial inside it — "Would it be alright if I said something? To him?" / "No." (p.51) — is a withhold performed *by a character, on camera, in the past, aimed at the present*.
- **Planted and paid.**
  - *Mister:* survey question four (p.3) → the cat in the road (p.32) → "What was his name?" "Mister." (p.37) → the draft's title.
  - *Delaware:* the one non-Unknown answer (p.3) → Milchick's congratulation, "Really stellar work. Delaware." (p.6).
  - *Angels:* Cobel's Catholic mother and "let's go find some new angels" (pp.10–11) → Kaia: "he was killed by angels" (p.46).
  - *Gold:* gold paper WELCOME letters (p.16) → a shelf of "metallic gold boxes" falling into the void (p.24) → the present wrapped in "gold paper" that killed Kaia's father (p.46).
  - *Focus:* "acute workoholism" in the divorce filing (p.38) → "Decreased focus, they cited" in the firing (p.39) → "I'm offering you focus, and all that comes with it" (p.48). The flaw that destroyed his marriage and his career is sold back to him as the product's chief benefit.
  - *Pickles:* offered and refused (p.36) → "I insist" → "there were drugs in the pickle" (p.39). A three-page fuse burned entirely in comic register.
  - *The video:* promised (p.6), deferred (p.15), priced at 4% (p.21), stolen (p.27), watched (p.51). Five plants, one payoff, spanning the whole script.
  - *"Both halves of you would need to request it"* (p.48) → "I'm never going to leave here" (p.51). The rule is stated by the seller and felt by the product 3 pages later.
- **Structural rhyme as act architecture.** Mark loses consciousness twice — involuntarily on p.39 (drugged) and by contract every day at 5 p.m. (p.52–53). The pilot opens with him waking with no memory and closes with him walking into darkness; the last image is the first image inverted.
- **Comedy laid over horror in the same page.** Milchick confessing his professional inadequacy to Helly (p.24) is intercut with Mark hanging over a bottomless shaft. Officers Green and Irons doing a "bunch of crap" bit (p.47) sits between a house that vanished and a man's surrender. The draft never lets a horror beat play without a joke adjacent to it.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework
- **Printed acts on what became a streaming show.** The brief's primary lens anticipates a continuous run; this draft is written to **network/basic-cable architecture** — prologue + four numbered acts + tag, each act starting on its own page with an explicit `End of Act` button. On Apple TV+ there are no commercial breaks to serve. The acts here are not ad slots; they are **information gates**, and the draft's five buttons are all information events (a door opens; a sermon; a tape goes in; a man passes out; a man is told he has no choice). Useful lesson: act architecture survives the disappearance of the commercial break because it was never really about the commercial break.
- **A premise pilot delivered backwards.** Everything a conventional pilot puts in Act 1 — who the hero is, what he lost, what he agreed to, how the gimmick works — is here in Acts 3–4, at **75–94%**. The first half is consequence; the second half is cause.
- **The protagonist changes at the act break.** p.28 introduces a man in a nursery being woken by a foot, and the audience must recognise him as the same person it has followed for 27 pages while accepting that he is not. Few pilots dare a POV handover at 53%.
- **Act 2 is 30% of the script.** Sixteen pages is a structural bulge; it is really two acts fused (induction pp.12–22, break-in pp.23–27) with no button between them. A production draft would almost certainly break it at p.22–23 (Helly slides the map).
- **Three of five act-outs are stillness.** A door unlatching (p.4), a room going quiet (p.27), two people standing patiently (p.52). This is anti-convention for hour drama, where act-outs are jeopardy or reversal. It works because the pilot's currency is information, so *withheld* information is a cliffhanger.
- **No teaser violence, no crime, no body.** The inciting act of aggression against the hero is a customer-satisfaction survey.
- **The tag belongs to the antagonist.** The protagonist is present but silent, walking out of a lobby; the dialogue belongs to Cobel and a voice with no body. Ending a pilot on the villain's line about the hero's future is closer to a serial-thriller convention than an hour-drama one, and it is why the final page reads as a series pitch.
- **Two of the pilot's biggest images are never picked up.** The woman in the sealed cube (p.25) and Kaia's angels (p.46) are the sort of debts a pilot usually cannot afford. They are affordable here only because the episode has *already* paid off a comparably weird plant (Mister) inside the same hour — the audience has been trained to believe that this writer settles up.

### Where this draft differs from the aired episode
This is the pre-production spec titled **"Mister"**. The aired 2022 pilot, **"Good News About Hell"**, reworks it heavily. The differences are structurally instructive:

- **The cold open swaps protagonists.** Here, **Mark** wakes naked on the table and Milchick runs the six-question survey by intercom. In the aired episode, **Helly** wakes on the table and **Mark's** voice runs the questionnaire — which converts the opening from "our hero is born" into "our hero is already the institution's instrument," and gives Mark an innie arc (complicity → doubt) that this draft's innie cannot have because he is brand new.
- **Consequently, the innie hierarchy inverts.** In this draft Mark is the rook and Helly (19, three years in) is the senior employee who slips him the map; in the aired version Helly is the new hire and Mark is the department head promoted into Petey's chair. The rebel and the incumbent trade places.
- **The outie's wound is different.** Here: divorce from **Eleanor**, a firing by text, and a night on the **Skagit bridge** (pp.38–41). In the aired episode Mark is a **widower**, and grief for his dead wife is the engine of the whole series. The draft's wound is failure; the aired wound is loss.
- **The company is unnamed.** The words **"Lumon"** and **"Kier"** appear nowhere in these 53 pages. There is no founder, no handbook, no corporate scripture — only "an organization that's in the business of specificity" (p.40) and "the most revolutionary company on the planet" (p.42). The aired show's entire iconography is a later invention.
- **The department is different.** "Data culling" / the Culling Office (p.6, p.15) rather than Macrodata Refinement; the work is to "turn the green symbols blue" (p.17) rather than to sort numbers by feeling.
- **The mechanism is external.** Severance here runs on a **beacon** broadcasting a "hyper-sonar frequency" to an implanted chip (p.42) — an emitter that can be switched on and off from outside, demonstrated by torturing a rat. The aired version's chip is self-contained and location-triggered, and the rat is gone.
- **Cobel is the recruiter, not the neighbour.** She personally stalks, drugs, kidnaps and pitches Mark (pp.35–49). Her aired counterpart works the same manipulation covertly, as Mrs. Selvig. Milchick is "**Frank**" here (p.5), not Seth, and is introduced as a nervous, newly hired PR director (p.49) rather than a polished floor manager.
- **Irving is a different person.** "IRVING BAILIFF, 65… heavy and bald, but his demeanor is childlike" (p.12) — he sits under his desk, keeps a diary, and is protected by Helly. The aired Irving is rigid, devout and company-loyal. Dylan is "**Hogan**" here (p.15), "vaguely diseased."
- **Petey never appears.** He is referenced three times (pp.12, 13, 18) and implied on the last page ("like the other before him," p.53), but the outie plot does not contain him. The aired pilot's outside-world engine — Petey ambushing Mark, reintegrated — does not exist in this draft, whose outside-world engine is instead the recruitment flashback.
- **No elevator switch.** As above: the innie/outie boundary is a doorway and a `JUMP TO:` (p.9), not a lift.
- **The title.** The aired episode's title is lifted from Cobel's atheist-mother speech on **p.43** of this draft — a line already fully written here, under a different title.

## Lessons for the knowledge base
1. **A mystery pilot should answer mechanism and withhold meaning.** Severance answers *how* (chip and beacon, p.42), *who signed* (p.50), *why he can type* (p.19) and *why the exit loops* (pp.9–10) — cheaply, and mostly in the last third. It withholds *what the work is* (p.21), *who is in the box* (p.25), *what happened to Petey* (p.53) and *what one half wanted to say to the other* (p.51). Mechanism answered buys you the right to withhold meaning; the reverse arrangement exhausts an audience by episode four.
2. **Make the withholding a character's policy, not the writer's.** Helly states the ration ("Exactly four percent," p.21) and its rationale ("you're more likely to accept it once they've broken you some," p.21–22); Cobel refuses on camera ("No," p.51) and in person ("You'll never know," p.52). When every unanswered question has someone standing next to it refusing to answer, frustration is redirected from the writer to the antagonist — where it becomes appetite.
3. **Answer by changing point of view, not by explaining.** The pilot's entire answer block (pp.28–52, 47% of the script) is a *story*, not an exposition scene: a man kills a cat, apologises, and is destroyed for it. Structural rule: when your first half has raised nineteen questions, don't write a scene where someone explains — write the other half of the same life and let the answers fall out of it.
4. **Design an answer that is also a bigger question.** The rat on p.42 answers "how does severance work" and asks "then who else is being burned while unable to remember it?" in the same gesture. Every answer in a long-form mystery should be shaped so that granting it enlarges the field rather than closing it.
5. **In a split-protagonist story, render transitions as elisions and let residue carry them.** p.9's `JUMP TO:` deletes the outie's afternoon and proves it happened with sunburnt hands, a black suit, a calmer pulse and a guard holding an ice pack (p.10). You do not need a device to depict an impossible transition; you need a threshold, a cut, and three pieces of physical evidence on the other side.
6. **Make the second self the literal form of the first self's flaw.** The outie's defining behaviour is deferring feeling (p.32 driving away; p.38 "how many things in your life are you putting off feeling horrible about?"). Severance is that behaviour sold as a service. When the gimmick and the wound are the same thing, the sci-fi premise stops being a premise and becomes a character study — and the theme never has to be stated twice.
7. **Give the antagonist one unsevered privilege and say it in four words.** "Have you had it?" — "No." (p.44). One question, one syllable of answer, and the show has a class system, a hypocrisy, and a target. Cheaper and more durable than any speech about corporate power.
8. **Plant absurd details in throwaway registers; pay them as plot.** "Who killed Mister and how did he die?" is question four of a nonsense survey on p.3 and turns out to be the spine of an entire act at pp.32–37. Nonsense is the safest hiding place for a plant, because the audience files it as tone rather than information — and remembers it anyway.
9. **Act-outs in an information-driven show should be information events, not jeopardy.** A door unlatching (p.4), a tape sliding in and a room going quiet (p.27), two jailers waiting patiently (p.52). If the audience's forward motion is curiosity rather than fear, then a withheld fact is a stronger button than a gun.
10. **End the pilot on the antagonist's contingency plan.** The last page (p.53) installs a power above the antagonist ("Has he come to us defective?"), links three dangling threads ("like the other before him"), and closes on what happens if the hero refuses: "Then he'll have nothing left but the dark." A final line that describes the *shape of the hero's future resistance* is a series pitch disguised as a scene.
