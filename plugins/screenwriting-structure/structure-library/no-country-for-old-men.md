# No Country for Old Men (2007) — Structural Beat Map
**Source:** /mnt/user-data/uploads/Screenplay planning/Ressources/Script Library/Feature Misc/No_Country _(Shooting).pdf · **Writers:** "Adapted Screenplay by Joel Coen & Ethan Coen — Based on the Novel by Cormac McCarthy" (title header as printed) · **Format:** feature · **Genre:** neo-western crime thriller / miniplot tragedy · **Length:** 117 printed pp. (script runs 1–117, "THE END" on p.117). **The library PDF is an excerpt only: 8 PDF pages = a cover header + printed pp. 111–117** (PDF p.2 = printed p.111; offset +109). No draft date is printed on the excerpt.
**Framework lens(es):** PRIMARY — McKee's archplot/miniplot distinction and the deliberate withholding of the obligatory scene (the *scène à faire*). SECONDARY 1 — protagonist displacement across three centres of gravity (Moss the pursued, Chigurh the pursuer, Bell the witness). SECONDARY 2 — the off-screen death of the apparent protagonist and what it does to the remaining 15 pages.

**Page numbering & sourcing (read this before citing):** all citations are the script's **printed page numbers**, not PDF pages. Pages **111–117 are verified verbatim** from the library PDF. Pages **1–82 are verified** against two independent copies of the same shooting draft (Script Slug PDF and IMSDb text), which agree to within ±1 page; where they differ I give the range. Pages **83–110 could not be sourced page-numbered** — that band is marked **`~n est.`** and was interpolated between two hard anchors (p.82 = Chigurh/Wells hotel-room conversation; p.111 = Carla Jean's kitchen), using a verified scene order. Treat `est.` pages as ±2. Percentages are of 117 printed pages.

## Logline
A West Texas welder stumbles on the aftermath of a drug deal and walks off with two million dollars; a killer with a captive-bolt pistol and an unbreakable private code comes after him; and an ageing sheriff follows both men across his county, always arriving a few hours too late — until the chase ends without the confrontation anyone was promised, and the sheriff is left alone with a dream about his father.

## Structure overview
For roughly **85% of its length this is a textbook archplot**: a single active protagonist with a concrete external goal (keep the money, keep his wife alive), a relentless antagonist, causally chained escalation, a hard midpoint reversal, and a ticking device (the transponder) that guarantees convergence. Then, at **~p.102 (87%)**, the script kills its protagonist off-screen, in a scene we arrive at *after* it has happened, at the hands of characters who are not the antagonist — and the remaining fifteen pages convert the film into **miniplot**: passive protagonist, internal conflict, unresolved external question, open ending. The genius of the design is that the miniplot ending is not a late idea; **Bell's voice owns the first three pages of the script before he owns a single scene** (pp.1–3), so the ending is a return, not a swerve.

The other governing fact: **Moss and Chigurh never meet face to face in 117 pages.** They shoot at each other through a door and across a street (pp.59–65) and they speak once by telephone (~p.85 est.), and that is all. The obligatory scene is not merely withheld at the climax; it is withheld structurally, from the first page to the last.

**Screen-presence totals (approx.):** Moss ~58–62 pp. (~52%), Chigurh ~44–48 pp. (~39%), Bell ~38–42 pp. (~34%) including his 3 pp. of opening VO. **Bell has the fewest pages of the three and the most authority.** He also has both ends of the script — the only character who does.

**The structural replacement page: ~p.102 (87%).** Moss's death is the switch. Note the design detail: the script does not cut from Moss alive to Moss dead; it cuts to **Bell's car**, so the audience is inside Bell's approach when the protagonist dies. The displacement is not announced, it is *staged* — we simply find ourselves in the wrong car.

## The withheld climax
**What conventional structure demands at pp.95–105 (81–90%).** By every mainstream model, this script has earned and promised a specific scene. Field's second plot point and Act Three: the hunted man, healed, re-armed and now proactive (he chooses the El Paso motel, sets the rendezvous), faces the hunter. Snyder: "finale" — the hero storms the castle with everything he has learned. McKee, most precisely: the **obligatory scene** — the confrontation between the forces of antagonism and the protagonist that the story has explicitly promised. The promise is not implied here, it is **spoken aloud on the telephone at ~p.85 est.**, when Chigurh tells Moss to bring him the money and Moss tells him he's bringing something else. The script also supplies the classic pre-climax equipment: a rendezvous point (~p.91 est.), a hostage clock (Carla Jean), a shotgun, and a wounded antagonist who can now be hurt (pp.68–69).

**What the script delivers instead.**
1. **Moss dies at ~p.102 (87%), off-screen, killed by the wrong antagonist.** The cartel gunmen — characters with no names and no arc — do it in the gap between two shots. Bell hears gunfire from his cruiser, a pickup pulls out, and by the time we walk into the courtyard the woman from the pool is dead in the water and Moss is dead in the doorway. We arrive *after*.
2. **Chigurh is not present at the death of his own quarry.** The antagonist is denied his own climax as thoroughly as the protagonist is. Chigurh does not appear on the page between ~p.99 est. and **p.111** — a gap of roughly twelve pages across the exact stretch where a thriller places its final battle.
3. **The one remaining chance at a confrontation is staged and then evacuated (~pp.107–108 est.).** Bell goes back to the motel at night, sees the punched-out lock, draws his weapon, steps over the blood and searches the room. Nothing happens. This draft appears to place Chigurh in the dark on the other side of the door, which makes it a *deliberately unconsummated* confrontation rather than an ambiguous one: the two men are separated by an inch of wood and the script simply declines.
4. **The money is resolved by a dime.** The A-storyline's payoff is an unscrewed vent and a coin on the carpet (~pp.108–109 est.), discovered by a man who is not looking for money and cannot do anything with the information.
5. **Chigurh's last scene is an accident (p.113).** He is T-boned at an intersection on a green light — struck by the one force in the film he has always claimed to serve and never had to answer to. He is not caught, not shot, not confronted. He buys a boy's shirt for a sling (p.114), pays for silence, and limps out of the film. His storyline's final words are two teenagers arguing over who owns the money.
6. **The last violence in the script is done to the one person with no stake in the money (pp.111–112)** — and even that is elided, replaced by Chigurh checking the soles of his boots on the porch (p.112).

**Page of Moss's death:** ~p.102 est. (87%). **Page of the last Chigurh scene:** p.113–114 (97%) — the intersection and the boys. **Page of the last violence:** p.112 (96%), conveyed by footwear.

**How Bell's monologue functions as a substitute climax (pp.115–117, 98–100%).** The script replaces external resolution with an *internal* one, and it does so with four deliberate moves. (a) **It relocates the ending to the safest room in the film** — a kitchen, morning coffee, a wife who says "I'm not retired." Every other significant room in this script has a body in it. (b) **It changes the mode from event to report.** Bell does not do anything; he describes something that never happened. After 110 pages in which every important action was witnessed at one remove — the aftermath, the boots, the milk still sweating — the final beat is the purest form of that: an event that exists only as narration. (c) **It answers the opening.** The VO of pp.1–3 asked a question Bell could not process ("I don't know what to make of that"); the dream provides an image instead of an answer — the father riding ahead into the dark and the cold to make a fire, and the certainty that "whenever I got there he would be there." The film's emotional resolution is *inheritance and death*, not victory. (d) **It is the only place in the script where Bell is ahead of someone rather than behind.** He has trailed Chigurh and Moss by hours all film; in the dream he is behind his father — but for once the man ahead of him is going somewhere good, and waiting. That inversion is the substitute for the confrontation.

The substitution works structurally because the film has spent 117 pages training the audience to accept aftermath in place of event. **The ending is not a betrayal of the film's grammar; it is that grammar applied to the climax itself.**

## Storylines
- **A — The money (the engine).** Found pp.9–11, taken home p.11, betrayed by a transponder pp.26–27 → 48–52 → discovered pp.57–59, hidden in a duct p.39 and again in El Paso, and finally resolved as an open vent and a dime on the floor at ~pp.108–109 est. **The A-storyline is the only one that runs the full length and it is the only one that gets no scene of resolution at all** — no recovery, no dispersal, no reckoning. It simply stops being anybody's.
- **B — Chigurh's code and the coin (the theme).** Stated by demonstration (pp.3–5), formalised in the gas-station toss (pp.18–23), interrogated by its victim ("of what use was the rule?", pp.81–82), applied to a promise made to a dead man (pp.111–112), and finally rebutted by an intersection (p.113). The arc is complete and self-contained; **it converges with the A-storyline (Chigurh gets the money) but the script refuses to dramatise that convergence**, giving it to us as a dime.
- **C — Bell's retirement and moral exhaustion (the frame).** Opens the script in VO (pp.1–3) with no scene attached; enters as a body at p.28; accumulates evidence he cannot metabolise (pp.29–31, 43–44, 70–71, ~103–106 est.); makes the one promise he cannot keep (pp.77–78); arrives too late at the decisive event (~p.102 est.); goes back into the room anyway (~p.107 est.); is told by Ellis that you cannot stop what's coming (~pp.109–110 est.); and closes the script alone (pp.115–117). **This storyline never intersects the A-storyline in a way that changes it.** Bell affects nothing. That is the point and it is also the most-argued weakness.
- **D — Carla Jean (the cost).** Introduced as domestic texture (pp.10–13), sent away (pp.23–24), used as leverage in Wells's threat (p.75) and in Chigurh's phone promise (~p.85 est.), and finally the unwitting cause of the convergence when her mother gives away their destination (~pp.94–96 est.). She is the only character who meets Chigurh and answers him on his own terms — telling him she knew what he was the moment she saw him sitting there (p.111) — and the only one who is killed for a promise rather than for money.
- **Where they converge, and where they pointedly fail to.** All four storylines converge geographically on one El Paso motel at ~pp.99–102 est., and the script **empties the room before the audience gets there**. Bell and Chigurh converge on the same motel room at ~pp.107–108 est. and are separated by a door. Moss and Chigurh converge three times (Del Rio p.42, the first motel pp.48–52, Hotel Eagle pp.59–65) and never once occupy the same space with the same knowledge — their only completed contact in the entire script is a phone call at ~p.85 est. Bell and Chigurh **never share a line of dialogue in 117 pages.**

## Structural devices
- **The coin toss as recurring test — with a caveat specific to this draft.** In the released film the coin returns at the end. **In this shooting draft it appears once (pp.18–23) and never again** (pp.111–112 verified verbatim). What recurs on the page is not the coin but *its logic*: Chigurh's insistence that outcomes were "foreseen," that things "fall into place" (p.111), and then the intersection at p.113, which is the coin tossed by the world with Chigurh on the wrong side of it. The device is thematic recurrence rather than literal repetition — and the film's later addition of a second toss shows how a production can convert a theme into a motif.
- **The transponder (the pursuit machine).** From p.26 to p.59 the plot does not need Chigurh to be clever, only to be moving: the receiver supplies causality, geography and suspense (the beeping through town at p.48, the punched lock at p.51). Its destruction at the midpoint (pp.57–59) is what forces the second half of the film to run on **coincidence and family instead of mechanism** — which is precisely the thematic argument the ending makes. The gadget is doing structural work: it is the difference between a plot Moss can beat and a world he cannot.
- **Sound and silence in the action writing.** The script notates hearing rather than seeing: a sprinkler that "chugs in the yard" (p.111); "we can just hear the boys, small" as Chigurh limps away (p.114); the horses' hooves "drumming and scattering divots of earth and snow" (p.116). Violence is frequently introduced by sound off-screen (Bell hears the gunfire, ~p.101 est.). The absence of music in the finished film is already legible in the way these pages are written — the action lines are a sound mix.
- **Elided violence as a system.** Wells (~p.83 est.): cut away at the trigger, the phone rings. Moss (~p.102 est.): we arrive with Bell, after. Carla Jean (p.112): the door opens and a man checks his boots. Even Chigurh's own catastrophe (p.113) is written as "he just starts to turn his head to the right. A huge crash." **The script's rule is that the moment of impact is the one thing you never get** — and the ending simply extends that rule from individual deaths to the story's climax. This is the mechanism that makes the withheld climax feel authored rather than dropped.
- **The boots.** Chigurh checks his soles for blood twice in the film's design and here at the decisive moment (p.112). It is the most economical possible confirmation of an off-screen death: two lines of action, no body, no reaction shot.
- **The dream monologue as ending.** A four-beat structure: domestic small talk (p.115) → the admission that he had dreams → the first dream dismissed in two lines (money given by the father, and lost) → the second dream given nearly a full page of unbroken speech (pp.116–117). The first dream is discarded on purpose; its function is to make the second one land as the real one. **In this draft the second dream is then visualised**, cutting to the snowy pass and the rider with fire in a horn, explicitly called "the image that the movie began with."

## Deviations & tensions with the framework
**The case that the ending is a structural failure — stated fairly.**
1. **The protagonist is not the agent of his own climax, or even present at it.** Moss makes a plan (the El Paso rendezvous, ~p.91 est.), and the script kills him before he can execute it, using characters we have barely met. In classical terms the protagonist's final action is *rendered irrelevant*, which is the textbook definition of an unearned ending.
2. **The antagonist is neither defeated nor caught, and is absent for the twelve pages where the climax should be.** A pursuit thriller that removes both pursuer and pursued from its own final act has arguably abandoned its premise rather than transcended it.
3. **The character who inherits the film has not been dramatically active.** Bell investigates nothing to a conclusion, saves nobody, and by his own account quits. A protagonist who cannot fail (because he never truly tries) cannot produce catharsis.
4. **Fifteen pages of falling action with no reversal.** From ~p.102 to p.117 the script contains one violent event (Carla Jean) and one accident, and otherwise consists of conversations in kitchens and coffee shops. Under Snyder or Field this reads as a denouement four times too long.
5. **The promise made at ~p.85 est. is never redeemed.** McKee's own standard: if you promise the obligatory scene, you owe it. Refusing to pay a debt you deliberately incurred can look like a thesis, and can also look like an evasion.
6. **The A-storyline resolves off-screen and off-page.** No one ever accounts for two million dollars. For many readers that is not ambiguity, it is an unfinished plot.

**Verdict.** The ending is not a failure, but the reasons it works are narrower and more technical than its admirers usually admit — and this draft is *weaker* at it than the finished film.

It works for three structural reasons. **First, the contract was honoured for 85% of the pages.** The subversion only signifies because the script first builds an immaculate archplot: escalating pursuit, hard midpoint, working ticking device, competent hero. A withheld climax in a film that never built one is just a film without a climax. **Second, the substitute was planted on page one.** Bell's VO opens the script before any character exists; the ending is a return to the film's actual frame, not a swerve away from a broken one. Had the script opened on Moss, the last fifteen pages would be indefensible. **Third, the grammar of elision is established from ~p.83 est. onward and applied consistently** — Wells, Moss, Carla Jean — so that the missing climax is recognisably the same move the film has been making all along, at full scale. The withholding is *rhymed*, and rhyme is what separates a choice from an omission.

Where it is genuinely vulnerable: the film asks the audience to accept a thematic answer to a dramatic question, and there is no way to make that trade painless. The pages between ~p.103 and ~p.110 est. are the thinnest in the script, and the Ellis scene has to carry an enormous amount of argumentative weight in a very short space.

**Draft-vs-release differences — the most valuable findings here.**
- **The coin toss with Carla Jean does not exist in this draft.** Verified verbatim on pp.111–112: Chigurh explains that he gave his word to her husband, that Moss used her to save himself, and that "it was foreseen"; Carla Jean tells him she knew what he was the moment she saw him. There is no coin, no "call it," and no refusal. The film's most-quoted exchange — the victim rejecting the coin's authority and putting the choice back on the man holding it — **was added after this draft.** Structurally this is enormous: the release version gives the B-storyline (the code) a genuine climax, in which Chigurh's system is refused to his face, immediately before the world refuses it too at the intersection. This draft has only the second half of that pairing. The film's ending is *tighter* than the page's.
- **This draft visualises Bell's second dream; the film does not.** Pages 116–117 cut to EXT. SNOWY MOUNTAIN PASS – NIGHT and show the rider passing with fire in a horn, described as "the image that the movie began with," fading with the horn last, then "THE END." The finished film stays on Bell and cuts to black mid-thought. **The Coens' final act of subtraction happened after the shooting script** — they removed the last image the script provided, which means the released film withholds one level more than the document that produced it.
- **Both ends of the frame were altered.** The draft opens on mountains and falling snow and closes on the same image; the film opens on West Texas landscapes at dawn and closes on a face. The script's bookend was symmetrical and literal; the film's is asymmetrical and verbal.
- Minor: Wells's death is written to cut away at the trigger (~p.83 est.), which the film preserves; the accountant survives in both.

## Lessons for the knowledge base
1. **You can only withhold a climax you have actually built.** Pages 1–101 of this script are a conventional pursuit thriller executed at the highest level — engine (the transponder, pp.26–59), hard midpoint (pp.57–59), escalating set-pieces, competent proactive hero. The refusal at ~p.102 reads as authorship rather than incompetence *because of* that groundwork. Subversion is a payment drawn on a credit balance you have to build first.
2. **Plant the substitute protagonist before the audience knows there is one.** Bell narrates pp.1–3 with no scene attached and does not appear as a character until p.28 (24%). That three-page investment is the entire licence for the last fifteen pages. If the film's final POV has not spoken by the end of Act One, it cannot inherit the ending.
3. **Establish the grammar of your ending as a local device long before you use it globally.** Elided violence is trained into the audience across three deaths (Wells ~p.83 est.; Moss ~p.102 est.; Carla Jean p.112, conveyed by a man checking his boots) before it is applied to the story's climax. A structural choice that appears once looks like a gap; the same choice rhymed three times reads as a thesis.
4. **Trigger the plot with a virtue, not a vice.** Moss's ruin begins at 1:06 a.m. on p.12 when he goes back with water for a dying man — not when he takes the money on p.11. Placing the fatal error in a decent impulse makes the tragedy structural rather than moralistic, and it forecloses the audience's escape route ("he deserved it").
5. **A gadget can carry causality — and destroying it can carry theme.** The transponder supplies the middle act's entire mechanism (pp.26–59). Moss defeats it at the midpoint, and the second half is forced to run on coincidence and family instead (the mother at the bus station, ~pp.94–96 est.). Breaking your own plot device at 50% is a legitimate way to change what kind of story you are telling.
6. **Displace the POV at the moment of loss, not after it.** The script does not cut from Moss alive to Moss dead; it cuts to Bell's car, so the audience is already inside the successor's approach when the protagonist dies (~pp.101–102 est.). Handoffs land when the new POV is the vehicle for the loss rather than a reaction to it.
7. **Rhyme two hunters through one room to establish "always too late."** Chigurh searches Moss's trailer and drinks the milk (p.33); Bell enters the same trailer and finds the bottle still sweating (p.36). One image, three pages apart, permanently defines Bell's relationship to the plot — and pre-authorises his arrival after Moss's death 66 pages later.
8. **Give the antagonist a scene with no plot function to state the rules.** The gas-station coin toss (pp.18–23) advances nothing. Five pages, 4% of the script, spent purely on the terms of the universe — which is what makes an intersection at p.113 legible as an answer rather than an accident.
9. **An off-screen death buys you a different final act, so know what you are buying.** Killing Moss at 87% converts the remaining 15 pages from confrontation to inquest: morgue, colleague, empty room, elder, wife, dream. If you elide the protagonist's death, budget for a fifth act of *interpretation* and make sure you have a character equipped to do it.
10. **The last subtraction is usually made after the shooting script.** This draft ends by showing the dream (pp.116–117); the film ends on Bell's face. The draft also lacks the Carla Jean coin toss the film added. Compare drafts against release cuts as a matter of course: the gap between them is where the most transferable structural lessons live — here, one addition that gave the theme a climax, and one deletion that took the image away.
