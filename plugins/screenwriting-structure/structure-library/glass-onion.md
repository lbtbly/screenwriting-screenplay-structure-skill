# Glass Onion: A Knives Out Mystery (2022) — Structural Beat Map
**Source:** /mnt/user-data/uploads/Screenplay planning/Ressources/Script Library/Oscars/Oscars 2023/Glass-Onion-A-Knives-Out-Mystery-Read-The-Screenplay.pdf · **Writer:** Rian Johnson (title page reads "Adapted Screenplay / Written by Rian Johnson") · **Format:** feature · **Genre:** whodunit / satirical mystery-comedy (ensemble) · **Length:** 134 printed pp. (135 PDF pages; 150 numbered scenes)
**Framework lens(es):** PRIMARY — fair-play whodunit architecture with a mid-film rewind (two-halves mystery: the film plays a version of events, then replays the same period with the withheld information restored). SECONDARY 1 — the fair-play clue ledger (every first-half plant, its innocent disguise, its second-half re-read). SECONDARY 2 — ensemble mystery management (seven suspects introduced, differentiated, motivated, kept alive) plus the structural role of the fake-detective-story opening: the puzzle boxes and the murder-mystery weekend that is announced and then abandoned.

Page citations use the script's **printed** page numbers (top-right of each page). Printed page = PDF page − 1 (PDF p.1 is the title page; printed p.1 carries no number). Percentages are of 134 printed pages.

## Logline
When a billionaire tech "disruptor" invites his five oldest friends — each of whom he owns — to a murder-mystery weekend on his private Greek island, the world's greatest detective arrives uninvited; but the real case is the one nobody on the island knows has happened, and the woman standing among them is not who she appears to be.

## Structure overview
The film is a **two-pass mystery with a single hinge**. Pass One (pp.1–72, 54%) plays the weekend as a conventional whodunit with a false protagonist ("Andi") and a false question ("who is trying to kill Miles Bron?"). At **p.73** the script hard-cuts to a mirrored slug line — `A FRONT DOOR, PAINTED WHITE` against p.1's `A FRONT DOOR, PAINTED BLACK` — and Pass Two re-tells the same days from Helen Brand's point of view with the withheld premise restored (Andi is already dead; the woman on the island is her twin; Blanc is not a guest but a hire). There are **no act cards, no chapter titles and no on-page "REWIND" device** — the seam is carried entirely by the door rhyme and by repeated dialogue.

## The rewind
**Exact location: printed p.73 (PDF p.74), scene 52 — 54.5% of the script.** There is no montage, no card and no reversing-footage device on the page: the seam is a single mirrored slug line, `A FRONT DOOR, PAINTED WHITE` (p.73) against the film's first line, `A FRONT DOOR, PAINTED BLACK` (p.1), reached by a `HARD CUT TO:` at the bottom of p.72. **This is the clearest draft-vs-release difference in the document**: the release cut stages an explicit, showy rewind (footage running backwards, sound design) at this point; the script simply cuts, and trusts a rhyming slug line and one repeated line of dialogue ("Blanc! There is someone here for you. With a box.") to tell the audience what has happened. The scripted version is quieter and, on the page, more elegant; the film's version is the concession made for a mass audience in one viewing.

**Shape of the second pass:**
- **New material (pp.73–88, 16 pp., 12%)** — the twin reveal, the email, the Glass Onion bar, the boardroom refusal, the perjury trial, the discovery of the real napkin, and the plan. None of this overlaps Pass One at all. The rewind buys its replay by first spending sixteen pages on pure new information, so the audience arrives at the replay *hungry* rather than impatient.
- **Overlapping replay (pp.88–112, 24 pp., 18%)** — the same three days, from the ferry to the shooting. It compresses **55 pages of Pass One into 24**, i.e. roughly 2.3:1. Only about eleven Pass One scenes are revisited; everything else is dropped.
- **Convergence (p.112, 84%)** — the timelines fuse mid-scene: the lights snap on, Claire says "Oh god", and Blanc repeats "She's not going anywhere. Inside. It's time to finish this" — the identical exchange from p.72. From that line on there is one timeline again.

**What is re-shown vs newly shown.** The replay almost never re-shows a scene from the same seat. It re-shows: the pool (p.35 → p.91, moved to the garden *below* the infinity pool so the inaudible becomes audible); Birdie's confession to Peg (p.35 → p.92, verbatim, with a recorder now in the bag); Miles's disruption sermon (pp.38–39 → p.95, heard over Helen's face); the smokeless-garden alarm (p.42 → p.98, from the other side of the hedge); Duke at the window (pp.42–43 → pp.101–102, with the twig now identified and the dialogue inside now audible); Claire's "Something's off" (p.41 → p.98, now motivated); the drinks and Duke's rant (pp.55–57 → p.105, over Blanc's whispered instruction "Pick a fight and lose"); the party and Duke's death (pp.60–61 → pp.106–107, glimpsed in one-line intercuts); the blackout (p.66 → p.108); the hallway (pp.68–69 → pp.109–110); and the shooting (p.70 → p.111). Newly shown inside those same minutes: four villa searches, the news breaking on Helen's phone, Whiskey's spear gun, the journal that stops the bullet, and the hot sauce.

**Craft rules that make the replay dramatic rather than repetitive.** The script states two of them in its own stage directions, which is the best evidence of deliberate design:
1. **No cheating on the image.** At p.117 the true version of the glass handoff is specified as "*the actual piece of footage that originally happened, the same angle, the same take, everything*" — the first pass simply had Birdie's spinning dress in the foreground. At p.118 the Duke/Miles overlap is "*The mix is a TINY bit different this time and we clearly hear Duke complete his sentence*" — the line was always spoken, only buried. Nothing is retroactively added; attention is redirected.
2. **Every replayed scene must answer a question the audience is currently holding.** The replay is not chronological re-telling; it is a targeted response to eight open questions (why did Andi act unhinged? what were Lionel and Claire saying? who snapped the twig? why did Andi provoke Duke and fold? where was she during the blackout?).
3. **The second pass has its own goal, clock and antagonist.** Helen's search for the red envelope is a heist with a deadline (dinner), a rival (the killer), and a countdown (the news of Andi's death breaking). The rerun is therefore *suspense*, not recap.
4. **Tonal re-grading.** Pass One's most emotional beat (Andi's accusation, pp.39–40) becomes Pass Two's biggest laugh (a schoolteacher drunk on 9% kombucha, pp.93–95). The same footage delivers a different genre, which is the strongest possible argument that a second pass is not a repeat.
5. **Point of view, not information, is the withheld thing.** Pass One is honest; it is merely *incomplete*, and it is incomplete because Blanc — the film's usual POV — is himself lying to the audience by omission. Withholding is licensed because the detective is a conspirator, not because the camera lied.
6. **The seam is a formal rhyme, so it costs no exposition.** Black door / white door; the same knock; the same off-screen line. The reveal is delivered in eight lines of dialogue and one flashback to a morgue.

## Storylines
- **A — Helen/Andi: the revenge plot.** Enters as an unnamed woman with a hammer (p.16), arrives as a ghost (p.23), is named at p.74, acquires a goal at p.80 (get the envelope, get the son of a bitch), is "killed" at p.70 and resurrected at p.111, loses everything at p.126, and takes the only justice available at pp.129–133. It is the only storyline with a complete want/need structure: her want is evidence, her need is for someone — anyone — to say out loud what they saw, which she finally gets at p.134 after she has stopped needing it.
- **B — Miles and the Disruptors' complicity.** The five are bound by one shared crime committed before the film starts: perjury at Andi's trial (pp.85–86). Each also carries private leverage — Lionel's signature on a manned rocket (p.92), Claire's power-plant approval (p.91), Birdie's sweat-shop email and $30M (pp.103–104), Duke's cable-news deal and traded girlfriend (pp.101–102), Peg's dependency (p.41). Blanc lays the whole set out at p.53, which is unusual and deliberate: the ensemble's motives are *given away* at 40% so the film can be about proof rather than suspicion. Their arc resolves at p.134, when they testify at last — a beat that only works because their guilt was established as cowardice rather than malice.
- **C — Benoit Blanc: the bored detective and his jurisdiction.** Introduced losing at *Among Us* in the bath (pp.16–18): "My mind is a fueled up racing car and I've nowhere to drive it. I need a great case." He gets a great case and it humiliates him — it is not complex, it is dumb (p.114, p.123), and he can prove nothing (p.128). His arc is the surrender of the classical detective's authority: he says "I am not Batman" at p.77 and means it, then at p.128 hands Helen the Klear crystal, quietly stepping outside the law he has just insisted on. His last on-page action is lighting a cigar while a billionaire's house explodes (p.132): "Disruption."
- **D — Whiskey (the uncorrupted witness).** The only character on the island with nothing to hide and no perjury behind her. She is treated as scenery in Pass One, is the person Helen is decent to (pp.89–90, 106), supplies the fact that convicts Miles (the necklace, p.118), and is the first to break the silence at p.129: "You go girl!" The film's moral scoring is done through her.

**Convergence:** all four meet at pp.112–128, in a single room, in one continuous denouement — and then all four are re-sorted by the destruction sequence, which converts B from suspects into witnesses and C from detective into accomplice.

## Structural devices
- **The puzzle box as thesis object (pp.10–16).** A machine that appears infinitely layered and opens if you cross your eyes at it. The sequence shows three ways of solving it: the disruptors' collaborative over-thinking; Duke's mother solving two puzzles by accident without looking up from the sink ("It's a stereogram!", "It's a compass"); and Helen's hammer. The film's entire epistemology is stated as comedy at 8%, and the ending is the hammer.
- **The Mona Lisa.** Introduced as vanity (p.44), explained as aesthetics — "her smile disappears... layers and depth so complex they give you vertigo" (p.46) — deployed as a metronome (the sound-triggered glass SHHHTICKing shut at every DING through pp.55–70, training the audience to ignore Duke's alerts), used as a silent witness (p.70: "Maybe smiling, maybe not, it's hard to tell"), and destroyed at p.133. Its description is finally transferred to Helen in the script's last line (p.134).
- **The glass onion itself.** Named as a bar (p.28), as a building (p.29), as Blanc's metaphor (p.53), as the murder room (p.113), as the hiding place that hides nothing (p.114 — the envelope behind clear glass in a lucite room), and as the thing that explodes (p.132). It is one image doing act-structure work: densely layered on the outside, obvious at the centre.
- **Klear and the hydrogen house.** A Chekhov's gun the size of a compound. Revealed at p.47 with the line that the whole island already runs on it; diagnosed at p.92 ("the Hindenburg"); handed over at p.128; detonated at p.131. The finale is not a plot twist but a delayed consequence, which is why it plays as inevitability rather than deus ex machina.
- **The dead-woman-walking reveal.** Handled in three moves rather than one: an unnamed woman (p.16), a named ghost everyone reacts to (p.23), a corpse on a slab (p.74). The audience spends 73 pages attached to a character who does not exist, which is what makes her "death" at p.70 land and her survival at p.111 land twice.
- **The announced-then-abandoned murder mystery.** The game is set up in escalating detail (invitation p.15, rules p.49, Gillian Flynn credit p.52) and killed at pp.50–51 by the detective, in public, in ninety seconds. Structurally it does four jobs: it burns off the audience's genre expectations early; it demonstrates Blanc's method on a rigged case so his failure on a real one means something; it gives Miles a grudge that makes him talk (p.53); and it plants the murder plan.
- **The destruction finale as a catharsis substitute for legal resolution.** The script is explicit that the law has failed — "you need evidence. And you've got nothing" (p.127) — so the climax is built out of escalation rather than revelation: one glass, then a vase, then a sculpture, then the room joining in, then the room wanting her to stop (pp.129–131), then arson, then the Mona Lisa. The turn from communal fun to communal alarm is the beat that keeps it dramatic; the group's laughter dying is the actual climax.
- **Ambient clocks.** The hourly Philip Glass chime, the lighthouse sweep during the blackout (which lets the script show single frames of a tableau in pitch dark), and the DING/SHHHTICK pairing. All three are established as texture and later used as machinery.
- **Split-screen prologue.** Scenes 8–11 conduct five suspect introductions simultaneously, which is how a seven-hander gets its cast on the board in fifteen pages without a single "as you know" line.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework
- **The detective already knows.** From p.78 Blanc is not investigating a murder he stumbled into; he has been hired, has a co-conspirator, and is running an operation. Classical fair play assumes the detective's knowledge tracks the reader's. Here Blanc knows more than the audience for 22 pages, which is technically a violation — mitigated because he never *says* anything false to us; the withholding is of point of view, not of fact.
- **The victim is alive; the murder we witness is staged.** Andi dies before p.1 and "Andi" is shot at p.70 in a scene the audience watches, in close-up, with a body. That the corpse gets up (p.111) would be a cheat in a Christie novel; it is legitimised because the shooting genuinely happened, the shooter genuinely intended to kill, and the only fiction is the outcome — and because the fake blood was planted at p.44.
- **The villain is effectively identified early.** Blanc catalogues every other suspect's motive at p.53; by p.81 he has stated the case's only real objection to Miles ("Miles Bron is not an idiot... it would be an exceedingly stupid thing to do"), which is also the film's eventual solution. From roughly 60% the question stops being *who* and becomes *how do you prove anything against a man who owns the machinery of proof*.
- **The corpse the film is about is offscreen and pre-textual.** The murder under investigation (Andi's) is never staged until p.120. The onscreen murder (Duke's) is a *consequence* of the real one. This inverts the genre's usual arrangement, where the body in Act One is the case.
- **Fair play is inverted: the clues are not hidden, they are crowded.** Almost nothing is concealed from the camera. Duke's incriminating line is spoken aloud (p.34) and merely mixed under; Miles hands over the glass on camera (p.60); the malapropisms are in five separate scenes. The script's own stage directions insist on this at pp.117–118. The mystery is one of attention, not access — which is the only version of fair play compatible with a mid-film rewind.
- **The solution is deliberately unsatisfying as a puzzle.** Blanc says so himself: "It's so DUMB!" (p.123). A whodunit normally rewards the audience with ingenuity; this one refuses, because the film's argument is that the emperor has no clothes. It still satisfies because the *satisfaction* has been relocated: the pleasure is the click of ten disparate gags resolving into one indictment, not the elegance of the crime.
- **The detective does not deliver justice.** The denouement is complete at p.125 and worthless at p.128. The genre's contract — deduction restores order — is explicitly broken so that the restoration can be handed to the victim's sister and be physical rather than juridical.
- **Seven suspects, one shared secret.** Rather than seven independent motives for one murder, the script gives seven independent motives to protect *the same man*, plus one shared crime (the perjury). This is why nobody drops off the suspect list: they are all provably liars about the exact matter under investigation, which sustains suspicion without requiring seven murder plans.

## Lessons for the knowledge base
1. **A rewind is legitimate only if the second pass changes the frame, not the frame contents.** The script writes the rule into its stage directions: the true version of the glass handoff is "*the actual piece of footage that originally happened, the same angle, the same take, everything*" (p.117), and the buried line is restored only by a mix change (p.118). Design the first pass so that every clue is physically present and merely out-competed for attention; then the reveal feels like the audience's own failure rather than the author's trick.
2. **Buy the replay before you spend it.** Sixteen pages of *entirely new* material (pp.73–88: the twin, the email, the bar, the trial, the napkin) precede the first repeated scene. By the time the film re-shows the pool, the audience is desperate to see it again. Never open a second pass with repetition.
3. **Compress ruthlessly and give the rerun its own engine.** 55 pages of Pass One become 24 (pp.88–112, a 2.3:1 squeeze), and only ~11 scenes are revisited — each one chosen because it answers a question the audience is actively holding. The replay also carries a heist with a deadline (find the envelope during dinner, pp.104–107), so it moves forward while looking backward.
4. **Split your withheld information into identity and intention, and reveal them at different structural positions.** Identity (Helen, not Andi) lands at the hinge, p.74; intention (Miles, and how) lands at the climax, pp.116–125. One reveal at the midpoint would leave the third act with nothing; two reveals give the film two detonations 45 pages apart.
5. **Mark the seam with a formal rhyme instead of a device.** `A FRONT DOOR, PAINTED BLACK` (p.1) → `A FRONT DOOR, PAINTED WHITE` (p.73), same knock, same off-screen line. This costs one slug line and no exposition; note that the release cut added an explicit rewind on top, which is a useful record of how much hand-holding a general audience needs versus a reader.
6. **Let the detective state the film's structural principle out loud at 40%.** Blanc's glass-onion speech (p.53) tells the audience how to read everything that follows and simultaneously hands the villain his murder plan (paid at p.125). A thesis speech that is also a plot mechanism costs nothing and earns twice.
7. **Manage a large suspect field with one shared crime plus individual leverage.** The perjury (pp.85–86) makes all five demonstrably liars about this exact matter; the private squeezes (pp.41, 91–92, 101–104) differentiate them. This keeps seven people alive as suspects without inventing seven murder plots, and it sets up an ensemble-level payoff (p.134) that no single-suspect design could deliver.
8. **Kill the announced genre exercise early to promise the real one.** The murder-mystery weekend is set up across pp.15–49 and destroyed at pp.50–51. Burning the fake version at 38% clears the audience's expectations, proves the detective's competence on a rigged board, and generates the grievance that makes the villain confide.
9. **If your ending cannot be legal, plant the physical means in Act One and let a character choose it.** Klear is revealed at p.47, diagnosed as an explosive at p.92, handed over in a handshake at p.128, and used at p.131. When the courts fail at p.127, the audience already knows what the house is made of — so the finale reads as consequence, not escape hatch.
10. **Stage catharsis as escalation past comfort.** The smashing sequence (pp.129–131) works because the room joins in, laughs, and then wants her to stop — Lionel's "Whoa - ok", Birdie's "I think that belonged to Liberace". A cathartic climax that everyone approves of is a montage; one the other characters try to halt is a scene.
11. **A deliberately stupid solution can satisfy if the whole film has argued for it.** Pineapple juice (p.123) is a terrible puzzle answer and a perfect thematic one. Establish early (the puzzle box solved by a mother at a sink, p.12) that complexity is a costume, and the anticlimax becomes the point rather than a failure of invention.
