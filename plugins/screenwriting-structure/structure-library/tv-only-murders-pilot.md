# Only Murders in the Building 1x01 "True Crime" (2021) — Structural Beat Map

**Source:** /mnt/user-data/uploads/Screenplay planning/Ressources/Script Library/TV Pilots/Comedy/only-murders-in-the-building-101-true-crime-2021.pdf · **Writers:** Steve Martin & John Hoffman (title page: "Pilot Episode: True Crime / Written By / Steve Martin & John Hoffman") · **Format:** single-camera half-hour, streaming (Hulu; premiered 31 August 2021) · **Genre:** comedy-mystery — a closed-world whodunit played as a three-hander character comedy · **Length:** 37 printed pages (38-page PDF: title page + pp.1–37); title page reads "PRODUCTION BLUE: 11-23-20", while the running header on every page of the body reads "OMITB 101 / PRODUCTION BLUE / 11/3/21" — the title-page date (23 Nov 2020) is the draft; the header date is almost certainly a re-print/watermark artifact
**Framework lens(es):** PRIMARY — comedy-mystery hybrid load-balancing: how a half-hour installs a real murder and a comic trio in the same 37 pages without either deflating the other. SECONDARY 1 — the closed-world whodunit (Christie/Knives Out bounded suspect pool) converted into a *serial* engine. SECONDARY 2 — the true-crime-podcast frame as structure: narration, retro-cast V.O., competing voices, and what the device buys and costs.

Page citations are the script's **printed** page numbers, which appear at the **foot** of each page (p.1 is numbered); **PDF page = printed page + 1**. Percentages are of 37 printed pages. **Printed act breaks exist but only as act-*outs*:** the script prints `END ACT ONE` (foot of p.16) and `END ACT TWO` (foot of p.23) and `END OF EPISODE` (p.37). There is no printed `TEASER`, `ACT ONE`, `ACT TWO`, `ACT THREE` or `TAG` heading, no `FADE IN`, no `FADE OUT`. The teaser is marked only by the card **`TWO MONTHS EARLIER`** on p.2. Story-day tags run `(N1)` for the flash-forward, `(D2)/(N2)` for the murder day and night, `(D3)/(N3)` for the day after, and `(X1)/(X2)/(X3)` for footage and fantasy inserts — so the script's own numbering tells you the cold open is chronologically *last*.

## Logline
Three lonely strangers in a grand Upper West Side apartment building — a washed-up TV detective, a broke theatre director and a young woman with a hidden past — discover that they are all obsessed with the same true-crime podcast on the same night a neighbour they shared an elevator with is found shot in his apartment; when the police rule it a suicide and close the case, the three of them start a podcast of their own to prove otherwise, without knowing that one of them knew the dead man.

## Structure overview

The pilot is a **three-act half-hour with a flash-forward teaser**, and its act lengths are deliberately lopsided: a very long first act, a compressed second, and a third act as long as the first. It covers roughly **26 hours of story time** — one late afternoon and evening (D2/N2), then the following day into night (D3/N3) — with a single scene set two months in the future, played first.

Three structural facts govern everything else.

**The corpse arrives at 43%.** Tim Kono's body is not seen until p.16 — nearly halfway into a half-hour. In a conventional mystery pilot that would be fatally late. The teaser is the insurance policy: p.1 pre-loads a body, a suspect and a promise, which buys fifteen pages of leisurely comedy and character work before the show has to deliver a real one.

**The pilot closes its own case at the end of Act Two.** On p.23, having found Tim's discarded drafts of a suicide note, Charles says "That's our answer. Suicide," and the trio disbands — with 14 pages still to run. The re-opening (pp.24–25) is not triggered by evidence. It is triggered by three separate scenes of unbearable loneliness: a humiliating agent call, an empty box wrapped in children's paper, a shaking hand at a mirror. Mabel's reason for re-opening the case is "I know me — and I can't stop thinking about this," and Charles's is "Neither can I!" This is the hybrid's load-bearing joint: **the C-story (private wounds) is the engine of the A-story (the murder).**

**Almost nothing procedural happens.** There is no timeline, no suspect list, no forensics, no interview. The pilot delivers exactly three pieces of evidence — a garbage bag (p.19), a package containing an engagement ring (pp.31–34), and one glimpse of a figure in a tie-dyed hoodie going *up* the stairs during the evacuation (p.11) — and it defers the mystery machinery to episode two out loud: "So tomorrow we lay out a timeline?" / "I've also got maps, full blueprints of each floor of The Arconia—" (p.35).

## Mode balance

Measured slugline to slugline, to the nearest tenth of a page, with hybrid scenes split by the dominant intent of each beat inside them. Three buckets are needed, not two: the pilot's third mode is neither mystery nor comedy but **undefended loneliness**, and it is almost exactly as large as the mystery.

| Mode | Pages | % of 37 | Discrete blocks | Longest unbroken run |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Mystery** (crime, clue, procedure, threat) | ≈ 11.0 | ≈ 30% | ~14 | **1.5 pp** — pp.15.5–17 (the news of the death → the body) |
| **Comedy** (jokes, bits, character comedy, farce) | ≈ 14.5 | ≈ 39% | ~13 | **3.5 pp** — pp.12–15.5 (the bistro) |
| **Character / pathos** (loneliness, confession, wound) | ≈ 11.5 | ≈ 31% | ~12 | **2.8 pp** — pp.27–30, and even that is cross-cut with Jersey |

Roughly **39 mode changes across 37 pages — about one per page.** No mode holds the floor for more than three and a half pages anywhere in the episode.

**The asymmetry is the design.** Mystery wins on *frequency* and loses on *duration*: it appears in fourteen separate blocks but never sustains more than a page and a half. Comedy is the inverse — fewer blocks, much longer scenes. The result is that the murder feels omnipresent while occupying under a third of the script, and the comedy feels relaxed while never being allowed to become the episode's subject. Total on-body time is about **one page** (pp.16–17); total time with a police officer is about **two** (pp.17–19); the trio's actual investigative *labour* — sorting garbage, picking a lock, opening a box — is about **three**.

**Mystery owns the act edges.** Look at where the mystery blocks fall: p.1 (teaser), p.11 (act-one inciting event), pp.15.5–17 (act-one out and act-two in), pp.22.5–23 (act-two out), pp.30.5–31 (the find), pp.33–34 (the reversal), p.37 (the final image). Every structural hinge in the episode is a mystery beat; every long scene between the hinges is comedy or character. The mystery is the skeleton; the comedy is the body.

**How the comedy is stopped from deflating the mystery.** Five mechanisms, all visible on the page:

1. **The corpse is described without a joke.** p.16 is written in unbroken capitals as pure horror — "SKULL BLOWN TO BITS BY AN AT-POINT FATAL GUNSHOT EXPOSING BRAIN AND MATTER." The scene's comedy is *physical* (hyperventilating, dry-heaving) and belongs to the characters' bodies, not to the writing's attitude toward the death.
2. **The victim gets a want before he dies.** Tim's one page (p.8) is spent chasing something he can't get — "this one is very important to me." When the reveal comes on p.34 that it was an engagement ring, he retroactively becomes a man with a life, not a plot device.
3. **Every joke about the death is punished within the same scene.** Oliver directing Charles's line-readings over a corpse (pp.21–22) is followed *on the next half-page* by Mabel reading Tim's drafted suicide notes aloud (p.22), and the stage direction turns the joke back on all three: they relate to it and none of them will admit it.
4. **The authority figure is played straight and wins her scene.** Detective Williams (pp.17–19) is the only character in the pilot who is never made ridiculous. She calls the trio "true-crime numb-nuts," is factually wrong, and still exits with the moral high ground — which keeps the show's own premise (amateurs podcasting a real death) legitimately in question.
5. **The tonal switch is almost always carried by an object or a sound, not by a line.** DING! (pp.9, 19, 20, 36), THWONK (p.11), the garbage bag, the ring, the omelet, a photograph. Objects can change register without anyone having to change voice, which is why the seams don't show.

**Where the two modes reinforce each other.** Three places, all deliberate:

- **Genre-literacy as characterisation.** Because the leads are true-crime obsessives, exposition that would be inert ("we should check the timeline") is a *joke about who they are*. Mabel's "They'll have cops posted on 9" (p.16) is simultaneously a plot mechanism, a laugh, and her introduction as the smartest person in the show.
- **The comic flaw is the investigative tool.** Charles's vanity supplies the jimmy keys (p.27), Oliver's showmanship supplies the record button (p.21) and the podcast itself, Mabel's guardedness supplies the surveillance habit that produced the Bunny lead (p.25). Every capability is an embarrassment repurposed.
- **The murder is the only thing that makes them less lonely.** The C-story and the A-story want the same outcome, which is why the pilot can spend 31% of its pages on private pain without stalling the mystery — and why closing the case on p.23 reads as a *loss* rather than a resolution.

## The podcast frame

Roughly **7.8 pages — 21% of the pilot — sit inside the podcast frame** in one form or another. The device is used in four distinct registers, and the script is careful to introduce them in the order that teaches the audience most.

| Page | Voice | Register | Function | What it buys |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Charles (V.O.) | Sounds like spontaneous interior narration | Introduces Charles; states the closed-world thesis (400 eyes on the courtyard); establishes true-crime fluency as a personality | Character, theme and premise in one paragraph, with no confidant scene |
| 3 | Charles as "Brazzos" (1992 footage) | Diegetic archive | The catchphrase, cut mid-line across two decades | A 30-year career summarised in six words, and a payoff planted for p.34 |
| 4–5 | Mabel (V.O.) | Same slot, opposite voice — profane, defensive, funny about fear | Introduces Mabel; smuggles in the knitting-needle fantasy | Lets the show depict Mabel committing an imaginary murder without any character in the world learning she is capable of picturing it |
| 5–6 | Oliver (V.O.) | Same slot again — theatrical, quoting *Annie*, self-mythologising | Introduces Oliver; establishes the trampoline/bounce-back image | Licenses two inserts (the dance video) and one visual motif that will resolve the episode |
| 9–11 | Cinda Canning (V.O. and PODCAST V.O.) | The genuine article — NPR gravitas, sponsor read, Episode Six | Models the form the show is about to adopt; unifies three apartments into one scene | Time compression: an intercut listening session doubles as three character portraits, one theme (loneliness), and a tutorial in podcast grammar |
| 10–11 | Cinda's narration, visualised three ways | Subjective fantasy inserts (X3) | Ray Butler in seersucker / Doc Martens / Oliver's coat | The cheapest, funniest characterisation in the script — one line of audio, three interior lives |
| 21 | Oliver (recording on his phone) | The trio's first act of authorship | The podcast becomes a thing they *do*, not a thing they consume | Converts the audience's own listening habit into the protagonists' agency |
| 21–22 | Charles performing, Oliver directing | Meta-performance | "Hit 'killer' a little harder"… "really punch 'somewhere'" | Names the show's ethical problem out loud and makes it a character flaw rather than a blind spot |
| 35 | Charles (V.O.) → Charles at the mic | **The retro-cast** | The p.2 narration is revealed to have been the podcast's scripted opening | The whole cold-open apparatus is re-read at once; the frame is discovered, not announced |
| 35–36 | Oliver, Mabel, Charles | Production meeting | "sounds so PBS-y"; "I have a riff on a piece to Clair de Lune"; "no, thank you" | Reveals that Oliver's p.6 V.O. was also material, and that Mabel's absence from the frame is a *choice she is making* |
| 36 | Oliver at the mic | Title drop | "Only murders in the building…" | The show's title, the podcast's title and the season's containment rule become the same sentence |

**What the device permits.**

- **Three protagonists introduced in five pages with no shared scene and no confusion.** The V.O. slot is identical each time — walking home through New York, essayistic, ending on an image — so the *differences* (fear, vanity, theatre) do all the work. This is a rare structure: three cold introductions, sequential, none of them dramatised.
- **Exposition with no exposition scene.** Nothing in the pilot is explained by one character to another until p.14, and by then the audience already knows everything it needs.
- **Tonal control.** The narration's comic-essay register is what makes p.16's shredded skull survivable. The show has spent fifteen pages establishing that its voice can hold gruesome material lightly, so the moment it *stops* being light, the shift is legible as intent rather than accident.
- **Time compression and parallelism.** Cinda's narration is the only thing that can bind three apartments into a single scene (pp.9–11) — and later, the same trick lets Charles's text message and three separate bounce-backs (pp.34–35) play as one movement.
- **A built-in season architecture.** One podcast episode per television episode; a case that must be *narratable*; a reason for recaps; a reason for the leads to interview neighbours; and, in "Only murders in the building," an explicit rule for what the show will and will not cover.
- **Deniable interiority.** The V.O. lets Mabel narrate a violent fantasy (pp.4–5) in the first ten minutes without any other character witnessing it — information given to the audience alone, which is exactly what the last page needs.

**The risk it carries.** The frame is an invitation to *tell* rather than dramatise, and this pilot takes the invitation on pp.2–7: none of the three narrations advances a plot, and if the flash-forward were removed the episode would open with roughly five pages of charming, non-urgent essay. The frame also protects the leads from their own behaviour — a podcast about a neighbour's death, pitched for money in the man's building the same night, is a genuinely ugly act, and the narration's warmth makes it go down easily. The script's only defence is to stage the ugliness on purpose (pp.21–22) and immediately answer it (p.22). Finally, the retro-cast on p.35 is a one-shot trick: it works because the audience did not know what it was hearing, and it can never be played again.

## The closed world

**Establishing the boundary.** The Arconia is named in the pilot's third paragraph of narration and revealed on a REVERSE (p.2) as "a stunning 14-story pre-war landmark." Of the **58 sluglines** in the script, **42 (72%) are inside or at the Arconia**. Of the remaining sixteen: five are street scenes within blocks of it, five are fantasy or archive inserts, two are a bistro that exists in the episode only because the Arconia expelled its tenants, and **four are the pilot's single genuine excursion — Oliver's son's house in New Jersey (pp.26–33) — whose entire subject is what leaving the building would cost** ("You have to sell your apartm—" / "It's all I've got. It's who I am."). The one trip outside the closed world is a scene about the closed world's value. That is not accidental economy; it is the boundary being priced.

**Architecture as mystery engine.** The building is not a setting; it is a set of mechanisms, each installed with a joke and each usable as plot:

- **The courtyard.** "There's about 200 windows and 400 eyes with a view of that courtyard" (p.2) — offered as an argument for safety, functioning as a definition of the suspect pool. Charles's kitchen overlooks it (p.9); on p.19 he stands at that window and thinks "Is a killer out there?" The pilot converts its own opening reassurance into dread with no new information.
- **The elevator.** The only room in the world where any two residents must share a frame. Used four times: to introduce the trio and the victim (pp.7–9), to assemble the team floor by floor (p.20), to reopen the case and introduce Uma (p.24), and as a scene-transition sound on p.36. It also carries the building's superstition — Charles "hits 14 (because there's no button for unlucky 13)" (p.7). A building with a missing number is a permanent invitation.
- **The lines.** "They're on the A-line. I get mis-delivered packages on my C-line" (p.25). The building's floor plan is used as *deductive reasoning*. Two pages later that same system has produced the episode's only physical evidence, sitting on a neighbour's hall table.
- **The vents.** Mabel hears Bunny through a vent near her bathroom ceiling (p.24) and then uses the same vent as her plan of entry (p.26). The building leaks information in both directions.
- **The chutes.** "Why would he get on an elevator with that?! There's a chute on every floor" (p.20). The clue is not the garbage — it is the *violation of the building's routine*.
- **The freight elevator and the stairwells.** Charles's 28 years of residency is an investigative asset ("I can get us to the freight elevator," p.16); the stairwell is where the killer is glimpsed (p.11) and where the trio flees in the teaser (p.1).
- **The blueprints.** "I've also got maps, full blueprints of each floor of The Arconia—" (p.35). The season's evidence board will be the building's own drawings.

**The suspects, planted.** The pilot populates the pool with a light hand — most of these characters get four lines or fewer, and none is accused of anything:

| Page | Who | The plant |
|---|---|---|
| 7, 11, 15–16 | **Lester**, doorman | Controls the door; is the source of the news of the death; "I don't know — we're finding out" |
| 7 | **Ursula**, mail cart | Controls the packages — and packages are the plot |
| 9 | **The bassoon player** (unseen) | Heard through Charles's courtyard window; the building has audible strangers |
| 9 | **A tabby cat** on Oliver's floor | A wordless throwaway at the edge of frame that becomes evidence in the next episode |
| 2, 24 | **Uma Heller** | Introduced as an anonymous woman flipping Charles off in the street, revealed nine pages later to be a neighbour — a small, precise demonstration that in this world every stranger is a resident |
| 24–25, 31 | **Bunny Fillpot**, head of the Board | Mean, powerful, and physically in possession of the dead man's package |
| 17–19 | **Detective Williams** | Not a suspect but a structural obstacle: the official verdict the season must overturn |
| 36–37 | **Arnav** | Knows about Lucy — i.e. knows more about Charles's life than Charles has told anyone |
| 8, 37 | **Tim Kono** | The victim, retroactively the pilot's biggest question |
| 1, 4–5, 37 | **Mabel** | Placed in the pool by the final image |

**The season engine, stated on the page.** Three lines do it. "There's about 200 windows and 400 eyes" (p.2) supplies the population. "Only murders in the building." / "That… seems limiting." / "**Not yet! We've got one, who knows how many to come**" (p.36) supplies the format and the renewal logic in a single exchange — a rule that is simultaneously a joke, a title, and a promise of serialised bodies. And the fire alarm (p.11) proves the essential property of a *serial* closed world: the box can be opened and closed at will. A locked-room story ends when the room is unlocked; a locked-*building* story can run for years, because people keep coming home.

## Storylines

**A — the murder of Tim Kono.** Planted p.8 (one page, one want, one wrong floor), triggered p.11 (fire alarm and a tie-dyed hoodie going up), discovered p.16, foreclosed p.18 by the police, foreclosed again p.23 by the trio themselves, reopened p.25 on a package theory, evidenced pp.31–34 (the ring), and reframed p.37 (Mabel knew him). Total page count: roughly eleven. What makes it feel bigger is placement — the A-story owns the teaser, both act-outs and the final image.

**B — the trio's formation.** Three separate narrations that never meet (pp.2–7) → forced proximity with no contact (pp.7–9) → the same private ritual in three rooms (pp.9–11) → an accidental meeting caused by the same event that caused the murder (pp.12–15) → a shared shock (pp.16–17) → a shared idea, arrived at separately and converging in an elevator (pp.19–20) → a business (pp.20–21) → dissolution (p.23) → reformation on individual need (p.25) → division of labour, and two of the three exchanging real confidences (pp.27–33) → a working unit with a name (pp.35–36). **The B-story is the only one that runs continuously from page 2 to page 36**, which is the tell: the trio, not the murder, is the pilot's actual subject.

**C — three private wounds.**
- *Charles: inherited cruelty and a vanished domestic life.* pp.3–4 (grief avoidance) → p.9 (rituals) → p.14 ("by choice") → p.19/23 (the binned omelets) → p.23 (the humiliating offer) → pp.28–29 (the father monologue) → p.35 (he eats) → pp.36–37 (Lucy).
- *Oliver: bankruptcy and a son's exhausted love.* pp.5–6 (the persona) → p.7 (the lie about work) → p.9 (the performed life) → pp.14–15 (the grandchildren lie) → p.15 (letting Charles pay) → pp.20–21 (the podcast as rescue) → p.23 (the empty boxes) → pp.31–33 (the refusal) → p.34 (the bounce).
- *Mabel: a concealed history and a dead man she loved.* pp.4–5 (the violence fantasy) → p.10 (the empty apartment) → pp.13–15 (the refusal to be peeled) → p.19 (the unread screensaver) → pp.23–24 (tattoo, tremor, pills) → pp.29–30 (the Hardy Boys, "most were") → p.34 (the ring) → p.37 (the photograph).

**Convergence.** The three storylines fuse twice, in the same way both times. At **p.22**, Tim's drafted suicide notes are read aloud and describe all three protagonists — the A-story delivering the C-story's diagnosis. At **pp.23–25**, the C-stories individually make the A-story restart: three lonely mornings produce "I can't stop thinking about this" / "Neither can I!" The engine of this show is therefore not curiosity but need, and the pilot proves it by switching the case off and letting loneliness switch it back on. The B-story is the medium in which the other two meet: the trio can only investigate together, and can only confess while investigating.

## Structural devices

**1. Three V.O. introductions in an identical slot (pp.2–7).** The same formal container three times — a lead walking home through New York, narrating an essay, ending on an image — with everything else varied. Charles's ends on the Arconia ("safety in numbers"); Mabel's ends on a knitting needle and an at-peace smile; Oliver's ends on a man who always bounces back and a warning that "just when it's all starting to feel the same, that's when you get hit by something you never saw coming." Each closing image is a load-bearing plant: the building is the world, Mabel's fantasy is the teaser's alibi, and the bounce is the finale of the third act. Note also that the device is *retro-cast* on p.35: what appeared to be narration was authorship.

**2. The elevator as compulsory-encounter machine.** In a building of hundreds, the elevator is the only place where the closed world's inhabitants are forced into one frame with nothing to do. The pilot uses it for introductions (pp.7–9), for planting the victim mid-scene (p.8), for a joke that becomes the trio's founding gesture ("DING!", p.9), for the team assembly (p.20), for reopening the case (p.24), and finally as pure punctuation — a DING! used as a scene transition on p.36. Its most quietly effective detail is structural: there is no button for 13.

**3. The fire alarm as an alibi device (p.11).** One event, five functions: it empties the building (creating the murder's window), cuts main power (killing the podcast mid-punchline, so the joke's frustration and the plot's inciting incident are the same beat), scatters residents into a bistro (manufacturing the trio's meeting without contrivance), destroys the timeline's evidentiary integrity (everyone was outside; nobody has an alibi and everyone does), and provides the cover under which Charles passes the only visible suspect on the stairs. It is the most efficient page in the script.

**4. Mabel's concealed history as a distributed plant.** The information withheld — she knew and loved the victim — is paid for across nine separate placements (pp.4–5, 10, 13–15, 19, 23–24, 25, 29–30, 34, 37). The technique is worth isolating: **each plant is disguised as a different genre of scene.** The fantasy is comedy; the empty apartment is production design; the refusal at the bistro is manners; the screensaver is a throwaway; the tattoo is a body shot; the tremor is vulnerability; the Hardy Boys speech is bonding; the ring is romance. Nothing is ever presented as a withholding, so the last page reads as revelation rather than cheat. The strongest single plant is a self-interruption in a story she is telling voluntarily: "we got into solving 'mysteries' around the— complex we all lived in… Well, those were made-up mysteries. **Or, at least, most were…**"

**5. The final-page turn (p.37).** Structurally it does four things at once: it converts a protagonist into a suspect; it re-reads the teaser (now "It's not what you think" is a line we have a reason to disbelieve); it retroactively re-scores the entire episode, since every Mabel scene becomes a performance; and it hands the audience information that the other two leads do not have — the classic dramatic-irony engine for a serial. The delivery is deliberately passive: no confession, no confrontation, just an unattended iPad cycling photographs while she showers, humming "an odd little discordant tune."

**6. The bounce-back motif (p.6 → pp.34–35).** A digression inside a comic V.O. becomes the thematic climax, played on three characters in ninety seconds with no dialogue: Oliver falls off a porch and springs upright; Mabel drops a ring that rebounds and hangs at her eye-line; Charles drops a whole frying pan that returns to his hand. The escalation is from plausible to impossible, and the script tells you what it means in four words — "New life. For all of them."

**7. The omelet as a silent instrument.** Peppers bought (p.2) and shelved (p.9); a perfect omelet binned (p.19); another binned (p.23); eggs finally eaten (p.35); and then the meaning arrives from outside — a neighbour smells it through the wall and says "Reminds me of Lucy. It was her favorite, right?" (pp.36–37). A recurring image is used first as characterisation, then as a progress meter, then as an exposition delivery vehicle, without a word of explanation at any point.

**8. Fictional-case rehearsal.** The Oklahoma podcast (pp.9–15) is a full miniature of the show: a disappearance, a suspect, a clue in a dog's mouth, and three amateurs theorising over dinner. By the time the real body appears on p.16, the audience has already seen this trio investigate — their hierarchy, their vocabulary ("peeling of the onion"), their impatience with pacing, and their competence. The ensemble is pre-tested on a case with no stakes.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework

**The mystery's inciting incident is at 43%.** Standard mystery-pilot construction puts a body in the first ten per cent. This script puts it at page 16 of 37 and covers the gap with a flash-forward. The move is defensible but not free: **remove the teaser and the first fifteen pages have no engine at all** — three charming narrations, an elevator ride, and a listening party. The teaser is doing more structural work than any other page in the episode.

**Act Two is half the length of Acts One and Three.** Roughly 14.5 / 7.5 / 14.5 pages. The act containing the murder, the corpse, the police and the investigation is the shortest in the episode. This is the clearest possible statement of what the show believes it is: the mystery is the container, not the content.

**The pilot solves its own case at 62%.** Ending Act Two on "That's our answer. Suicide" — with the trio disbanding — is a negative act-out that voids the premise the audience bought. It works only because the script has already made the *loss of the friendship* the greater loss ("whatever it was they were feeling about this newfound connection"). Structurally this is a high-wire act: a comedy-mystery that turns its mystery off is betting everything on the audience having transferred its investment to the characters by page 23.

**No procedural apparatus whatsoever.** No timeline, no suspect list, no forensics, no interviews, no board. The pilot's total evidentiary yield is a garbage bag, a package and a hoodie. The blueprints — the thing a mystery series actually needs — are explicitly deferred to "tomorrow" (p.35). For a knowledge base this is worth flagging as a *choice*: the pilot's job was to make you want to spend time with these three, and it spends its pages accordingly.

**The tonal risk is real and is met once, narrowly.** Pages 21–22 put Oliver directing an actor's line reading about a fresh corpse. There is no version of that beat that is safe. The script's mitigation — Mabel pulling the dead man's suicide drafts out of a bag on the very next half-page — works, but it is the only defence in the episode, and it is deployed within sixty seconds of the offence. The general principle the script is following: *you may joke about a death exactly once before you must pay for it.*

**The withheld information about Mabel edges toward unfairness.** She is a point-of-view character with V.O. narration (pp.4–5), and V.O. conventionally implies candour. She narrates her sleeping habits and her fear of men and never mentions the man she is grieving. Three things keep it on the right side of the line: the narration is later revealed to be *performed* (p.35), so its unreliability is retroactively licensed; she is the only lead who refuses to be "peeled" (p.15) and is seen refusing; and the single most incriminating image — the photograph — is put on screen at 51% (p.19) with the camera pointedly not looking at it. Even so, the pilot's contract with the audience is unusual: it asks you to like a narrator who is lying to you by omission for thirty-six pages.

**The pace is leisurely by design and would not survive a network note.** Three separate two-page monologue-adjacent scenes (Charles's father, Oliver's ask, Mabel's Hardy Boys) sit in the third act of a half-hour comedy. What makes them affordable is cross-cutting: the burglary runs under the confessions, so no scene is ever *only* a confession, and the intercut structure means the longest unbroken character run in the episode is under three pages.

### Draft vs. aired episode

This is a production-blue draft and it tracks the broadcast episode closely; the aired premiere plays essentially this structure, these acts and this ending. Observable differences and points to verify against broadcast:

- **Character name.** The draft's **"BUNNY FILLPOT"** (pp.24, 25, 31) is **Bunny Folger** in the series — a rename between this draft and air, worth noting since the character becomes a major season-two figure.
- **Detective's first name.** The draft gives **"DETECTIVE DENA WILLIAMS"** (p.17) with a casting note — "(straight out of THE WIRE)" — that describes the intended type rather than the eventual performer. Broadcast dialogue does not, to my recollection, use the first name.
- **Date inconsistency in the document itself.** The title page reads "PRODUCTION BLUE: 11-23-20"; every page header reads "11/3/21." Treat the title page as the draft date and the header as a re-print artifact — the episode premiered 31 August 2021, so a November 2021 header cannot be a draft date.
- **Details specified here that should be checked against the cut:** the cold open's title card wording ("TWO MONTHS EARLIER"), Jan & Dean's "Manhattan" over the teaser, the tabby cat on Oliver's floor (p.9), the whale tattoo as the shared marking in the final photograph (pp.23, 37), the neighbour "ARNAV" (pp.36–37), the restaurant "Ben & Charlie's Bistro" (p.12), and Daniel Johnston's "Don't Be Scared" over end credits (p.37). Music and small-part names are the two categories most often changed after a production draft.
- **Length.** 37 pages for an episode running roughly 34 minutes — approximately one page per minute, which is unusual for a half-hour and reflects how much of this script is dialogue-light behaviour (three intercut listening sessions, three bounce-backs, an omelet).

## Lessons for the knowledge base

1. **Buy your comedy time with a corpse on page one.** A flash-forward teaser is not a stunt; it is a *budget line*. Only Murders spends its most shocking image (bloodied protagonist over a body, p.1) before any character is named, and that purchase funds fifteen pages of unhurried comedy and loneliness before a real body appears at 43% (p.16). If your pilot's engine starts late, put a promise of the engine first — but make the promise specific enough to be re-read later (the tie-dyed hoodie, "It's not what you think").

2. **Run the mystery at high frequency and short duration; give the comedy the long scenes.** Measured: ~11 pages of mystery in ~14 blocks, none longer than 1.5 pages, occupying every act edge (pp.1, 11, 15.5–17, 22.5–23, 30.5–31, 33–34, 37); ~14.5 pages of comedy in ~13 blocks with runs up to 3.5 pages (the bistro, pp.12–15.5). The result is a murder that feels ever-present on under a third of the pages. **Mystery is the skeleton; comedy is the body.**

3. **Never let a joke be the last thing that happens to a death.** Two rules, both visible: the corpse itself is described with zero jokes (p.16, all caps, pure horror), and the one scene that jokes about the death — Oliver directing Charles's read (pp.21–22) — is answered within the same scene by Mabel reading the victim's drafted suicide notes (p.22), which turn out to describe all three protagonists. Comedy-mystery survives on this exchange rate: joke, then pay.

4. **Give the victim a want before you give him a death.** Tim Kono is on screen for roughly one page (p.8) and spends it chasing something he can't get — "this one is very important to me." When the ring appears on p.34, that one page retroactively converts a plot device into a man. One page of want is cheaper and more effective than a page of eulogy.

5. **In a closed world, architecture *is* exposition — and every mechanism should be installed as a joke.** The courtyard's "400 eyes" (p.2), the missing 13th floor (p.7), the A-line/C-line mail system (p.25), the vent (pp.24, 26), the chute on every floor (p.20), the freight elevator (p.16), the blueprints (p.35). Each arrives as character comedy and each is later load-bearing. Build the machine while the audience thinks it is watching a bit.

6. **Make the one exit from your closed world the most expensive scene in the script.** The pilot leaves the Arconia's block exactly once — four scenes in New Jersey (pp.26–33) — and uses them to establish that losing the apartment would be losing an identity: "It's all I've got. It's who I am." An excursion that prices the boundary is worth more than five scenes that merely respect it.

7. **Close the case at the end of Act Two and reopen it on need.** "That's our answer. Suicide" (p.23, 62%) voids the premise; three wordless scenes of humiliation (pp.23–24) restart it; the restart line is not a clue but a confession — "I know me — and I can't stop thinking about this" / "Neither can I!" (p.25). This is the reusable move for any hybrid: **when the plot pauses, the character engine must be strong enough to restart it, and you should prove that in the pilot.**

8. **Give each amateur exactly one capability, and derive it from the flaw you're already mocking.** Charles's vanity yields the jimmy keys (p.27); Oliver's showmanship yields the record button and the podcast itself (p.21); Mabel's guardedness yields surveillance (pp.16, 25). No one acquires a skill; everyone repurposes an embarrassment. This is what makes an amateur-detective ensemble feel earned rather than convenient.

9. **Rehearse the ensemble on a fake case before handing them a real one.** The Oklahoma podcast (pp.9–15) gives the trio a disappearance, a suspect, a clue and a dinner-table theorising scene with no stakes. By the time the actual body arrives on p.16, their hierarchy, vocabulary and competence are established, so the pilot never has to spend a page explaining how these three would investigate anything. A dummy case is the cheapest possible ensemble test.

10. **Reveal a meta frame; don't announce one.** Charles's page-2 narration plays as interior monologue for thirty-three pages and is then discovered to be a script being read into a microphone (p.35). Announcing the device up front would have cost the pilot its three-voice cold introduction and its tonal cover; revealing it late re-reads the whole episode for free. The corollary is that this is a one-shot trick — spend it on the pilot.

11. **Withheld information must be paid for in plants, and every plant should be disguised as a different kind of scene.** Mabel's secret is placed nine times (pp.4–5, 10, 13–15, 19, 23–24, 25, 29–30, 34, 37) as, in turn: comedy, production design, good manners, a throwaway, a body shot, vulnerability, competence, bonding and romance. The audience sees the evidence and files it under nine different headings. The alternative — silence — is what makes late reveals feel like cheating.

12. **State the series rule out loud, as a joke, in the last three pages.** "Only murders in the building." / "That… seems limiting." / "Not yet! We've got one, who knows how many to come" (p.36). One exchange delivers the title, the containment principle, the objection to it, and the promise of more bodies. A pilot that can compress its own format into a laugh has proved the format is sturdy.
