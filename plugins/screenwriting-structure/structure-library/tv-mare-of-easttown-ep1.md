## Logline
On the twenty-fifth anniversary of the basketball shot that made her a local legend, a Pennsylvania detective spends one Friday failing at everything — a year-old missing-girl case she privately believes is unsolvable, a mother she lives with, a daughter she is driving away, an ex-husband who gets engaged two houses down, and a grandson who is not her son — while, in parallel and entirely outside her knowledge, a seventeen-year-old single mother in the same town is catfished, beaten and, by the following dawn, found shot in the face in the creek.

## Structure overview

The episode is a **closed twenty-four-hour loop**. It opens before dawn on a Friday with Mare's phone waking her, and ends the following morning with Mare's phone waking her again. Everything in between — two cases, four households, five parties, one ceremony, one one-night stand, one assault and one murder — happens inside a single calendar day (Siobhan dates it on p.34: applications due January 15th, "It's the 10th"; Ryan on p.41: "It's Friday"; Lori on p.44: "It's 7:35").

There is no printed act structure and, more radically, **no case structure**. A conventional crime pilot opens with a body, gives the detective the case, and spends the hour on the investigation. This one opens with a *fake* body — an off-screen scream at dawn (p.1) that turns out to be a woman reporting a peeping tom — and then withholds the real one for sixty-three more pages. The architecture that replaces the investigation is **an inventory of a town**: forty-three named characters shown on screen, almost all of them introduced doing something domestic, none of them introduced as a suspect.

The central design decision is that **the episode's turn is emotional, not criminal, and it sits exactly at the midpoint**: at p.30 (~48%) Mare learns from her mother that her ex-husband is engaged. That is the pilot's inciting incident in every functional sense — it is what changes the protagonist's state, and every scene after it is downstream of it (the fight with Siobhan, the party seen through a window, the drinking, the pick-up, the sex, the 3:30 a.m. return). The murder is not an inciting incident for episode one at all. It is the **hook for episode two**, dropped on the last two pages.

**Ordering and density.** The introductions run in four waves, and the ordering is the argument:

1. **Pages 1–11 (1–17%) — the institution.** Ten characters, all of them either police or complainants. The episode teaches you the *machinery* of the town before any of its private life.
2. **Pages 12–15 (18–24%) — the victim's triangle, complete, in four pages.** Erin, DJ, Dylan, Brianna, with motive and threat included. This is the densest and most consequential block in the script, and it lands before a quarter of the episode is gone. Everything the audience needs to suspect three people is delivered *before* they know there will be a murder — which is why the reveal on p.64A lands as recognition rather than information.
3. **Pages 16–35 (25–56%) — Mare's concentric circles**, in order of blood: colleagues (Trammel), old friends (Beth), the ex (Frank), the grandson (Drew), the mother (Helen), the priest-cousin (Father Dan), the daughter (Siobhan). Each is introduced at home, mid-chore.
4. **Pages 37A–56 (60–86%) — the crowd.** Band, Rosses, twelve teammates, tavern, Richard, the woods. Twenty-plus names in twenty pages, made survivable by the two mass-gathering set-pieces (the ceremony and the woods party) that let a dozen people be introduced in a single wide shot.

The density trick is that **almost nobody is introduced to the detective**. Mare meets exactly one new person in the episode (Richard). Everyone else she has known for decades, which is why the script can throw forty-three names at an audience without an interrogation scene: the *town* does the introducing, not the investigation. And the two set-pieces are load-bearing — the anniversary ceremony exists precisely so that twelve middle-aged women with a shared history can occupy one room at 70%, and Sharp's Woods exists so that a dozen teenagers, including the detective's daughter, can occupy one clearing at 84%.

**What the test shows.** Ingelsby's claim — that the case exists to force the character's grief into the open — is only half-true *in this episode*, and the half that isn't true is the interesting part. The case does not touch the grief for the first 66% except by rhyme. What forces the grief into the open here is not the murder but the **ex-husband's engagement** (p.30) and the **anniversary ceremony** (pp.44–48). The murder's job in episode one is different: it is the mechanism that will make Mare's grief *unavoidable in the episodes to come*, by handing a detective who cannot look at her own dead child a dead child to look at. The pilot's structure is therefore honest about its own sequencing — it opens the wound with domestic material first, then delivers the instrument that will keep it open.

One correction worth recording: **the custody fight for Drew is not in this episode.** Drew's mother is never named, never mentioned and never referenced; Kevin's death is never stated; the words "custody," "rehab" and "overdose" (as applied to Kevin) do not appear. The grief spine in episode one consists of exactly five pieces of evidence — an action line on p.1, the word "disaster" on p.30, "One. A daughter" on p.20, the turtle/Kevin exchange on pp.62–63, and the apparition on p.65. That is the entire dose.

## The delayed inciting incident

**Exact location:** scene 59, printed **pp.64–64A** — physical pages 78–79 of 80 — **~98%**. Erin's body is revealed in the last three-and-a-bit pages of an 80-page script, in a scene with no character in it.

Three candidate "incidents" and why only the third counts:

- **p.30 (~48%) — Frank's engagement.** This is the true inciting incident *of the episode*: it changes the protagonist's state and every subsequent scene is caused by it.
- **pp.53–56 (82–86%) — the ambush at Sharp's Woods.** This is the episode's violence beat and its emotional climax, but it is a crime the police never learn about in this hour.
- **pp.64–64A (98%) — the body.** This is the inciting incident *of the series* — and it is delivered after the episode's protagonist has already gone to bed.

**What fills the sixty-three pages before it:**

| Content | Printed pp. | Physical % of episode |
|---|---|---|
| Two complete non-murder cases (the Carroll prowler; the Freddie Hanlon burglary from sighting to shelter placement) | 1–7, 15–24, 60A | ~19% |
| The Katie Bailey cold case — political pressure, the file, the confrontation, the flyer | 10–11A, 26–27A, 43–44, 47–48, 61 | ~14% |
| Erin's last day, seen entirely from her own POV | 12–15, 24–26, 35–37, 49, 53–56 | ~20% |
| Mare's family and the engagement | 27–35, 37A–40, 61–63 | ~24% |
| The anniversary ceremony and its aftermath | 44–49 | ~9% |
| Richard / the bar / the one-night stand | 49–53, 56–60 | ~11% |
| Morning montage and the button | 63–65 | ~4% |

**Why the delay works.** Four reasons, all of them mechanical rather than tonal:

1. **The suspects are pre-loaded, so the reveal is recognition, not exposition.** By the time the body appears, the audience already holds a threat (p.14), two motives (jealousy and $1,800), a catfish, a violent father, a documented assault with a witness list, and three separate men in hooded sweatshirts. Episode two therefore never has to introduce a suspect pool; it can begin at the interview stage. In a conventional pilot the corpse arrives at 10% and the next fifty pages are spent installing the people this episode installed *before* anyone was dead — which means installing them as suspects, which means the audience meets everyone in bad faith. Here everyone is met in good faith and converted retroactively.
2. **The victim is a protagonist, so her death costs something.** Erin is given the most intimate shot in the episode (p.12), a mother's love scene, a dead mother of her own, a friend, a makeover, a bike ride with her eyes closed, and a public humiliation. Sixty physical pages of investment produce a corpse the audience mourns. A body found on page 6 is a plot problem; a body found on page 79 is a bereavement.
3. **The delay is what makes the grief spine legible.** If the case starts at 10%, every domestic scene reads as a *break* from the case. Because there is no case, the domestic scenes read as the show. When the murder finally arrives it is read through the grief the episode has spent an hour installing, rather than the reverse — which is the only way the series' central move (a mother of a dead child investigating a dead child) can land as anything other than a device.
4. **The episode has its own complete arc without the murder,** so the delay costs no structural integrity: the anniversary is planted at 8% and fires at 69–76%; the engagement is planted at 26% (the dropped call) and detonates at 48%; the turtle is bought at 24% and named at 95%. Remove Erin entirely and pp.1–63 still constitute a finished hour about a woman having the worst good day of her life.

**Where it strains.** The cost is that the episode has **no dramatic question for its first half**. Between p.1 and p.30 the only forward-driving hook is "what happens at the woods at 10?" — planted at 38%, which is late. The Katie Bailey case is stated to be unsolvable by the person investigating it (p.44: "we're never gonna find her. Never"), so it generates dread, not curiosity. What holds the first half is entirely performance, voice and comic specificity — the ferret drawing, the magnet on the fridge, the K-9 speech — which is a bet a lesser script cannot make. This is a structure available only to writers with sentence-level command and a star attached.

## Structural devices

- **The anniversary as a pressure device.** Planted at 8% by a neighbour (p.6), reinforced by a newspaper at 11% (p.8), dismissed by Mare at 34% ("Do you not remember sayin' how stupid the whole thing was"), and fired at 69–76% (pp.44–48). It does five jobs at once: it sets a hard appointment in a day with no plot; it delivers twenty-five years of biography as archive footage rather than dialogue; it assembles twelve women with a shared history in one room; it supplies the episode title on the character's ugliest beat; and it dates the story against Katie Bailey's identical one-year anniversary without ever remarking on the coincidence. **A ceremony is the cheapest legal way to put a whole town in one location and a whole backstory on one screen.**
- **The opening call — and the closing one.** Note that it is *not* a 911 call. Betty Carroll left **three messages on Mare's personal phone** rather than calling the station, and Mare's response is to physically move the police-station magnet to the centre of the fridge (p.3). The device establishes the show's actual premise — this detective is a public utility with no boundary between job and home — and it is bookended exactly: p.1, a phone wakes Mare at dawn for a non-crime; p.65, a phone wakes Mare at dawn for the crime. The second buzz is written as "the same ring, of course, but it seems louder somehow." The loop is the episode's real act structure.
- **The Erin / Dylan / Brianna triangle** as the entire suspect apparatus, installed pp.12A–15 (19–24%) and detonated pp.53–56 (82–86%). Its efficiency: one scene in a driveway supplies a custody dispute, a money motive, a jealousy motive, an explicit threat, a hoodie and a car. The catfish then converts Erin's single most romantic line — "I want you to dance again," first heard as tenderness on p.35 — into the trap that kills her, when it is spoken back at her in a girl's voice on p.55. **A line planted as intimacy and repaid as cruelty is worth three scenes of plot.**
- **Katie Bailey as the parallel case.** She is the show's argument in miniature: a girl who has been missing exactly as long as the episode's ceremony is old, whose face is literally being erased by weather (p.61), and about whom the detective privately says "we're never gonna find her. Never." She sets the stakes for Erin — *this is what happens here when a girl disappears* — supplies the professional clock, gives Mare an antagonist (Dawn) who is also a mirror (a mother who lost a child), and gives the episode its only redemptive act. The flyer being re-taped at 94% is Mare answering, at 3 a.m. and alone, the challenge her Chief made at 17%.
- **The hooded sweatshirt, three times.** Kyle next door (p.5), Dylan at the door (p.13), and the man who dissolves in an alley (p.60A). Reported first by a witness at 5% ("he was wearin' a hooded sweatshirt… he looked like a ferret"). The motif turns an item of clothing worn by half the town into a permanent low-grade suspicion, and the third instance is never explained.
- **The final-page image.** Kevin, 20, in profile on the desk, Converse toe tapping, watching two sparrows — an apparition given to us *before* the plot's summons, and given without comment or explanation. Then the phone. The construction is precise: the audience's last exclusive knowledge about the protagonist is her private haunting; the case has to interrupt it to exist. Compare the second-to-last image — a dead girl's borrowed earrings quivering in a current — and the pair states the series' method: **two mothers, two dead children, one of them a case and one of them a ghost.**
- **The object chain.** Cat earrings (from a dead mother) → swapped for Trisha's gold chandeliers (p.37) → the only thing recognisable on the corpse (p.64A). And: a $39.99 aquarium bought as a joke (p.15) → assembled by Siobhan and Drew (p.33) → named Kevin (p.62). Two objects carry both spines and neither is ever discussed.
- **The passing train.** Established at the station on p.8, then used to rattle the office walls while Mare stares at Katie's photograph on p.27A. Ambient pressure and a thesis about a town things pass through.
- **The ironic detective statement.** Patty Delrasso's drunk *Sherlock* monologue (p.49) — "He can walk into a room and just like — (SNAPS) He's got it. EV-E-RY-THING" — is the show telling you, at 78%, exactly what kind of detective story this will not be. It arrives eight pages after Mare has failed to identify anything at all and fifteen pages before the audience gets a body.
- **The song montage as suspect board.** Five scenes, one Anaïs Mitchell cue, no dialogue (pp.63–64): the ex, the daughter, the ex-boyfriend and his girlfriend, the other grieving mother, and the victim's father coming home at dawn. It is a whip-round of every household with a stake in what is about to be found, and it is presented as a lyrical morning-after sequence.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework

- **The slow burn is total, not relative.** Standard crime-pilot construction puts the body in the teaser and the case on the detective's desk by 15%. Here the body arrives at 98% and the detective never learns of it. There is no crime scene, no forensics, no partner assigned, no suspect interview, no witness statement and no clue in the entire episode. The only investigative act performed on screen is taping up a flyer.
- **Ensemble density outruns any conventional pilot budget.** Fifty-four named characters in eighty pages. The framework advice — introduce your principals slowly, one strong impression each — is disregarded wholesale. What makes it survivable: (a) every character is introduced doing a domestic chore, so the audience files them by activity rather than by name; (b) two mass-gathering set-pieces absorb twenty of them; (c) surnames do structural work (Delrasso, Hanlon, Ross, Bailey, McMenamin, Sheehan appear across generations, so a new face inherits an existing file); and (d) Mare is pre-acquainted with everybody, so no scene has to spend dialogue on who someone is.
- **The withheld crime inverts the genre's information contract.** Normally the audience knows more than the town and less than the killer. Here the audience knows more than the *detective* — we have watched the victim's whole last day, including her humiliation and the identities of everyone present — and the pilot ends with the protagonist about to be told something we already know. The dramatic irony is the pilot's payload, not the mystery.
- **The protagonist has no want and no plan.** She is not pursuing anything for eighty pages; she is being pursued (by a Chief, a Commission, a grieving mother, an ex-husband's happiness, a daughter's applications and an anniversary she can't skip). The engine is pressure, not desire — legitimate for a limited series, fatal in a network pilot.
- **No act breaks, no cliffhanger structure.** The script runs continuously A1 → 60 with no TEASER, no ACT ONE, no FADE IN and no CUT TO. Even the natural act-out candidates are refused: p.30 (the engagement) plays straight into the next scene; p.56 (the beating) cuts to a bar; the discovery of the body at p.64A is not the last scene, and the episode gives its final two minutes back to a domestic image.
- **The two most consequential characters in the series get the least screen time.** Ryan Ross gets three lines of homework dialogue at 65%; John Ross gets a bad lie and a coat. Placing your ending inside your most disposable-looking beat is only possible in a pilot this crowded — the density is the hiding place, which is precisely the primary lens's answer: *episode one builds a community web dense enough to hide a killer by making the killer indistinguishable from the furniture.*
- **The tone contract is comic for the first quarter.** A ferret cartoon, a fat-shaming joke at roll call, a man pissing in car windows, "I already caught the bastard! He's in a cage in my daughter's bedroom." The episode establishes comedy as its floor so that the fall — a girl beaten in a wood, a body in a creek — has somewhere to fall from.

## Lessons for the knowledge base

1. **A murder mystery can spend its entire first hour on everything except the murder, provided the suspects are installed before there is a crime.** Erin's triangle is complete with threat and motive by p.14 (~21%); the body lands at ~98%. The reveal then costs zero exposition and episode two can open at the interview stage. Evidence: Brianna's "You're gonna get it, Erin. Trust me. When you least expect it" (p.14) is fifty physical pages ahead of the corpse.
2. **Introduce every suspect in good faith, doing a chore.** Dylan is a deadbeat dad at a door (p.13), Kenny is a man burning his mouth on pizza (p.25), Father Dan is a priest making Manhattans (p.29), John Ross is a man lying about a party (p.42). None is introduced as a suspect, so the audience files them as *people* and converts them retroactively — which is both more humane and more efficient than fifty pages of shifty behaviour. The corollary: **the character who will matter most can be hidden in the most ordinary beat available** (Ryan Ross, doing homework, three lines, 65%).
3. **Give the victim a protagonist's POV before killing her.** Erin gets the episode's most intimate shot (p.12), a love scene with her son, a dead mother, a best friend, a makeover, a hands-free bike ride and a public humiliation — sixty physical pages of investment. A body found early is a plot problem; a body found late, after this, is a bereavement, and the audience arrives at episode two already grieving rather than merely curious.
4. **A community-wide social obligation is the most efficient structural device available to an ensemble mystery.** The 25th-anniversary ceremony (planted p.6, fired pp.44–48) simultaneously supplies a hard appointment, twenty-five years of backstory as archive footage, twelve characters in one room, the episode title, the protagonist's lowest moment, and a date-stamp that rhymes with the cold case. Any wedding, funeral, reunion, championship or memorial can be made to do all six.
5. **Bookend the episode on the same mundane trigger with the stakes inverted.** A phone wakes Mare at dawn for a peeping-tom report (p.1); a phone wakes Mare at dawn for a homicide (p.65) — "the same ring, of course, but it seems louder somehow." The loop supplies act structure to a script with no act breaks and makes the pilot a complete object without resolving anything.
6. **Deliver the protagonist's central wound in the last five per cent, through an object and a child, and never state it.** The turtle is bought as a joke at 24% and named "Kevin" by a four-year-old at 95%; the death itself is never described, only implied by a past tense, one word ("the disaster," p.30) and one action line ("Pretending, if only for a few moments, that it's her son"). Withholding the backstory converts every earlier scene into re-readable material on a second viewing.
7. **Test the "case forces the grief open" claim by charting contact points — and expect the domestic material to do the early work.** In this episode the case touches the grief only four times (pp.43–44, 44–48, 61, 63–65). What actually opens the wound at the midpoint is an **ex-husband's engagement** (p.30, ~48%). The lesson generalises: in a grief-driven mystery, the case is the *long-term* instrument; the pilot still needs a domestic detonation at its own midpoint, or the first half has no turn.
8. **A cold case is a better first-episode engine than a hot one, because it supplies dread instead of curiosity.** Katie Bailey has been missing exactly one year, is described by the investigating detective as unfindable, and has a face literally weathering off a lamp post (p.61). She sets the price of disappearing in this town, gives the protagonist an antagonist who is also her mirror, and provides the only redemptive act in eighty pages — a fresh flyer taped up at 3 a.m., "Nice and tight."
9. **Say what kind of detective story you are not, out loud, in a minor character's mouth.** Patty Delrasso's drunk *Sherlock* speech at 78% ("He's got it. EV-E-RY-THING") arrives after the protagonist has solved nothing all day. One comic monologue does the genre-positioning that a lesser script attempts with tone.
10. **Density is a hiding place, and surnames are free density.** Fifty-four named characters survive eighty pages because families recur across generations — Delrasso (teammate and bully), Hanlon (teammate, addict brother), Ross (best friend, husband, two children), Bailey (missing girl, campaigning mother), Sheehan (detective, ex-husband, daughter, dead son, grandson). A shared surname makes a new face inherit an existing file, and a town that feels genealogically real is a town in which one more ordinary person can be the killer.
