## Logline
A fifteen-year-old raised in the African bush and home-schooled by zoologists is adopted as a pet by her new high school's ruling clique, agrees to infiltrate them as a joke, and executes a sabotage campaign so successfully that she becomes the girl she was sent in to destroy.

## Structure overview
The script runs on **two clocks laid over each other**. The outer clock is the **American school year** — first day, Halloween, Thanksgiving, Christmas talent show, spring, Spring Fling — and it does the act architecture almost invisibly: each holiday is a set-piece, and each set-piece raises Cady one rung. The inner clock is a **four-item checklist** written on a child's chalkboard in a basement at p.39 (33%) — "hot body / older boyfriend / skanked-out clothes / army of whores" — which converts a social comedy into a heist. The script then cuts back to that chalkboard four times (pp.63, 66, 70, 71) to cross items off, so the audience always knows exactly how far the mission has progressed and, crucially, how far Cady has fallen.

The distinctive move is that the two clocks are **counter-signed**: every calendar occasion that in a normal teen comedy would mark Cady's growing belonging (Halloween party, talent show, Spring Fling nomination, her own house party) is the occasion of a moral loss. Snyder's "Fun and Games" and "Bad Guys Close In" are not sequential here — they are the same scenes, read twice.

**Overall verdict:** eight of fifteen beats land within two points of Snyder's marks, including the four hardest (Opening Image, Theme Stated, Midpoint, Final Image). The script's two real deviations are structurally linked and both are consequences of the genre: a **Debate that barely exists** (because a fall requires a hero who says yes) and a **Break into Three that arrives at 84%** (because a fall's hero must be publicly humiliated before she can be forgiven, and humiliation takes pages).

## Storylines

**A — The infiltration (Cady ↔ the Plastics).** Recruited p.17 (15%), inducted p.19 (17%), compromised p.29 (25%), tasked p.39 (33%), armed pp.42–48 (36–41%), victorious in four stages pp.63–71 (54–61%), crowned p.71 (61%), exposed p.97 (83%). This plot owns the checklist, the calendar, and every set-piece. Its dramatic question is not "will she win?" — she wins at 61% — but "what will be left of her when she does?"

**B — Cady/Aaron.** Planted p.12 (10%), prohibited p.20 (17%), stolen from her at Halloween p.37 (32%), pursued through self-inflicted stupidity pp.34–41 and 60–62, consummated and immediately poisoned p.62–63 (53–54%), destroyed by vomit p.79 (68%), and then **dormant for 22 pages** until a transactional reappearance at p.101 and the closing kiss at p.114 (97%). The thinnest strand in the script: Aaron makes exactly one thematic contribution ("there's good and bad in everybody," p.63) and takes no independent action after p.79. The diagnostic is who is missing from the convergence scene — Aaron is the only principal not in the gymnasium at pp.90–97.

**C — Janis and Damian's revenge.** The oldest wound in the movie (Regina's eighth-grade excommunication of Janis, narrated by the perpetrator at pp.46–47) and the script's actual engine. Janis supplies the mandate (p.31), the plan (p.39), the operations room (the basement chalkboard, pp.39–71), the moral audit ("You're not pretending anymore! You're plastic!", p.80) and the detonator (the public confession, p.97). Damian is the tool-user and the pressure valve — he executes the candy canes (p.48), the failed photo raid (pp.54–55) and the fake-Barry testimony (pp.103–104). Their arc quietly indicts them too: Janis's revenge costs a teacher her job and lands a girl in the hospital, and the script never lets her apologize for it — her p.97 "apology" is sarcastic to the last word.

**D — Regina's counterattack.** Structurally a *late* storyline: Regina is a possessive obstacle in Act One, an unwitting victim through Act Two, and only becomes an active antagonist at p.81 (69%) — where she reads the situation correctly, forges her own victimhood in the Burn Book, and destroys the entire sophomore class in six pages. It is the fastest, cleanest antagonist reversal in the script and it makes her the only character who solves a problem by *escalating* rather than confessing. She is granted interiority twice, both times after defeat: the sweatpants line (p.71) and the doll house (p.111).

**E — The adult layer (Ms. Norbury, Mr. Duvall, Betsy and Chip).** Two systems of authority running in parallel. Norbury is the competence mirror (she catches the deliberate failure at p.73 — "All the work is right. Just the answers are wrong") and the thesis-speaker (pp.93–96); she is also the script's collateral damage, which converts theme into debt. Duvall is the institution: he issues the punishment that creates the confession chamber (p.87) and hands out the crown (p.112). Betsy and Chip hold the pre-story world and administer the dark night (p.88) and its refusal (p.89).

**Convergence.** The script converges three times, and the geometry is precise: **the gymnasium (pp.90–97, 77–83%)** puts A, C, D and E in one room with no exit — every plot is resolved by being spoken aloud; **the school board (pp.101–104, 86–89%)** closes the adult layer alone; **Spring Fling (pp.110–115, 94–98%)** reassembles A, B, C and D for the object-resolution. Note that no single scene contains all five — the love story never shares a frame with the confession, which is exactly why it feels bolted on.

## Structural devices

**1. V.O. as a five-tool instrument, then its removal.** Cady narrates from p.2 to p.117 and the V.O. does five distinct structural jobs: *time compression* ("On October 3rd he asked me what day it was," p.34, covering weeks in six lines); *field-notes irony* (the Girl World rules p.19, the Halloween rules p.36); *real-time contradiction* of what she is saying out loud (tutoring, p.35: "Wrong. He was so wrong"; p.62: "No, that's wrong… There you go"); *self-indictment* (the word-vomit sequences, pp.57, 63, 78); and *epilogue machine* (pp.115–117 disposes of eight characters in two pages). The load-bearing choice is the **withdrawal**: the apology at p.97 — the emotional climax of the arc — is the one major beat delivered with no V.O. cover at all, and the freeze-frame at p.117 lets the V.O. protest the ending it can no longer control.

**2. Anthropology as a decaying device.** The framing is stated in the premise (zoologist parents, four years in Namibia, pp.3–4) and rendered as *image* twice — the mall as watering hole with jungle SFX and mating giraffes (pp.29–30, 26%) and the cafeteria cheetah attack (pp.39–40, 33%). After p.40 it survives only as *figure of speech*: Chip's lions-and-warthogs argument (p.89, 76%), Regina's "home-schooled jungle freak" (p.98, 84%), and the last line, "like treading water in a shark tank, but now, I just float" (p.117). Whether the decay is design or drift is arguable — the effect is that the conceit reads as Cady's *detachment mechanism* and fades as she is absorbed — but the practical consequence is that this draft's biggest formal idea is spent in the first third and never returns as spectacle. (The release film redistributes the savanna imagery toward the climax; this draft does not.)

**3. The Burn Book as an escalation ladder.** Five rungs, each with a different owner and a different blast radius: **private artifact** (p.28, "It's just a joke" — a record of past cruelty, safely historical) → **Cady's authorship** (p.29 — she stops being a reader) → **weapon reversed** (p.81, Regina writes herself in) → **mass publication** (pp.82–85, xeroxed, slid under doors, thrown like confetti) → **institutional catastrophe** (pp.85–87, a teacher fired, a coach fled, a Saturday detention for an entire grade). The craft lesson is in the *targeting*: the page that destroys an adult's career is the page the protagonist wrote 52 pages earlier as a throwaway joke, which means the script's largest consequence is attached to its smallest choice.

**4. The chalkboard as mission HUD.** A toy chalkboard in a basement, four items, four cutaways (pp.63, 66, 70, 71) each no longer than three lines. It gives the middle of the script an unmistakable progress bar, makes the audience complicit in wanting the items crossed off, and then — because each cutaway is butted against Regina's humiliation — turns that complicity into the movie's argument. Cheap to shoot, impossible to misread.

**5. The school calendar as act architecture.** First day (p.1) → October 3rd (p.34, 29%) → Halloween (pp.36–38, 31–32%) → Thanksgiving (p.42, 36%) → candy canes and the Winter Talent Show (pp.46–53, 39–45%) → "By Spring break" (p.55, 47%) → Spring Fling nominations (pp.58–59, 50%) → Spring Fling (pp.104–115, 89–98%) → "The following fall" (pp.115–117). The dance is named at p.10 (9%) by Damian and is the third act's destination, so the script never needs to announce a goal — the school year announces it. **One wobble:** the V.O. says "By Spring break, something had changed" at p.55 (47%) and then "In January, Regina had put a Spring Fling dress on hold" at p.65 (56%) — the two references sit out of order, a draft artifact rather than a design.

**6. Escalating split-screen and formal one-offs.** Phone treachery escalates visually: two panels (p.24, the Planned Parenthood prank), three panels (pp.33–34, the three-way calling attack on Cady), then three panels with a silently held line (pp.67–69, the trap that finishes off Gretchen and Karen). Alongside it sit single-use devices deployed exactly where the ordinary channel has failed: the direct-to-camera testimonial montage (p.45), the subtitled shoe-store fight with Betsy (pp.66–67), the subtitled Vietnamese gossip in the coda (p.116). Each formal break marks a relationship that can no longer be played straight.

**7. Plant/payoff economy.** The script's density here is unusual for a comedy:
- **The bus** — physical near-miss p.5 (4%) → metaphor for falling in love p.13 (11%) → literal, "the same spot," p.98 (84%).
- **Kalteen bars** — found in Cady's own cupboard p.42 (36%) → deployed p.43 → doubted p.56 → visibly working p.70 → exposed by Shane p.80 → **prescribed by the doctor** to make Regina healthy p.99 (85%).
- **The rigged scale** — p.44 (38%) → "They should fit. I weigh 112" p.50 (43%) → confessed p.97 (83%).
- **Barry's ecstasy in the locked drawer** — p.22 (19%) → Cady's Burn Book joke p.29 (25%) → confiscated p.85 (73%) → the entire atonement pp.100–104 (85–89%).
- **Janis's map** — drawn p.12 (10%) → studied in bed p.18 (16%) → "You Are Here" navigates Cady back to her friends p.98 (84%).
- **Spring Fling** — explained p.10 (9%) → Regina's reign p.15 (13%) → nominations p.58 (50%) → the broken crown p.113 (96%).
- **The word "plastic"** — Janis's insult p.11 (9%) → Janis's accusation p.80 (68%) → the crown's material p.113 (96%): "It's just a piece of plastic."
- **"Grool"** p.35 (30%) → the closing kiss p.114 (97%). **"Fetch"** p.16 → p.44 → killed on purpose at p.53 ("Stop trying to make fetch happen") — a runner about the impossibility of self-authored status, whose joke *is* that it never pays off.
- **Karen's drums** p.64 (55%) → marching band p.116 (99%). **The Egg McMuffin girl** p.5 (4%) → in the Burn Book p.28 → lands the killing blow on Regina p.71 (61%) → praised by name from the stage p.113 (96%).

## Deviations & tensions with the framework

**A corruption arc inside a redemptive template.** Snyder's fifteen beats describe a hero who is flawed, tested, broken and cured — an ascending shape with one deep trough. Mean Girls is a descending shape: the protagonist gets everything she set out to get by p.71 (61%) and the getting is the damage. The template mostly survives the transplant because Fey doubles up the beats rather than fighting them — pp.42–71 are simultaneously "Fun and Games" (the premise delivering on its promise: sabotage comedy) and "Bad Guys Close In" (the same footage read as descent). Two places show the strain:
- **The Debate collapses to two and a half pages** (pp.17–19). A fall needs a protagonist who says yes; a hero who agonizes over joining the Plastics is not corruptible on schedule. The price is that Act Two has to be started twice, at p.19 (social threshold) and p.39 (mission threshold), with a 20-page stretch in between where the protagonist has no goal beyond "observe."
- **The Break into Three slides to 84%.** In a triumph shape the hero synthesizes A and B stories into a new plan at 75%. Here the hero has to be publicly stripped first — riot (pp.82–87), family rejection (p.88), social death (p.90), diagnosis (p.95), exposure (p.97) — and that takes pages. The consequence is a compressed 17-page third act asked to carry seven movements.

**The bus.** Cady's apology at p.97 is genuine, complete, itemized and *refused*; the plot answers the refusal with a municipal vehicle. By the strictest reading this is a deus ex machina at the exact hinge of the story. Three things keep it standing. First, it is planted twice (p.5 physical, p.13 metaphorical) and the action line at p.98 explicitly cites the earlier plant, so it is a payoff rather than an invention. Second, the moral turn *precedes* the intervention by one page — Cady has already changed before the bus removes her opponent, so the bus grants her no victory. Third, the script names the objection and puts it in the antagonist's mouth thirteen pages later: "Why? Are you a superhero? Do you control the buses? Stop trying to make this about you" (p.111). What the bus actually accomplishes structurally is **the destruction of the win condition** — it makes "did Cady beat Regina?" unanswerable, which is exactly the question a corruption arc must not be allowed to answer. It works, but it works by admitting what it is.

**The group-therapy third act (pp.90–97, 77–83%).** Eight pages of an ensemble sitting on a gym floor doing exercises, in which a supporting character states the theme aloud. On paper this is everything a third act should not be: static, single-location, expository, and it resolves a social plot with a lecture. Four things make it work:
1. **It is imposed, not chosen.** Duvall's punishment traps Cady in the one room where the truth must surface — dramatic irony rather than protagonist initiative, which is the correct move when your protagonist has no moral standing left to act from.
2. **It escalates physically before it escalates verbally.** Gretchen is dropped in the trust fall (p.92) and says the theme with her body — "If someone falls, and you don't catch them, they get hurt!" — thirty seconds before Norbury says it with her mouth.
3. **It decentres the protagonist first.** The parade of other cliques (pp.91–92) and Norbury's "don't think Regina is the meanest girl in this school" (p.94) shrink the Plastics to one of a dozen groups, retroactively voiding the entire mission Cady sacrificed herself to complete.
4. **The comedy is the pressure valve.** "She doesn't even go here"; "I have a gorgeous bunghole"; "Karen, you are at your peak right now." Every sincere line is bracketed by a joke, so the sermon never gets to sit unchallenged.
The honest weakness is load: Ms. Norbury delivers the diagnosis for four characters in three pages, and the ensemble's social thesis is resolved in the two pages of the "say one true good thing about your body" exercise. It is fast, and it is the mentor doing work the protagonist could not.

**The B story does not carry the theme.** Snyder is explicit that the B story exists to hold the movie's argument while the A story holds its plot. Aaron holds nothing; he is a prize, and after p.79 he vanishes for 22 pages. The thematic work is done by a teacher, which effectively promotes the adult layer into the B-story slot and leaves the romance decorative. Evidence that the script knows: Cady's closing V.O. treats him as loot — "I mean, I was still gonna take her old boyfriend. I'm not crazy" (p.114).

**Where this draft differs from the release film (worth flagging for library users).** The school is **Evanston High School** in real Evanston, Illinois (the film relocates to the fictional North Shore High); "**On Tuesdays we wear pink**" (the film: Wednesdays); **Kevin Gnor** (the film: Gnapoor). Most importantly, this draft's atonement runs through a **Barry / ecstasy / school-board sequence** (pp.21–22, 85, 100–104) in which Cady rehabilitates Ms. Norbury by producing a witness and takes two weeks' detention for spreading the rumor — and the **authorship of the Burn Book is never resolved with the administration at all**, which is a genuine loose end: Duvall's investigation (pp.82–85) simply stops. The release film replaces this with the tighter and more thematically direct choice of having Cady confess to writing the Burn Book herself and absorb the punishment. The draft also ends on an eight-character "following fall" montage and a soda-out-the-nose freeze-frame rather than the film's junior-Plastics button. Read as a rewrite lesson: **the draft's atonement invents new machinery in Act Three; the film's atonement pays an existing debt.** That is the difference between a 17-page third act that runs and one that sprints.

## Lessons for the knowledge base

1. **When the hero must be seduced rather than choose, stage the threshold in gates.** Mean Girls crosses into Act Two four times — invitation p.17 (15%), social threshold p.19 (17%), moral threshold p.29 (25%), mission threshold p.39 (33%). Snyder's single door is the wrong shape for a fall, because a character who deliberates at the door cannot be corrupted convincingly. Put the *moral* gate on the 25% mark (the small, irreversible, deniable compromise) and let the mission arrive later.

2. **In a corruption arc, "Fun and Games" and "Bad Guys Close In" are the same footage.** Every sabotage scene (pp.42–71) is funny on first read and damning on second, and the script guarantees the second read by butting each victory against its victim: cross off "older boyfriend" (p.63) then cut to Regina sobbing; cross off "hot body" (p.71) then give her the only defenseless line she has ("These sweatpants are all that fits me right now"). You do not need separate descent scenes; you need adjacency.

3. **Attach your largest consequence to your smallest choice.** Cady's throwaway doodle at p.29 (25%) — one joke in a scrapbook, made under peer pressure, resolved with a sheepish nod — is what costs a teacher her career at p.85 (73%) and structures the entire third act. Audit your script for the inverse failure: a catastrophe that traces back to nothing the protagonist did.

4. **Give the middle act a visible progress bar.** Four items, a toy chalkboard, four three-line cutaways. It solves the classic Act Two problem (the audience can't tell how far along they are) for almost no page cost, makes the audience complicit in wanting the list completed, and gives you a free structural landmark when the last item falls — here at p.71 (61%), the moment the mask becomes the face.

5. **A narrator can be made to lose control of the narration.** "Word vomit" (pp.57, 63, 78) turns Cady's own speech into an external event the V.O. reports in horror — a comic device that is really a character-arc instrument, since it dramatizes an interiority that has been colonized. Then withhold the device at the climax: the apology at p.97 is the only major beat with no V.O., and the silence is the arc.

6. **Plant physically, re-plant metaphorically, pay off literally.** The bus is a near-miss at p.5 (4%), a simile for falling in love at p.13 (11%), and an event at p.98 (84%) with the action line citing the original location. The double-register plant is what lets a coincidence read as inevitability.

7. **A deus ex machina survives if the moral turn precedes it and the script admits it.** Cady apologizes on p.97; the bus arrives on p.98; Regina names the cheat on p.111 ("Are you a superhero? Do you control the buses?"). The intervention removes the *victory*, not the *change* — which is precisely what a fall needs, because a corruption arc that ends in a won fight is a triumph arc wearing a costume.

8. **End a corruption arc by re-aiming the competence, not restoring the innocence.** Cady solves her third-act problem by asking "How would Regina handle this? She would 'attack' and 'confuse'" — and the V.O. thanks Regina by name (pp.100–101). She keeps the predator's toolkit, keeps the boyfriend (p.114), and keeps a fragment of the crown in her hair (p.113). Two words of V.O. do more character work than the apology, because they concede that the corruption was also an education.

9. **A static "everyone in one room" climax works under four conditions.** Impose it institutionally rather than have the hero convene it (Duvall's punishment, p.87); escalate physically before verbally (the dropped trust fall, p.92, states the theme with a body); shrink the protagonist's world first so her victory is voided (the other cliques, pp.91–92); and bracket every sincere line with a joke. Break any one and it becomes a lecture.

10. **Let the school calendar do the act work, and announce your finale in Act One.** Halloween, Thanksgiving, the Christmas talent show and Spring Fling supply four set-pieces, four time-jumps and a deadline without a single line of structural exposition — and Spring Fling is named at p.10 (9%) by the character who dismisses it, so the destination is set 102 pages before arrival. (Corollary caution from this draft: check your calendar for order — "By Spring break" at p.55 precedes "In January" at p.65.)

11. **Spend your high-concept framing device across the whole script or it will read as a first-act mannerism.** The animal-kingdom conceit is rendered as spectacle only at pp.29–30 and pp.39–40 (26% and 33%) and thereafter survives only as dialogue metaphor (pp.89, 98, 117). Whatever your governing conceit is, plot its appearances on a page graph before you lock the draft — if the last third has none, either restore them or cut the early ones and let the premise carry it.
