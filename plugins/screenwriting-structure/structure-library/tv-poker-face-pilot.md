# Poker Face 1x01 "Dead Man's Hand" (2023) — Structural Beat Map

**Source:** /mnt/user-data/uploads/Screenplay planning/Ressources/Script Library/TV Pilots/Comedy/poker-face-101-dead-mans-hand-2023.pdf · **Writer:** Rian Johnson (sole credit on title page) · **Format:** TV pilot, streaming hour (Peacock; aired 26 Jan 2023, written and directed by Rian Johnson) · **Genre:** inverted mystery / howcatchem procedural with comedy voice · **Length:** 65 printed pages (66 PDF pages incl. title page); title page marked "1.1", no draft date printed; PDF metadata: Fade In, file created 15 May 2020
**Framework lens(es):** PRIMARY — the howcatchem / inverted mystery (the Columbo model: crime shown first, pleasure = watching the detective close in). SECONDARY 1 — the clue ledger (every fact planted in the crime half, paid in the detection half, plus the audience-ahead / audience-behind schedule). SECONDARY 2 — the procedural engine (human lie detector + fugitive-on-the-road) and the deliberate crippling of the gift so it cannot solve the show.

Page citations are the script's **printed** page numbers (printed page N = PDF page N+1; the title page is unnumbered and PDF page 1 of 66). Percentages are of 65 printed pages. **There are no printed act breaks** — no TEASER, no ACT ONE, no END OF ACT, not even a FADE IN. The script runs as one continuous block punctuated by exactly three hard transitions: `CUT TO BLACK / FADE UP ON` (p.9, the rewind), `HARD CUT TO:` (p.23), and `SMASH CUT TO:` (p.31, into the "SLAIN" headline). Everything else is a straight scene cut.

## Logline

A casino cocktail waitress who can tell when anyone is lying watches her best friend get murdered by the men she works for — and, with no badge, no evidence and no jurisdiction in a county the killers own, has to find a way to hurt them that has nothing to do with the law, before running for the rest of her life.

## Structure overview

The pilot is built in **two halves with an unlabelled rewind at p.9 (~14%)**, and the halves are radically unequal: the crime occupies 9 pages, the detection 56. The crime half is told entirely from the killers' side and ends on a shot that **hides the detective inside it** ("Then walks back across the room and sits across from an *unseen woman*" — p.9). The detection half then rewinds roughly 36 hours and runs Charlie's parallel story forward until the two timelines physically touch at p.29 ("Cliff and a distraught Natalie in the waiting room. **We've seen this scene already**") and overlap at p.30 (the murder-confirmation call, now shot from the detective's chair).

That produces a three-stage engine, not the two-stage Columbo engine:

1. **Crime (pp.1–9).** The audience is given everything except the content of the laptop and the identity of the woman in the room.
2. **Rewound run-up (pp.9–31).** No mystery at all — pure dramatic irony. The audience watches the victim's last 36 hours knowing how they end, while the detective is busy being offered a job.
3. **Investigation (pp.32–61) and launch (pp.62–65).** The detective learns a crime happened at p.32 (49%) — the exact centre of the episode — and spends the back half not *identifying* the killers (the audience already knows) but finding something she can *do* about them.

Two things about this shape are unusual enough to be the file's headline findings. First, **the detective does not enter her own case until the midpoint**, which no standard Columbo does. Second, **the murder plot and the heist plot are welded to the same scenes** — Sterling recruits Charlie and orders Natalie's death within the same twenty minutes of story time (pp.28–31), so every heist beat in the back half is simultaneously a murder beat.

## The rewind

**Exact location: the bottom of printed page 9 (~14%).** The transition is `CUT TO BLACK` / `FADE UP ON` / `EXT. BULL RUN - EARLY MORNING`. This is the only CUT TO BLACK in the script and one of only three hard transition cues in 65 pages, so the punctuation is unambiguous even though nothing on the page names it as a flashback. The rewind goes back roughly **36 hours** — to the morning of the day before the murder. The rewound timeline then runs for 22 pages and rejoins the crime half at p.29–30.

**What is re-shown (literally replayed):** only two scenes, totalling perhaps ninety seconds of screen time.

| Crime-half original | Detection-half replay | What the replay adds |
|---|---|---|
| pp.3–4: Cliff brings a distraught Natalie into the reception room and calls a code red | p.29: Sterling, having just put Charlie into the private elevator, opens his office door — "Cliff and a distraught Natalie in the waiting room. We've seen this scene already." "Cliff. What's up?" | Geography and near-miss. Charlie left through one door as Natalie arrived at another. The audience now knows the two women were separated by a wall |
| p.9: Sterling takes a call in the observation room, sits back down opposite an unseen woman. "You alright?" — "Yeah. Where were we?" | p.30: the same beat from the reverse angle — the unseen woman is Charlie; the call is "Okay, it's done"; "The slightest perceptible hitch from Charlie" | Identity (the woman is the protagonist), meaning (the call is the murder confirmation), and a clue (Charlie half-registers the lie and files it, retrieving it at p.52) |

**What is newly shown:** everything else — Charlie, her trailer, John-O, the gift and its rules, the Frost legend, the heist offer, Jerry's rampage, the ankle-holster pistol, the sleepover, the "Miss Galahad" scene, Caine at the tables, the 48-hour clock, the crow's nest, the handshake. The rewind is roughly 95% new material with two anchor scenes of overlap.

**The craft rules that keep an already-solved replay dramatic:**

1. **Hide one person, not one fact.** The crime half plays fair on every plot point and cheats on exactly one thing — the identity of the woman in the room (p.9). One concealed identity buys 22 pages of forward momentum, because the replay is now a reveal.
2. **Don't label the rewind; give it cues.** No title card, no dissolve, no "36 HOURS EARLIER." The script says so explicitly to the reader on p.11 — "We'll have plenty of time and cues over the next few scenes to recognize her and realize we've flashed back in time, **no need to get it immediately**." The cues are physical: the Elvis sunglasses (p.11 → matching p.6's locker scene), the hula-girl bobblehead (p.8 → p.12), the beat-up Sentra, the same metal detectors. Recognition is a pleasure; a caption is an instruction.
3. **Give the audience a person to fear for, not a puzzle to solve.** From p.10 to p.31 there is no mystery whatsoever. The tension is entirely "this is her last day and she doesn't know it," which is why the rewind spends its longest scenes on the least plotty material (the locker room, the sleepover, "Miss Galahad").
4. **Make the replayed scenes cost something.** Both replays land on the protagonist's guilt: she was ten feet away when Natalie came for help (p.29), and she was in the room when the murder was confirmed (p.30). The replay is not "here is what you already saw"; it is "here is where you were."
5. **Let the rewind arm the third act.** Every object the climax needs is issued during the rewind, in scenes that read as character comedy: the cloud joke (p.14), the combo padlock (p.14), the ankle holster (p.22), the "gamblers talk" legend (p.18), the balcony (p.28). The replay period is the pilot's armoury.
6. **Converge before the midpoint, not at the climax.** The timelines rejoin at 45–48%, leaving a full half for the actual investigation. If the convergence were the climax, the episode would be a shaggy-dog flashback; landing it early turns the whole rewind into an extended first act.
7. **Cut out of the overlap on a promise, not a recap.** The rewind ends on a handshake and a $1.5m deal (p.31) and SMASH CUTS straight to the word "SLAIN." The two timelines are stitched with a reversal, so the seam is invisible.

- **Behind (p.43 → 44):** the ATM spark — "Oh oh oh oh —" — is withheld for a page.
- **Behind (p.50 → 59):** "Charlie breathes, unlocks her phone." The audience sees the action and not the reason, which is the only fair way to spring a reversal on people who already know whodunit.
- **Level (pp.57–58):** the metal-detector deduction is delivered to Sterling and the audience simultaneously — the classic Columbo "explanation as climax."

## The lie-detector rules

Every use of the gift, and the limit each use installs. The design principle is visible: **the ability is 100% reliable and almost entirely useless**, so the plot cannot be short-circuited by it.

**Net effect on the format.** Across 65 pages the gift never once identifies the murderer. It identifies that *something is wrong* ("something's off, there's a lie here. I need to find it," p.39), it confirms one negative (p.50), and it makes her a target (p.53). The case is solved by a timeline, a cloud backup and an indicator light. That is the deliberate design: an ability that answers "is this true?" in a genre whose real question is always "what happened, and can you prove it?"

**The series engine, stated plainly.** A protagonist with (a) a portable ability that needs no equipment, no partner and no institution; (b) no money and no ability to earn it at cards, so she must take jobs among strangers; (c) a compulsion to intervene for "the little guy"; (d) a rich, patient, geographically unlimited pursuer who does not know where she is; and (e) no phone. Each week she takes a job inside a small sealed world, hears a lie, and must convert it into proof or leverage without police help before the pursuit catches up. The pilot spends 65 pages installing exactly these five things and nothing else.

## Storylines

**A — The murder (Natalie and Jerry).** pp.1–9 (commission), pp.32–46 (investigation), pp.47–58 (proof), p.58 (the gotcha). The A-story is unusual in that it is *complete* by p.9 and *known* by p.9; what the back half dramatises is not discovery but the search for a remedy. Note the split culprit: Cliff pulls the trigger, Sterling gives the order, and Charlie's proof (the missing gun) only convicts Cliff — she never gets anything on Sterling except his own reaction.

**B — Charlie's jeopardy and the casino heist.** pp.15–21 (the summons and the offer), pp.26–28 (the plan), pp.34–37 (the recon), pp.50–51 (the rig), pp.54–56 (assembly), pp.59–61 (the weaponisation). The heist is a **false A-plot**: it is built with full procedural detail — camera placement, eyelines, a shill named Don McClintock, a clicker with a two-signal code — and then never happens. Its actual functions are structural: it supplies proximity (Charlie is in the killers' office all week), a deadline ("forty eight hours," p.28) that competes with grief, a reason she cannot simply leave town, and, at p.59, the only weapon in the episode. It also supplies the pilot's cruellest irony — Sterling recruits his friend's murder-witness as an accomplice roughly ten minutes before ordering the murder.

**C — The Frost dynasty / the pursuit.** Sterling Sr. is present from p.6 ("Should we call your father?"), through a photograph (p.15), a framed picture (p.16), a legend (pp.17–19), a jurisdiction (p.39: FROST COUNTY SHERIFF), a loyalty (p.54: "I worked with your father for twenty five years"), a verdict (p.61: "You stupid fucking child"), and finally a voice (pp.64–65). The son's whole motive — "This is my last chance to prove I'm not what he thinks I am... I'll do whatever it takes" (p.31) — is the C-story acting on the A-story. Natalie dies because a man is trying to impress his father.

**Convergence.** All three collapse into three pages (59–61) through a single object: the voice memo Charlie started on p.50. It was recorded for the B-plot, it is deployed as the A-plot's punishment, and its consequence (Sterling's suicide) ignites the C-plot. One prop resolves two storylines and creates the third — and the pilot's last shot of the casino (p.61: the mark's empty suite, curtains billowing) is simultaneously the heist's failure, the murderer's downfall, and the reason a father will hunt her forever.

## Structural devices

**The Columbo homage, itemised.** Crime first, no whodunit (pp.1–9). A detective the killers cannot take seriously — she is variously called "a regular little Michael Westen" (p.53) and "Encyclopedia Brown" (p.58), the show naming its own genre twice. The apparently harmless, ramble-heavy interrogation that is actually a trap (the elevator ambush, pp.49–50, with the coffee doing the work Columbo's cigar does). The staging error the killer made in front of us (the gun in the wrong hand, p.9 → p.40). The killer improvising a second murder to cover the first (Charlie off the balcony, p.59). And the climactic explanation delivered to the murderer's face rather than to a courtroom (pp.57–58). What is *not* Columbo: nobody is arrested.

**The "bullshit" tell as metronome.** The word lands on pp.12, 13 (×2), 15, 19 (×2), 30, 50, 51 and 57 — roughly once every six pages, always as a scene's turn. Its final use is the most important: on p.57 it is not a diagnosis but a **demand** ("Say 'bullshit' if it isn't true — look me in the eye and say it"), and it goes unanswered. The device is retired at its own climax, which is how the script proves the ability is not the solution.

**The closed-world casino, traversed twice.** The vertical map — 21st-floor executive offices, the 20th-floor presidential suite ("Room twenty ten"), the crow's nest, the casino floor, the employee lockers, the two metal detectors, the parking garage, the stairwell, the second-floor window — is established by Natalie's flight in pp.2–7 and then re-run by Charlie's flight in pp.62–63. The victim surveys the escape route; the detective completes it. The same architecture also produces the clue (the detectors), the murder-suite geography (the ceiling boom-down, p.33), and the killer's death (the balcony).

**The final-page launch.** Four beats in the last two pages, in strict order: the moral debt paid (the email, p.64), the joke that keeps it from being sentimental (OPRAH@OPRAH.COM), the threat verified by the gift (p.65), and the severance (the smashed phone). Then one image: "drives off a long straight road into the middle of America." No promise of a mythology, no teaser, no next-case tag — the premise is a direction of travel.

**Screens as the entire forensic layer.** Charlie never sees a body, a coroner, or a lab. Her evidence is: a phone photo (p.45), a printed crime-scene photo glimpsed on a desk (p.38), a TV news broadcast of security footage (p.48), a video monitor (p.50), a voice memo (p.59), an iPad's deleted-items folder (p.45), and a call log timestamp (p.33). The modern update on Columbo's ash-and-shoe-scuff physicality is that every clue in this pilot is an image someone else recorded.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework

1. **The detective enters her own case at the midpoint (p.32, 49%).** Classic Columbo puts the lieutenant at the crime scene by 20–25%. Poker Face spends the first half of its pilot on a second timeline the format will never run again at that length. This is pilot-only expenditure: the series buys its premise here and will pay it back with 15-minute crime halves thereafter.
2. **The rewind itself is not a Columbo move.** Columbo cuts from murder to arrival. The rewind is heist-film grammar (two parallel lines converging) imported into a howcatchem, and it creates a 22-page stretch with no detection in it at all.
3. **The mystery keeps one card face down.** We never see the laptop screen. In strict inverted-mystery construction, motive is given with the crime; here the *content* of the crime is withheld until pp.45–46 and never actually described. That is whodunit withholding inside a howcatchem — and it is what allows the p.11 podcast plant to feel like theme rather than telegraphing.
4. **The obstacle is power, not knowledge.** Charlie has the killers cold by p.40 and it changes nothing, because the sheriff's office is literally named after them (p.39) and Sterling can call off an investigation with a phone call (p.40). The suspense engine of this howcatchem is not "will she work it out" but "what could she possibly do about it" — a substitution the classic model rarely makes.
5. **No arrest, and no legal ending.** The gotcha (p.58) is legally worthless by the killer's own accurate account (p.58: "this string of 'I think this cause I think this'"). The remedy is invented: she ruins him commercially by leaking the cheat-tape to the mark. The show therefore has to build an extra-legal punishment system in its pilot — "hit 'em where it hurts" — and does so by borrowing the villain's own father's maxim.
6. **The killer dies rather than being taken.** Sterling's suicide (p.61) is not a howcatchem beat; it is a tragedy beat, and it exists to promote the C-story. It also leaves the hand of the murder (Cliff) alive and armed, which is why the pilot ends in a foot chase rather than a confession scene.
7. **The victim is the detective's best friend.** Columbo never knows the dead. The personal stake is what powers the pilot's emotional charter ("Miss Galahad") but is structurally unrepeatable — from 1x02 onward the show must generate the same urgency from a stranger, which is exactly what the pp.11–12 podcast rage is designed to license.
8. **The gift is explained by the antagonist.** The rules scene (pp.19–20) is an interrogation, not exposition: Sterling has already deduced the ability from his father's account and is testing it. The pilot never has to have Charlie explain herself sympathetically.
9. **The heist's machinery is deliberately wasted.** The clicker code ("One click for true, two for bullshit," p.51) is set up with a repeat-back for emphasis and never used. A conventional draft would pay it. Leaving it unfired is what makes the cancellation of the heist land as a reversal rather than an anticlimax.
10. **A minor internal inconsistency.** The news report on p.48 states that Jerry's casino rampage occurred "two days before the slaying." On the script's own clock it is roughly thirty hours: the rampage happens on Charlie's first day (pp.21–23), Natalie sleeps at the trailer that night (pp.23–25), and she is murdered the following evening (pp.29–31 / pp.5–9).

**Draft versus aired episode.** This PDF is an early draft — title page marked "1.1," file metadata dated May 2020, roughly two and a half years before broadcast (Peacock, 26 January 2023, written and directed by Rian Johnson). The architecture is the aired architecture: murder first, unlabelled rewind, the metal-detector gotcha, the balcony, the Denny's email, Sterling Frost Sr.'s closing phone call. Verified differences are cosmetic rather than structural — the whale is **Saaed Caine** here and **Kazimir Caine** on air, and Cliff's surname is styled *Legrand* in the script and *LeGrand* in the credits. Two structural notes that follow from the medium rather than the draft: the script contains no act breaks and no main-title placement (on air, the title sequence occupies the gap the script fills with `CUT TO BLACK / FADE UP ON`), and the 65-page length maps closely onto the aired hour-plus runtime, i.e. this draft was already at length rather than being cut down.

## Lessons for the knowledge base

1. **In an inverted mystery, hide one identity, not one fact.** The crime half plays scrupulously fair on every plot point and conceals exactly one thing: who the woman in the observation room is (p.9). That single concealment converts the entire rewind from recap into revelation and buys 22 pages of forward motion in a story whose ending is already known. If you must cheat in a howcatchem, cheat on *whose eyes we are behind*, never on what happened.

2. **Don't caption the rewind — cue it.** The script explicitly refuses a title card and tells the reader why: "We'll have plenty of time and cues over the next few scenes to recognize her... no need to get it immediately" (p.11). The cues are objects the audience has already been shown in close-up (the hula-girl bobblehead, p.8 → p.12; the Elvis sunglasses; the same metal detectors). Recognition is participation; a caption is instruction, and an audience that decodes your structure is invested in it.

3. **Make the killer's cover-up manufacture the evidence.** Sterling deletes the photo (p.5) and the deletion is precisely what files it in Recently Deleted, where Charlie finds it (p.45). He tells Natalie not to clock out (p.5) and that instruction creates the timeline anomaly that opens the case (p.35). In an already-solved mystery the audience cannot enjoy discovering the crime, so give them the pleasure of watching the criminal's competence become his undoing in real time.

4. **Solve the case with an image the audience has already seen twice, in throwaway staging.** The metal detectors beep for Cliff on p.6 and beep for Natalie on p.12 as pure business; the solution on p.58 is that they *don't* beep for Jerry on the news footage of p.48. A howcatchem clue must be visible, unremarkable and repeated — the audience should feel they could have caught it, which is the genre's whole reward.

5. **Cripple the superpower in its second scene, not its tenth.** The gift is demonstrated on p.12 and shown to be useless on p.13 ("I'm fine." — "Bullshit. But alright."), and the show's hard ceiling — silence defeats her — is staged as the climax of the pilot's biggest confrontation (p.57), where the murderer simply declines to speak. A procedural ability must have its limits dramatised in the pilot's most important scene, or the audience will spend the series wondering why it doesn't just work.

6. **Give a lie detector no truth signal.** The card test (pp.19–20) is built so that two lies get "Bullshit" and the truth gets *silence*. One-directional abilities generate three-act cases automatically: hear the lie, find out why it was told, find the physical proof — because the ability can confirm nothing on its own. Compare the one moment it is indispensable (p.50), where Cliff's honest "No" is what completes the deduction: the most valuable reading in the episode is a truth.

7. **When the audience already knows whodunit, make the obstacle power rather than knowledge.** Charlie has her deduction by p.40 and it is worthless because the county sheriff's office carries the killers' name (p.39). Everything after the midpoint is about leverage, not identification, which is why the climax is a leaked recording rather than a confession — and why the show has to invent a non-legal punishment ("hit 'em where it hurts," pp.47/60/65) before its first episode ends.

8. **The detective may only surprise the audience with information the audience was shown.** The two moments Charlie gets ahead of us are the ATM PIN (p.43, withheld one page) and "Charlie breathes, unlocks her phone" (p.50, withheld nine pages until "But I was this afternoon," p.59). Both were staged on screen with the intention withheld. In an inverted mystery this is the only fair way to spring a reversal — an unseen action would read as a cheat.

9. **Build a false A-plot to buy proximity, a deadline and a weapon.** The heist is fully engineered — eyelines, lamp cameras, a shill, a two-click code (pp.27–28, 37, 50–51) — and never executed. It exists so the detective is inside the killers' office all week, so grief has to compete with a 48-hour clock, and so a recording exists at p.59 that nobody was looking for. Elaborate machinery is worth building if its *cancellation* is the reversal.

10. **A weekly-anthology engine is built from subtractions, not additions.** The last four pages remove the protagonist's employer (p.61), her safety (p.62), her legal recourse (p.64, delegated by email), and her phone (p.65). What remains — an ability requiring no equipment, no money, no allies, no traceability, and a pursuer with unlimited reach but no location — is a machine that must produce a new sealed world every week. Ask of any pilot's last page not what the hero has gained but what has been taken away, and whether the remainder generates episodes on its own.
