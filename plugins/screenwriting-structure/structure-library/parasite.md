**Framework lens(es):** PRIMARY — tonal-shift architecture (where the genre hinge sits, how it is prepared, the register of each block). SECONDARY 1 — McKee five-part structure (inciting incident, progressive complications, crisis, climax, resolution) plus controlling idea. SECONDARY 2 — kishōtenketsu (ki-shō-**ten**-ketsu), tested against a Western three-act reading.

Page citations use the script's **printed** page numbers (top-right of every page from p.2 on); printed page = PDF page − 2. Percentages are of 141 printed pages. This is a translated, scene-numbered shooting/final script issued for awards consideration, so it tracks the release cut almost shot-for-shot; romanisations differ from the subtitles (script: Ki-Tek, Mun-Kwang, Kun-Sae, Yon-Kyo / film: Ki-taek, Moon-gwang, Geun-sae, Yeon-kyo).

## Logline
A jobless family living in a Seoul semi-basement talks its way, one forged credential and one destroyed rival at a time, into every service job in a rich family's hillside mansion — until a fired housekeeper comes back for something she left in the basement, and the con comedy they are living inside turns out to be a horror story with a man in it.

## Structure overview
The script is built as **two films joined at the exact centre**. The first half is a caper: a want, a plan, four progressive infiltrations, each executed with rehearsal scenes, a named music cue ("The Belt of Trust," p.40–49) and a triumphant Steadicam victory lap (pp.56–58). The second half is the same house seen from underneath: a home-invasion thriller that becomes a flood movie that becomes a massacre. The join is a doorbell on **p.71 (50.4%)**.

What makes the two halves read as one film is that the second half is *caused* by the first — Mun-Kwang only rings that bell because the Kims got her fired (pp.44–51) — and that every weapon, object and skill used in the last twenty pages was installed as a joke in the first fifty. The film also keeps a single controlling idea running under both registers: **a vertical order lets the poor rise only by displacing each other, so the war is always fought below the line where the rich can smell it but never see it.** The word that carries the argument is "plan" (pp.2, 13, 73, 88, 106, 107, 111, 139); the sense that carries it is smell (pp.20, 53, 57–59, 101–102, 118, 128).

## The tonal hinge

**Exact page: 71 (50.4%), at the bottom of Scene 70** — the doorbell rings through the mansion in the middle of the drunken living-room bacchanal. The genre is *confirmed* four pages later on **p.75 (53%)**, when the jar cabinet rolls aside and the steel door appears; it is *sealed* on **p.80 (57%)**, when Ki-Tek falls down the stairs and "Ow! Dad, my foot!" is recorded on Mun-Kwang's phone, ending the Kims' control of information for the rest of the film.

The hinge is placed with unusual precision: 71 of 141 pages. Everything before it is executed *by* the Kims; everything after it happens *to* them. The proof is verbal — Ki-Woo says "This wasn't in our plan" on p.73 and "This wasn't part of the plan" on p.88, and by p.106 Ki-Jung is shouting "What's the goddamn plan?" at a father who has none.

**How the turn is prepared.**

1. **The architecture is honest before it is scary.** The storage basement is introduced on p.28 in a throwaway comic beat (Mun-Kwang fetching plum extract for Yon-Kyo's nerves). By the time the audience is asked to believe in a room below the basement, they have already been down one flight and seen the cabinet.
2. **A visible anomaly, unexplained, seen three times.** The motion-sensor lights blink on p.31 and again on p.35 in scenes about something else entirely; the explanation lands on p.91. The script even signposts the deception in its own prose: "The MOTION-SENSOR LIGHTS that we noticed throughout the film weren't motion-sensor lights after all."
3. **A ghost is installed in Act One and left running.** Yon-Kyo yelps when Ki-Jung asks what happened to Da-Song in first grade (p.30); a dark shape sits in every one of his drawings ("same spot, same pattern," p.30). The horror element is not imported at the hinge — it has been on the kitchen wall since 21%.
4. **The best clue is a laugh line.** Dong-Ik, praising the fired housekeeper: "She was a big eater. Ate twice as much as other people. But I suppose she worked twice as hard to make up for that" (p.52). Retroactively this is the entire second half.
5. **Mun-Kwang is the house's incumbent, not its furniture.** She "has been in the house the longest — longer than the family" (p.43); she survived an ownership change; the previous owner recommended her. The Kims' fourth con removes the one person with a claim older than the Parks'.
6. **Tone is graded, not switched.** Dread enters the comedy long before the hinge: "haunting, nerve-scraping music" over the peach-fuzz razor (p.44), a gathering storm on the page Mun-Kwang is fired (p.51), "unsettling music" over the slow-motion urine brawl (p.59–60), and Ki-Tek's rehearsed murder-face on p.70. Conversely, comedy survives well past the hinge — Kun-Sae's nervous laughter, the North Korean newsreader bit (p.83), the japaguri clock (p.87), Ki-Tek rationing tuna (p.136).
7. **The violence is rehearsed as slapstick.** Ki-Woo grabs the viewing stone to attack a drunk and is disarmed (p.59); Chung-Sook's shot-put history is on the wall in scene 5 (p.6); peaches are established as a body-weapon on pp.44–48. Nothing in the massacre is a new capability.

**Why the audience accepts the genre change.** Three reasons, all structural. First, **causality is unbroken**: the intruder is a person the protagonists destroyed in Act Two, so the thriller is the caper's bill, not a new movie. Second, **the new content is thematic before it is generic**: the discovery is not a monster but a poorer family, which is the escalation the satire was already promising — the Kims' semi-basement was the film's first image, so a deeper basement completes an argument rather than betraying it. Third, **the film's authorial signature never changes**: the same long precise setups, the same music-cue-driven sequences, the same deadpan blocking, the same habit of undercutting a big emotion with a small physical joke. The register moves; the voice does not.

## Kishōtenketsu vs three-act pass

**Verdict: kishōtenketsu is the better description of the macro-shape; McKee is the better description of the last forty pages.**

The mid-film turn is a genuine *ten*, not a Western midpoint, for four testable reasons. (i) **It does not escalate the existing goal.** A midpoint reversal raises the price of what the hero already wants; the Kims are not fighting harder for the jobs after p.71 — the objective itself is voided and replaced by containment. (ii) **It is introduced by a character with no prior narrative function in the plan.** Mun-Kwang has been an obstacle *removed*; her return is not the antagonist's counterattack, because there is no antagonist. (iii) **It works by re-description rather than by conflict.** The information delivered on p.75 changes the meaning of everything already seen — who the parasites are, what the lights were, who the ghost was — which is precisely the *ten*'s function: a panel that appears unrelated and forces retrospective reinterpretation. (iv) **The fourth movement reconciles rather than resolves.** Nothing is settled at p.141; the panels are simply revealed to fit — Ki-Tek in the bunker, Ki-Woo on the hill, the plan restated, the house occupied by strangers. A Western resolution answers the dramatic question; this one closes a pattern.

Where the Western model earns its keep is inside the *ten*. From p.95 onward the script runs an entirely orthodox tragic engine: obligatory scene, crisis, climax, resolution, with a protagonist making an irreversible choice under maximum pressure (p.128–129). The most useful reading is therefore **layered**: kishōtenketsu for the film's four-movement architecture and its refusal of a single spine, McKee for the mechanics of the final act it contains. Analysts who force a pure three-act reading are driven to call p.71 "the midpoint," which is technically true and analytically useless — it hides the fact that the second half has a different protagonist, a different genre, a different causal logic and a different question.

One further note: the script has **two inciting incidents**, at p.10 (7%) and p.71 (50%). Each opens a movement with its own want, obstacle and cost. Treating the second as a "reversal" rather than an inciting incident is what makes conventional beat-sheet readings of this film feel wrong.

## Storylines

- **A — The Kim family infiltration (con-caper).** Ki-Woo installed pp.13–19, Ki-Jung pp.21–31, Ki-Tek pp.32–42, Chung-Sook pp.44–56; complete at p.56 and celebrated by the Steadicam. The A-line's engine is not greed but *replacement*: each new job requires the destruction of an incumbent (Min-Hyuk's absence, Yun's dismissal, Mun-Kwang's exile). The line dies at p.80 when the family's identity is recorded on a phone; from there the Kims have no plan, only reactions.
- **B — The Parks (the surface).** Never antagonists, never even opponents. Dong-Ik is generous, prompt, faintly weary; Yon-Kyo is credulous and kind; Da-Hae falls in love; Da-Song is the only Park who perceives anything, and he perceives it with his nose (p.58) and his drawings (p.30). Their function is to be a *permeable membrane* — they employ, they praise, they never look down. Their one recurring assertion is spatial: "the line" (pp.52, 54, 102). They are punished not for cruelty but for a reflex — pinching the nose (pp.118, 128).
- **C — Kun-Sae and Mun-Kwang in the bunker (the buried B-story).** Present in the house from before the Parks arrived (p.78) and running silently under every scene of Act Two: the blinking lights the audience has seen twice (pp.31, 35), the ghost in the drawings (p.30), the housekeeper who "ate twice as much" (p.52). This is a whole storyline told entirely in clues until p.75, then given seventeen pages of foreground (pp.75–91), then killed. Its argument — a couple bankrupted by a Taiwanese Castella franchise, the same one that bankrupted Ki-Tek (pp.33, 79) — makes it the Kims' own future, which is exactly what Ki-Tek inherits on p.135.
- **D — Ki-Woo's stone (the object-line).** Given p.9 ("luck. And money"), venerated p.13, displayed at the family table p.58, seized as a weapon p.59, rescued from the flood and hugged p.108, described as haunting him — "It keeps following me" — p.112, packed p.115, unpacked p.120, carried down the stairs p.123, unusable p.123, turned against him p.124, and finally shown at its origin in a clean stream in the fantasy (p.139). It is the film's only continuous first-person arc and the one storyline that ends where it began.
- **Convergence.** All four lines meet twice. **First on p.80**, in the bunker, when Ki-Tek falls down the stairs: A is exposed to C, B is asleep, D is upstairs on a table. **Second on pp.124–129**, in the garden: D reaches C (the stone in Kun-Sae's hands), C reaches A (the knife in Ki-Jung), A reaches B (the axe in Dong-Ik). The two families that the film is nominally about never once confront each other directly — the entire class war is conducted through proxies from the same class.

## Structural devices

- **Vertical space as act structure.** Every movement change is an altitude change: the semi-basement with its toilet on a plinth (p.1); the uphill walk and the gate atop a stairway (pp.13–14); the "large, oppressive staircase" (p.17); the storage basement (p.28); the bunker beneath it (p.75); Ki-Jung eavesdropping as the camera booms up the stairwell (p.37); Ki-Tek's fall down the stairs (p.80); Chung-Sook's kick down the stairs (p.90); the four-page walk downhill (pp.105–107); the flood (pp.107–110); Ki-Woo's "Not there. Further down" (p.119); and the fantasy's last instruction, "Then all you have to do is walk up the stairs" (p.140). The script never states the metaphor; it stages it thirteen times.
- **The flood as a literal descent.** The same rainstorm is a spoiled camping trip for one family (p.87), an inconvenience solved by "a garden party instead — it was actually a blessing in disguise" (p.117), and a total loss for the other (pp.107–110). Crucially the flood arrives *immediately after* the crawl-out, so the film's longest downward movement runs unbroken from the coffee table to the sewage — one continuous fall from 73% to 78%.
- **Smell as the class detonator.** Six escalating beats, none of which can be answered: a child's armpit gag (p.20); a husband's joke about clothes (p.53); Da-Song's "same smell" (p.58); the family's diagnosis, "it's the basement smell… it won't go away unless we leave this place" (p.59); Dong-Ik's sofa speech, "that smell definitely crosses the line" (pp.101–102); Yon-Kyo's nose-pinch in the car (p.118); and Dong-Ik pinching his nose at Kun-Sae's dying body (p.128), which triggers the axe. Smell is chosen because it is the one class marker the Kims cannot forge — they can fake diplomas, accents, résumés and names, but not this.
- **The scholar's rock (suiseki).** A gift of luck that becomes an object of faith, then a weight, then a weapon turned on its owner. It is also the film's irony index: Ki-Woo calls it "symbolic" on p.9 and is nearly killed by it on p.124, and the fantasy sequence pointedly returns to the moment "the VIEWING STONE was first discovered… from a beautiful, pristine stream" (p.139) — the only image in the film of the object before it entered the economy.
- **The Morse code line.** Planted as character (Cub Scouts, pp.20, 100), demonstrated as a failure (Da-Song timing the blinks and finding no words, p.110), and paid as the film's only successful act of communication between the two halves (Ki-Woo transcribing onto a prescription bag, his flyers and his jeans, pp.133–134). It also converts the p.91 gag — a man head-butting light switches — into a father's voice.
- **Rehearsal as a structural unit.** The script literalises performance: the family scripts and directs Ki-Tek's tuberculosis speech in intercut "flashbacks" (pp.46–47, 49), Ki-Woo shouts "Cut" and "Action," and Ki-Tek later fakes an outburst of murderous rage and asks "How was that, Spielberg?" (p.70). By p.82 he is "doing the method-acting thing, playing 'scary Ki-Tek,' but no one's really buying," and by p.88 his violence is flagged as "not acting." The performance frame is installed so it can be removed.
- **Named cues and camera moves on the page.** "The Belt of Trust" is named on p.40, runs across five scenes and "comes to a dramatic end" on p.49; the infiltration Steadicam is described as a single unbroken shot with a scripted interruption (pp.56–58); the urine brawl is framed through a phone's high-speed app (p.60). The document is directing as it writes — a consequence of it being a post-production script, and a useful model for how sequence-level unity can be specified in prose.
- **The fantasy coda as a two-part device.** Ki-Woo's plan is granted in full and in sunlight (pp.139–140) — college, money, the deed, the father walking out of the basement into the light — and then cancelled by a SMASH CUT to a freezing hillside and one sentence: "I have no idea how to get this letter to you" (p.140). The wish-fulfilment is not a fake-out to be enjoyed; it is evidence, offered so that the final image can price it.

## Deviations and tensions with the framework

- **There is no antagonist.** Dong-Ik never opposes the Kims; he likes them. Kun-Sae is not introduced until 53%, is a victim, and never has a goal beyond staying. The blocking force is a smell and a staircase. Every conventional model that requires an opposing will has to be bent to fit.
- **The obligatory scene never happens.** The confrontation the premise promises — the poor family versus the rich family — is never staged. The Kims and the Parks do not have one scene of open conflict in 141 pages. The collision is displaced onto Kun-Sae and Mun-Kwang, who are the Kims' own class. This is the film's argument expressed as a structural refusal.
- **The protagonist changes hands mid-film.** Ki-Woo drives Act One and Two: he narrates the cons in V.O. (pp.43–45, 52), invents Jessica, plans the removals. At p.89 the script writes him out — "No longer the man with the plan. He just does as he's told" — and Ki-Tek takes the wheel through the climax. Ki-Woo then returns as narrator for the coda (pp.129–141). Two protagonists in relay, handed off exactly where the genre changes.
- **The climax is unplanned and unmotivated by objective.** Ki-Tek has no goal at p.129; he has a trigger. This violates the rule that a climax must answer the protagonist's want, and works anyway because the script spent p.111 arguing that having no plan is the only honest policy — the murder is the thesis being tested on its author.
- **Two inciting incidents, two Act Ones.** p.10 and p.71. The second half's "Act One" (pp.71–83) is compressed into twelve pages because the world it introduces is already built.
- **A very long resolution.** Twelve pages (91–100%), running two separate epistolary movements, a flashback that re-stages the escape, a fantasy sequence, and a final reversal. Conventional structure would call this three endings; the film needs all three because its argument is that nothing changed, and one ending cannot prove a negative.
- **The midpoint is not a midpoint.** Placing a genre hinge at 50% removes the mid-act sag by making the second half a new film, but it costs the script the conventional 75% "all is lost" — which here is distributed across pp.107–112 (flood, death, gym) rather than concentrated in one beat.
- **Draft versus release cut.** As a NEON awards-season document this tracks the finished film closely; the meaningful divergences are small and instructive. The script's final scene (159) ends on the winter hillside with a wide of the mansion neighbourhood, "as if the houses are trying to talk to us," whereas the release cut returns to the semi-basement room for its last image — a change that swaps a social final chord for a personal one. The script also narrates its own reveals in authorial address the film withholds ("That's when we realize -- it's not a toy," p.129; "the MOTION-SENSOR LIGHTS… weren't motion-sensor lights after all," p.91), and its opening scene begins directly on the Wi-Fi hunt rather than on the film's socks-in-the-window crane-down. Read this document as a record of the finished film's structure, not as a window onto its development.

## Lessons for the knowledge base

1. **Put a genre hinge at the mathematical centre and make it the bill for Act Two.** p.71 of 141 is 50.4%, and the person at the door is the person the heroes destroyed on p.51. A tonal shift reads as inevitable when its agent is a consequence, not an arrival. Test for any script attempting this: can you name the page in the first half where the protagonist caused the second half?
2. **Plant the second genre inside the first genre's furniture, not its foreshadowing.** The lights blink twice in scenes about other things (pp.31, 35); the storage basement is introduced fetching plum juice (p.28); the fatal clue is a fond joke about a big eater (p.52). None of these read as ominous on first pass, which is why they all land on second.
3. **Rehearse the climax as comedy.** Ki-Woo swings the stone at a drunk and is disarmed (p.59); Chung-Sook's shot-put photos hang in scene 5 (p.6); peach fuzz is a weapon on p.44. By the massacre the audience has already seen every capability used, so the violence feels like arithmetic rather than escalation.
4. **Give the abstract theme a physical carrier that cannot be argued with.** Class becomes *smell* — unfakeable, unmentionable, and escalatable in six discrete beats (pp.20, 53, 58, 59, 101–102, 118) before it pulls the trigger on p.128. A theme that can be dramatised as a body's involuntary reaction does not need a speech.
5. **Use altitude as the act structure and never say so.** Thirteen staged changes of level, one per movement, from the toilet on a plinth (p.1) to "all you have to do is walk up the stairs" (p.140). If a story is about hierarchy, the blocking can carry the argument, freeing the dialogue to be about ramen.
6. **Reveal information at the point where it costs the most.** Yon-Kyo tells the ghost story on pp.92–95 while the ghost is being taped up twenty feet below, and Da-Song's seizure rule ("fifteen minutes") is planted on p.94 so it can be screamed at Ki-Tek over his bleeding daughter on p.127. Placement, not content, is what makes exposition dramatic.
7. **Install a clock at the hinge to convert revelation into action.** The japaguri call — "Eight minutes, according to navigation" (p.87) — arrives sixteen pages after the doorbell and turns a static blackmail standoff into a farce-suspense sequence. A mid-film genre turn needs a mechanical pressure source or it stalls into explanation.
8. **Change protagonists at the genre change, and say so on the page.** "No longer the man with the plan. He just does as he's told" (p.89). The schemer runs the caper; the father runs the tragedy; the son returns to narrate the elegy. If the second half is a different genre, it may need different competence at its centre.
9. **Deny the obligatory confrontation when the theme requires it.** The Kims and the Parks never fight. Displacing the collision onto Kun-Sae and Mun-Kwang — the same class, one floor down, four years ahead — dramatises the film's controlling idea more efficiently than any confrontation scene could, and explains why the ending feels like diagnosis rather than defeat.
10. **Let the resolution grant the wish before it cancels it.** The fantasy (pp.139–140) plays sincerely and completely — the sunlight, the deed, the father walking out of the basement — and is then billed with one line and one cut. Wish-fulfilment used as evidence is far crueller, and far more moving, than wish-fulfilment withheld.
