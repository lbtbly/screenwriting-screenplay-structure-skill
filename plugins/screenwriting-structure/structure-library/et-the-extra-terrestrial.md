## Logline
A fatherless ten-year-old, ignored by his family and mocked by his brother's friends, finds a stranded alien botanist in the vegetable garden, hides him in a closet, and falls into a bond so total that the two share one nervous system — so that when the creature builds a machine to call his ship home, the boy has to help kill the only thing that ever chose him.

## Structure overview
The script is punctuated by the writer's own **six FADE OUT / FADE IN transitions** — at pp. **7, 24, 45, 68, 87 and 112** — which carve it into seven movements plus a two-page coda. Three of those fades (7, 45, 87) sit at or beside sequence boundaries; three (24, 68, 112) are time-elisions inside a sequence. Onto that macro-punctuation the eight sequences map cleanly, at an unusually even 12–17 pages each.

The critical structural fact: **the film's stated goal — "E.T. phone home" — does not arrive until printed p.62, the exact midpoint (55%).** Everything before it runs on the question *can Elliott keep him?*; everything after runs on *can Elliott give him up?* The midpoint is a want-reversal, not an escalation, and it is the reason the second half of the film feels like a slow amputation.

Act Three is only 12 pages — 11% of the script.

## Eight-sequence pass
Sequence lengths run 12–17 printed pages — remarkably even for a 1981 shooting draft. Boundaries at pp.45 and 87 coincide with the writer's own FADE OUTs.

## Storylines
- **A: Elliott ♥ E.T.** Sight (p.16) → contact (p.21) → concealment (p.24–31) → fusion (p.28, 48A–54, 58–60) → the goal reversal (p.62) → the machine (p.76) → sickness (p.80) → surrender (p.94) → death (p.98) → resurrection (p.100) → release (p.112). The obstacle is never really the government; it is that Elliott wants to *own* what he loves ("I have absolute power," "He belongs to me," "I thought I'd get to keep you forever"). The story cures him of that, and charges him E.T. for the cure.
- **B: The family (Mary, Michael, Gertie).** Michael runs the doubter-to-ally arc: mockery (p.8), scepticism (p.46–48), the "we" catch ("What's this 'we' stuff; you say 'we' all the time now," p.65), the betrayal argument ("And, Elliott, we'll lose you," p.84), the rescue-mission drive (p.103), and the hand on Gertie's shoulder at the ship (p.111). Gertie carries the theme: she teaches E.T. to speak (pp.55–57), delivers the *Peter Pan* answer that governs the resurrection ("I do!" p.66; "I wish," p.98), and carries the geranium up the rocky incline to the ship (p.110–112). Mary is the film's blind spot made human — she is present in nine scenes with E.T. and sees him in one. Her own storyline (a divorced woman who "hates Mexico," up all night with a policeman, screaming "No guns! They're children!") is played almost entirely in the margins of her children's plot.
- **C: KEYS and the scientists.** A ring of keys and a pair of boots (p.5) → the noise that terrifies (pp.6, 16, 18, 44) → the M&M in the soil (p.24) → the surveillance van (p.63) → the shadow and the caught bell (p.76A) → the vinyl house (p.87) → a human face and a promise (pp.87, 93) → hands pounding a dead chest (p.97) → the passenger seat of Mary's car (p.106) → holding everyone back at the landing site so the children can say goodbye (p.110). The antagonist arc is a de-escalation: the film converts him rather than defeating him, and his conversion is staged as a costume removal.
- **D: The D&D boys (frame + posse).** Tyler, Steve, Greg open the film jeering at Elliott (pp.7–9, 47) and close it as his flying escort (pp.105–109) and his players (p.113). They convert the private fantasy of page 8 into a public fact.
- **Convergence:** all four lines fuse at the clean-room in pp.88–100 — Mary's blood taken, Michael's blood pressure, Gertie's hair snipped, KEYS in the suit, Elliott on the gurney, the D&D boys behind the sawhorses outside — and then again in the last two minutes at the landing site, where every character in the movie is standing on the same hillside.

## Structural devices
- **The empathic link as plot mechanism, not metaphor.** Introduced as an unexplained joke (umbrella / grape juice, p.28), escalated into a set-piece (drunk E.T. / biology class, pp.48A–54), used for comedy (the levitating chair, pp.58–60), registered in *grammar* ("What's this 'we' stuff," p.65), diagnosed in dialogue only at p.89 ("Elliott feels his feelings"), weaponised for stakes ("You're killing us," p.92), and finally severed as the proof of death ("None on creature. Boy starting to regain," p.94). Show, use, escalate, name, exploit, break — the full life-cycle of a story device across 66 pages.
- **POV discipline in both directions.** "We cannot see THE CREATURE'S face" (p.4) precedes and licenses "seen only from the waist down… a huge ring of KEYS" (p.5). The alien's face is granted at p.16; the hunter's at p.87. The invasion of the house is staged entirely as shadows, hands and a Geiger counter (pp.76A–76B). Withholding is applied as a *system*, so it never reads as a gimmick.
- **Sound as the transition and the villain.** The keys "displace all other sound" (p.5); E.T. imitates them to frighten Elliott (p.16); the Act One curtain is "the SOUND of KEYS grows louder" (p.44); and the antagonist's conversion is scored by retiring the noise — "The SOUND OF KEYS is replaced with the SOUND OF KEYS' artificial, liquid-oxygen breathing" (p.87). The film's single most famous cut, "The SOUND of KEYS becomes MUSIC. The SOUND of FRIGHTENED ANIMALS becomes BOYS' VOICES" (p.7), is on the page.
- **Planted setups paid at exact distance.** *"I'll be right here"* — Elliott to E.T., installing him in a closet (p.31, 27%) → Elliott to E.T., letting him go on the gurney (p.94, 83%) → E.T. to Elliott, finger to forehead, "with perfect pronunciation" (p.112, 99%). *"Stay" / "Come"* — the only two words Elliott teaches (pp.27–31) become the two options of the ending (p.111). *Peter Pan* — Mary reads the Tinker Bell revival through a wall (p.66) → Gertie says "I wish" over the body (p.98) → "I'm going to believe in you, all my life" (p.100). *Flash Gordon* — "To Be Continued" (p.32) → the spaceman builds a radar machine (p.52) → the COMMUNICATOR (p.76) → the machine still broadcasting in the cut coda (p.113). *The geranium* — arrives droopy (p.41) → blooms (p.45) → yellows (p.80) → dies (p.95) → revives (p.102) → is carried up the incline and taken aboard (pp.110–112).
- **Biological telemetry.** The geranium and the heart-light externalise E.T.'s inner state so that a non-speaking character can have a readable emotional life. Both are established in the first 40% at low stakes and both are used at the climax at maximum stakes.
- **Escalating gag architecture.** The "adults can't see him" rule is run four times at rising risk: Mary walks past him among the stuffed animals (p.48A) → Gertie shows him to Mary in a cowgirl hat and Mary doesn't look (p.55) → Mary kisses him on the nose (p.71) → Mary sees him (p.85). The gag's exhaustion *is* the act break.
- **Frame and reversal.** The camera's opening move is a fake push into space corrected by a crescent moon (p.1); the cut coda's last move is a real pull-out from a bedroom to the solar system and beyond (p.113). The D&D game bookends it. Both bookends survive only in the script.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework
- **The prologue is a ninth sequence pretending to be half of the first.** Pages 1–7 are a self-contained mini-film with its own question, crisis and answer, closed by the script's first FADE OUT — but at 6% it is too short to be a sequence in the Daniel/Gulino sense, and it is welded to what follows by an audio match rather than a cut. Treating pp.1–16 as one two-movement Sequence 1 keeps the eight-sequence grid honest at the cost of admitting that the sequence contains two separate dramatic questions. Both readings are defensible; the script itself votes for the prologue by fading out on it.
- **The stated goal arrives at the midpoint, not at the end of Act One.** By orthodox Field, "E.T. phone home" (p.62) should be Plot Point I. Instead the first half runs on a possessive question (*can I keep him?*) and the midpoint replaces it with its opposite. This is why Act One feels like a different, gentler film, and why the second half is relentlessly downward.
- **Act Three is 12 pages (11%).** The classical proportion would be 25%. Everything from the death onward is compressed into a single unbroken run, and the "all is lost" descent (pp.80–98) does the work a longer third act would normally do. The result is a film whose last twenty minutes feel like free-fall — but it also means Plot Point II lands at 87%, extremely late.
- **The protagonist is unconscious or immobilised for the entire penultimate sequence.** Elliott is strapped to a gurney from p.89 to p.100 and cannot act. The sequence method assumes a protagonist pursuing an objective through obstacles; here the objective is pursued by *nobody*, and the sequence resolves on a speech of renunciation. It works because the film has spent 60 pages making Elliott's *feeling* the plot's causal engine — but it is not a shape the method predicts.
- **The antagonist is defeated by being humanised, not opposed.** KEYS never loses a confrontation; he takes off a helmet (p.87), makes a promise (p.93), and gets into the mother's passenger seat (p.106). The Save the Cat "bad guys close in" beat is present, but the "bad guys" turn out to be the same species of believer as the hero — which strips the climax of an opponent and forces the film to generate its final tension from *distance and time* (can they reach the ship?) rather than conflict.
- **The two-stage climax is the script's structural signature, not a flaw.** Almost every sequence peaks twice — terror then trust (p.16 / p.21), miracle then price (p.74 / p.77), death then resurrection (p.98 / p.100), flight then farewell (p.109 / p.112). Sequence theory expects one climax per unit; this script consistently pairs a physical peak with an emotional one a few pages later, and the emotional one is always the real curtain.
- **The draft's ending is not the film's ending.** The script closes on a coda (pp.112–113): a second D&D game, the Halloween Polaroid on the wall, the clay planets on wires, the COMMUNICATOR still transmitting from the roof, Elliott's "Hey, man, I talk goblin," and a camera move out to deep space. It resolves the frame, the mockery, the boy's isolation and the Flash Gordon "To Be Continued" — and Spielberg cut all of it, ending instead on the departure. The cut trades formal completeness for emotional finality. It is the clearest available case study in the KB of a structurally *correct* ending being sacrificed for a better one.
- **Minor draft-versus-film differences.** KEYS' film line "I've been wishing for this since I was ten years old" is not in this draft; his equivalent speech is the clean-room appeal at p.93. There is no rainbow at the departure — the ship simply becomes "the first star of the evening" (p.112). The title page still reads *A Boy's Life*.

## Lessons for the knowledge base
1. **A sequence that fails is a better engine than one that succeeds.** Sequence 1A (pp.1–7) asks "can he get back to the ship?" and answers *no*. The unanswered need created by that failure powers 106 pages. When a first sequence resolves positively, the writer has to import a new problem; when it resolves negatively, the problem is already load-bearing.
2. **Introduce a story device as a joke, name it sixty pages later.** The empathic link appears as a gag (umbrella → grape juice, p.28), is exploited for comedy (pp.48A–60), becomes the source of stakes (p.92), and is only *explained* by a doctor at p.89. Audiences learn rules faster from demonstration than from dialogue; save the explanation for the moment the rule turns lethal.
3. **The midpoint can reverse the protagonist's want rather than raise the stakes.** "E.T. phone home" (p.62, 55%) converts Elliott's goal from *keep* to *release*. Every subsequent scene in which he helps is a scene in which he loses — which is how a film with no villain sustains 45% of its running time on rising pain.
4. **Externalise a non-speaking character's inner life in a prop with a visible state.** The geranium (planted p.41, blooms p.45, yellows p.80, dies p.95, revives p.102, leaves Earth p.112) lets the script report E.T.'s condition in cutaways, from other rooms, to characters who cannot ask him. Any protagonist who cannot narrate their own state — an animal, a child, a machine, a coma patient — needs one.
5. **Plant the resurrection's rule in another room, in someone else's scene.** Mary reads the Tinker Bell revival aloud through a wall at p.66 while E.T. heals a cut finger; Gertie says "I wish" over the corpse at p.98; Elliott says "I'm going to believe in you, all my life" at p.100. The rule that will overturn death is established 32 pages early, by a character who does not know the alien exists, in a scene whose ostensible business is bedtime. That is how you make a resurrection feel legislated rather than cheated.
6. **Spend the magic on pleasure first so you can spend it on necessity later.** The bicycle flies at p.74 for fun; it flies at p.109 to save a life. A capability shown once as delight and once as rescue reads as character; shown only at the climax it reads as a rabbit from a hat.
7. **Turn off the magic before the hero has to win.** The link severs at p.94 and E.T. dies at p.98: Elliott spends the turn into Act Three with no powers, no ally and no plan, and wins the act by *lying* (the shirt over the heart-light, the whisper in Michael's ear). The magical-visitor form demands that the last decisive act be the hero's own.
8. **Give the antagonist a single sensory signature and retire it at the conversion.** KEYS is a sound and a pair of boots for 82 pages; his face and his humanity arrive in the same paragraph as the line "The SOUND OF KEYS is replaced with the SOUND OF KEYS' artificial, liquid-oxygen breathing" (p.87). The audience's fear is disarmed at the level of the ear before it is disarmed at the level of plot.
9. **Let the hero's flaw be the thing the antagonist overhears.** KEYS locks onto Michael quoting Elliott — "You have absolute power, remember? 'I found him. He belongs to me'" (p.64). The pursuit is literally summoned by the possessiveness the story exists to cure, so the external and internal plots share one cause.
10. **The most emotionally correct ending may be structurally incomplete — take it anyway.** This draft's coda (pp.112–113) closes the D&D frame, the goblin taunt, the "To Be Continued" comic and the opening camera move. The film cuts every one of them and ends on departure. Formal symmetry is a means; the last feeling is the end. Know which of your bookends you can afford to lose.
