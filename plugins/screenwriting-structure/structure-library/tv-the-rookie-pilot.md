## Logline
On the day his divorce is final, a 43-year-old small-town Pennsylvanian survives a bank robbery by talking his way through it, moves to Los Angeles and becomes the LAPD's oldest rookie — and across his first two days on patrol, under a training officer who won't call him her partner and a watch commander openly trying to break him, he saves a child, gets a fellow officer shot, and has to decide whether the uniform is a midlife crisis or a calling.

## Structure overview

This is the knowledge base's canonical **network** exemplar, and its most useful property is that its act grid is wildly *unequal*. Act One runs 21 pages — 35% of the script, more than twice the length of Acts Two and Three — because it is doing the work of a teaser, a premise pilot and a first act simultaneously. The remaining four acts get progressively longer as the plot takes over from the setup: 8, 8, 12, 11.

The first five pages function as an **unlabelled teaser**: a self-contained Pennsylvania bank robbery that ends on a decision ("How do you think I'd look in uniform?"), a music cue and a `Six Months Later` chyron. Hawley doesn't mark it `TEASER / END OF TEASER`; he lets a song and a hard geographic cut do the job a printed act break would do. Everything after p.5 is Los Angeles, and the episode is then structured as **two consecutive shifts**: Day One (pp.5–37, ending at night with the sex reveal) and Day Two (pp.38–58, ending at night with a song montage), plus a Day Three morning coda (pp.58–60) that resets the series to its running position.

Each act opens on a different tactic. Act Two opens on the *aftermath* of the Act One out (same location, minutes later). Act Three opens on the *continuation* of the Act Two out (the search). Act Four opens on a *time jump* to the next morning. Act Five opens on the *same gunfight* the previous act ended in, from a helicopter. Only one of the four acts is allowed to resolve its cliffhanger immediately; the other three make the audience carry a question across the break.

Structural asymmetry worth naming: **the first commercial break does not arrive until 35% of the script.** After that the breaks come at 48%, 62% and 82% — i.e. once the machine is running, the acts land almost exactly on the classic feature turn positions (midpoint, 60%, break into three). Act One buys that late first break by front-loading everything the series needs forever.

Is this Nolan's fault? | **No cutaway at all.** Act Five opens with a helicopter shot craning over the houses back into the same gunfight; the physical question is answered over pages 50–51 and Bradford's survival is withheld until p.58. Structurally this is the only act-out of the four that runs on pure suspense, and it is placed at the last break — where a network hour needs the audience to sit through the longest commercial pod |
| `END OF EPISODE` | 60 | 100% | **Series-engine button** — an unrelated new crime read aloud in roll call | What's next week's case? | (No next act.) The final beat is the format itself: the show announces that it renews by dispatch |

Craft pattern across the four printed act-outs: **jeopardy → jeopardy → revelation → jeopardy**, with the one non-physical button placed at the exact centre of the back half. Two of the four are delivered by camera move or sound rather than by dialogue (the crane-up on p.29, the open radio channel on p.49). Three of the four end on Nolan's *responsibility* rather than his danger — the pilot's cliffhangers are moral even when they are loud.

## Storylines

Measured by dominant-track scene count across 86 scenes and by line-volume converted to page equivalents:

- **A — Nolan & Officer Talia Bishop (≈47 of 86 scenes; ≈34 pp; ~56% of line volume; pp.1–60).** ~22 major beats. Structure: teaser origin (1–5) → assignment and contract (10–13) → case #1 and its fatal consequence (15–21) → over-reach and rescue (26–31) → private life (32–37) → order disobeyed (40–47) → catastrophe, blame, redemption (49–58) → re-entry (58–60). Bishop's own arc (professional distance → her son → "there's plenty of blame to go around") runs entirely inside this track.
- **B — Lucy Benitez & Officer Tim Bradford (≈17 scenes; ≈12 pp; ~21%; pp.5–58).** ~10 beats: the pre-shift arrest (5–7), the Spanish trap (18–20), Ghost Head (23–24), the foot chase (28), the auto shop (35–36), Aimee (43–45), the ambush and Bradford's shooting (48–51), the hospital (51–53), the encampment (58). The track's engine is "everything is a test"; its serialized payload is Bradford's wife.
- **C — Jackson West & Officer Angela Song (≈7 scenes; ≈4 pp; ~6.5%; pp.5–58).** 6 beats only: the graduation (5), Zoe in the hallway (8), the Tar Pits taser (14), the Porsche ticket / leverage play (42–43), freezing under fire (51), Song's riot act (53–54), the bathroom stall (55) and "I'm scared" (58). The thinnest track by far — and the one with the cleanest single arc, precisely because it has no room for anything else.
- **Ensemble scenes (≈15 scenes; ≈10 pp; ~17%).** Roll call ×3 (9–10, 39–40, 59–60), the food-truck table (24–26), the grid search (30), the hospital waiting area, the A'Ja raid. These are the load-bearing scenes: every track is refreshed in the same room and the audience re-orients.
- **Cross-track spine — Nolan vs Sgt. Grey (pp.6, 7, 9–11, 23, 31–32, 39–40, 52–55, 58–60).** ~10 beats. The only thread that appears in all five acts and both bookends the hour.
- **Serialized secrets parked for the series:** Nolan–Lucy (37, 38–39, 40–41 — she can't comfort him in front of Bradford); Nolan–Zoe chemistry (8–9, 36–37, 54–55); Bishop's son Jacob (22, 41–42, 58); Aimee (43–45, 58); Jackson's fear and his father's leverage (14, 42–43, 51, 53–55, 58).

Effective A-track dominance is higher than 56%: Nolan is present and usually driving in most of the ensemble scenes, so he carries roughly two-thirds of the hour, while the other two rookies are each given exactly one memorable set piece per act-block.

## Series engine

**Case-of-the-week machinery.** The pilot demonstrates four distinct ways to start a story and runs five cases in sixty pages:

1. **Dispatch pushes it.** "7-Adam-15 possible battery in progress, 1401 South Sycamore" (p.15). The radio is the show's inciting-incident generator: it can interrupt any character scene at any moment (it interrupts a burrito on p.26, a confession on p.45), which means no scene in the series ever needs a manufactured exit.
2. **The rookie pulls it.** Nolan "buys" the Hollywood call over Bishop's objection (p.26) — establishing that the protagonist can seize a case and be punished for it.
3. **Roll call issues it.** The Selby BOLO (p.40) — the only case with a name, a face and a flyer, and therefore the only one that can carry an act structure.
4. **The street throws it.** The lawnmower TC (p.18) and the 7-11 panhandlers (p.43) — small incidents whose only purpose is to trigger a character reveal. Crucially, neither is *solved*; they exist so a T.O. can be exposed.

Cases per hour: five (domestic → Ghost Head → Unicorn Man → the TC/panhandler pair → Selby). Only one of them (Selby) runs across acts; the rest are one-to-three pages. That ratio — **many disposable calls, one spine case** — is the show's weekly recipe, demonstrated rather than described.

**The training-officer device is the real engine.** Three permanent two-handers, each mismatched on a different axis, all created on p.10:

| Pair | Built-in friction | Renewable every week because… |
|---|---|---|
| Nolan / Bishop | Oldest rookie under the youngest T.O.; "We're not partners. I'm your T.O." (p.12) | The evaluation is written *daily* for six months (p.31) — a scorecard the series can run every episode |
| Lucy / Bradford | "Everything is a test… there's no way to know" (p.19) | The rules are deliberately unknowable, so any interaction can be a graded confrontation |
| Jackson / Song | The legacy and the careerist: Song needs him to be "the kind of layup I need to make detective-trainee" (p.10), his father sits on her promotion list (p.42) | Both parties have something to lose from the other's failure |

Add the two structural authorities — Sgt. Grey (who wants the protagonist gone, p.11, and says so again at p.59) and Capt. Zoe (who outranks him and likes him, pp.36–37, 55) — and the show has a conflict source in every scene without needing a criminal in the room.

**Serialized spine.** Nolan's twelve-month probation is the season clock ("in thirteen months, you'll be a P2," p.39). Grey's hazing contract (p.59) is the season antagonist. Four private arcs are opened and left running (Bishop's son, Bradford's wife, Jackson's fear, the secret relationship). Nothing is resolved that would cost the series an engine — including the antagonist, who is explicitly *not* won over.

## Structural devices

- **The "oldest rookie" premise as built-in conflict.** The concept generates opposition for free: Grey's hatred (p.11), Bishop's assignment as punishment (p.10), the "bring your dad to work day" mockery (p.9), the brochure prank (Beauty School p.32, Nursing School p.39), "Officer Dumptruck" (p.39), and Lucy's counter-argument that his disadvantage is actually privilege (p.39). It also generates the *solution* — twenty years of marriage and one child produce the empathy that defuses Carlo (p.16), the counsel that opens Bishop (p.42), and the recognition of an infant inhaler that breaks the case (p.54). A premise that supplies both the obstacle and the superpower is the most economical structure a series can have.
- **Roll call as an act-opening machine.** Three roll calls at pp.9–10, 39–40 and 59–60 — one per day of story, each doing four jobs at once: exposition, comedy at the protagonist's expense, the issuing of a case, and a status reading on Nolan's standing. Roll call #1 assigns the T.O.s (the engine). Roll call #2 humiliates him and hands out the BOLO (the plot). Roll call #3 grants him grudging respect and dispatches next week's crime (the format). Note that the **shooting range does not appear in this draft** — it exists only as a line of backstory in Jackson's mouth ("Ride alongs instead of sports. Shooting range instead of music," p.35). The ritual furniture Hawley actually builds is roll call, the kit room, the shop check, the daily evaluation, and the word "Boot."
- **The three-way cross-cut, established in camera.** On p.13 a single continuous parking-lot move goes Bishop/Nolan → "we FOCUS PAST HIM" to Bradford/Lucy → "PAST THEM: We find Song and Jackson" → back to "we STAY WITH THEM." That one paragraph teaches the audience the show's grammar; thereafter the script can cut between the three cars without any orienting beat (pp.14, 18, 30, 42–45, 47–51). The device is re-used at scale twice: the food-truck table (pp.24–26, all six in one location, rookies lying at one table and T.O.s telling the truth at another) and the closing song montage (p.58, four tracks resolved in twelve lines).
- **Rhythmic track rotation.** Once a track has had a scene, the script leaves it alone for four to eight pages. Nolan is never off screen for more than about three pages; Lucy returns every 4–6; Jackson every 8–12. The result is that the A-story never has to share a beat, but the audience is never allowed to forget the other two.
- **Plants harvested as punishment, not reward.** The fence gag (p.28) returns as body-cam footage in roll call (p.39). "That we'll be back" (p.18) returns as a re-respond (p.20). The unicorn delusion (p.27) becomes the clue that locates the car (p.31). The "I found my bad guy" text (p.47) becomes "Officer down" (p.49). Every setup in this pilot pays off as a consequence for the hero.
- **Sound and camera as act punctuation.** Two of the four act-outs are non-verbal: the crane-up over Hollywood (p.29) and the gun battle heard on an open radio channel (p.49). Two of the five acts open on aerial shots (pp.30, 50). The script uses PRE-LAP transitions as track-switches — "PRE-LAP FIGHTING" into the Tar Pits (p.14), Bradford's voice arriving over Bishop's car (p.18), a dispatch call bridging into the 7-11 (p.43).
- **Twin-day symmetry.** Day One ends in a bedroom reveal (p.37); Day Two ends in a montage of four bedsides and one parking lot (p.58). Two roll calls open the two shifts; two locker-room scenes (p.32, p.58) bookend them, the first with Nolan collapsed in front of his locker and the second with him standing before his uniform deciding whether to put it back on.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework

- **No printed teaser, and a 21-page Act One.** Standard network practice would label pp.1–5 `TEASER` and hold Act One to 10–13 pages. Hawley instead runs 35% of the script before the first commercial break. It works only because the teaser material is genuinely self-contained and because pp.5–21 are relentlessly incident-driven (six locations, nine character introductions, two cases). Any pilot copying this structure without that density will die in the first block.
- **The act grid is asymmetric by design, and the asymmetry inverts the usual shape.** 21 / 8 / 12 / 11 pages, with the two shortest acts (Two and Three, 8 pp each) sitting either side of the midpoint. Most network hours run their acts within a page or two of each other. Here the pilot spends its length where a *pilot* needs it (setup) and lets the middle acts run lean because the machinery is already installed.
- **The Act One out resolves immediately.** The most conventional network mistake — a cliffhanger the next act answers in its first line — is committed deliberately at pp.21–22. Hawley compensates by opening Act Two on new serialized information (Bishop's son) rather than on the resolution, so the act still starts with a question.
- **The Act Three out has no plot stakes at all.** A five-line sex reveal is asked to hold a commercial break at the 62% mark. It survives because it is a *misdirect* (Captain Zoe has been set up eight lines earlier) and because the consequence is institutional rather than romantic — a rookie sleeping with a fellow rookie is a fireable offence in the world the script has just built.
- **The protagonist doesn't win the climax.** Nolan disarms himself and is used as bait; Song fires the shot, Grey and Bishop finish it (p.58). Network convention would let the hero make the arrest. Hawley trades the conventional beat for the more truthful one — the rookie's contribution is nerve, not marksmanship — and gets the theme back in exchange.
- **The C-track is starved.** Jackson and Song get roughly four pages and six beats in sixty pages. The arc is coherent (fear → denial → leverage → freeze → confrontation → confession) but it is carried almost entirely by two lines: "You're not ready to be a cop… until you can admit you're scared" (p.54) and "I'm scared" (p.58). This is the honest cost of three-hander pilot structure: the third track survives on compression, and only works because its flaw is legible in a single image (p.14).
- **Draft artifacts that survived into the network draft.** Nolan is "43" on p.1 but "the forty year old rookie" on p.10. Bradford and Lucy's unit is "7-Adam-21" on p.18 and "7-Adam-19" from p.43 onward. Song radios for "Officer Jackson" on p.55 (his surname is West). The Act Five climax flips time of day inside continuous action — `EXT. 24TH STREET - MORNING` (p.51) and `INT. HOSPITAL. EMERGENCY ROOM - NIGHT` (p.51) and `INT. A'JA'S HOUSE. LIVING ROOM - DAY` against `INT. A'JA'S HOUSE. BACK YARD - NIGHT` (pp.55–57). `UTLITY CLOSET` is misspelled on p.7. Useful evidence for the KB that a shooting-adjacent network draft is not a clean document — the act architecture is load-bearing, the slug lines are not.
- **A "rookie" show with no training montage.** Six months of academy are elided by a chyron (p.5). Everything the audience needs to know about competence is dramatized on the street on the first day. The pilot's real subject is not learning to be a cop; it is whether a man is allowed to start over.

## Lessons for the knowledge base

1. **In a network pilot, spend the length where the series needs it — the act grid does not have to be even.** The Rookie runs 21 / 8 / 8 / 12 / 11 pages. Act One is 35% of the script because it must install nine regulars, three permanent partnerships, an antagonist and an engine; the middle acts can run at eight pages because that work is done. Plan act lengths against setup debt, not against a template.
2. **A teaser is a function, not a label.** Pages 1–5 are a self-contained short film with its own inciting incident and its own button, and they are never labelled `TEASER`. What actually separates them from Act One is a music cue, a location change and a time-jump chyron. If the transition is strong enough, the printed break is optional.
3. **Build the engine on one page and it will run forever.** The T.O. match game (p.10) creates three mismatched two-handers in twelve lines — mismatched on age, on method, on ambition. Every subsequent scene in the series inherits conflict from that page without further setup. Look for the page in your pilot that does this; if there isn't one, the show is a closed-ended story.
4. **Rotate act-out types, and put the non-physical button in the middle of the back half.** The four printed act-outs run jeopardy (p.21) → jeopardy (p.29) → revelation (p.37) → jeopardy (p.49). The one button with no gunfire is a five-line reveal at 62%, precisely where an audience that has just watched a child rescued needs a different kind of hook. Four identical cliffhangers exhaust an audience; four different ones train them to stay.
5. **Engineer at least one act-out to be delivered by camera or sound rather than dialogue.** The crane-up over "thousands of cars" (p.29) turns a threat into an impossible task without a word; the open radio channel (p.49) delivers a firefight to the man responsible for it as pure audio. Buttons that are *images* survive a commercial break better than buttons that are lines, because the audience carries a picture, not a sentence.
6. **You may resolve an act-out immediately — if you replace it with new material in the first thirty seconds.** Act Two opens on the same crime scene the previous act ended in (p.22) and defuses the guilt in six lines. It pays for that by immediately opening a brand-new serialized thread (Bishop's phone call about her son) inside the same page. An act that answers its own question must ask a different one before it goes anywhere else.
7. **Make the protagonist's disqualifying trait the thing that solves the story.** Nolan's age is the source of every obstacle in the hour — and it is why he can read a fighter making weight (p.16), counsel an exhausted mother (p.42) and recognize an infant inhaler (p.54). A premise that generates both the opposition and the solution is self-financing.
8. **Prove the weekly engine by running it several different ways in one hour.** Five cases in sixty pages, started four different ways: dispatched (p.15), poached (p.26), issued at roll call (p.40), stumbled into (p.18, p.43). The two "stumbled into" cases are never solved — they exist purely to expose a T.O. A pilot that demonstrates multiple story-generation methods is demonstrating a series, not an episode.
9. **Don't resolve the antagonist; re-contract him.** Grey's closing speech (p.59) restates his opposition in stronger, more principled terms — "I will haze, harass and humiliate you every chance I get" — and Nolan accepts the terms. Season-long opposition is preserved and the relationship still moved, because a *stated* contract is a change of state even when nothing is forgiven.
10. **Close a multi-protagonist pilot with a simultaneity device, and close the episode on a dispatch.** Twelve lines of song montage (p.58) resolve four serialized threads at once — three of them without dialogue — and then the last spoken line of the pilot is next week's crime ("Description matches the crew we've been hunting. So hunt harder…"). The ending is not "the story is over"; it is "the machine is on."
