## Logline
On the day of his eightieth birthday, a media emperor who has spent eighteen months signalling that he will hand the company to his eldest working son instead announces he is staying — and asks his four children to sign away their power to his third wife by four o'clock — setting off a day of separate, secret negotiations that ends with the son winning the deal of his life at the exact minute his father's brain gives out.

## Structure overview

The pilot is built as **six movements with no printed punctuation between them**. Every turn is marked by three things at once: a change of location, a change of who is winning, and the release of a piece of withheld information. The architecture is a **funnel**: four cities and four separate storylines in the first quarter, one apartment for the middle third, one field and two helicopters at the end. As the physical space contracts, the number of simultaneous negotiations rises.

The single most important structural decision is the **placement of the central turn at 58–60%**. The trust announcement and "I'm going to give it a couple of years — I'll stay in situ" (pp.42–43) sit where a four-act broadcast hour would place its *third* act-out. The first 40% is almost pure installation. That is affordable only because every installation scene is also a transaction: the induction video is a humiliation (pp.5–6), the phone call home is a power test (pp.10–13), the birthday gift is a status bid (pp.36–38).

**The ordering logic.** The pilot introduces its principals **by distance from the deal, not by rank in the family**: Logan alone in a dark room (p.1) → Kendall in the room where the money is being spent (p.2) → Shiv on another continent, working for somebody else (p.3) → Greg at the bottom of the company's own org chart, inside its mascot (p.5) → Roman crashing a room he no longer belongs in (p.19) → Connor already at the party, installed like furniture (p.28). Three consequences follow:

1. **Birth order is inverted.** The eldest child arrives last, at 39%; the second son opens the episode. The introduction sequence pre-argues the series' claim that primogeniture does not govern here — before a single character says so.
2. **Every entrance is a status statement.** Roman is preceded by a prank (the business alchemist, p.19); Greg is preceded by a corporate video in which his cousin's face is projected on a wall (p.6); Connor is not preceded by anything at all.
3. **The outsiders arrive before the insiders.** Lawrence (p.2), Alessandro (p.2) and Frank (p.2) are installed before three of the four heirs — so that when the family finally assembles at p.29 we already know what is being fought over and who else wants it. The party is the *second* movement, not the first, which is why it can function as a pressure chamber rather than an exposition scene.

**Why this becomes the series' repeatable engine.** Three properties make these reversals renewable rather than consumable. **(1) Every flip has a price, and the price is always charged to the winner, later.** Logan wins the signature on p.24 and pays for it with a son who briefs against him on p.64. He wins the auction on pp.56–59 by promising two jobs he cannot both deliver. A flip without a price generates plot; a flip with a deferred price generates seasons. **(2) Nothing is ever made final.** The announcement is deferred ("pending events," p.44), the trust papers are never signed (p.72), and the CEO role is left in "a degree of uncertainty" (p.72) — so every settlement is reopenable. **(3) Any two characters in a room have something to trade.** The pilot runs more than a dozen distinct negotiations — father/son, father/daughter, husband/wife, cousin/in-law, employer/retainer, billionaire/groundsman — which means the show never needs an external plot to generate a scene.

**On the unsettled protagonist.** Succession's pilot refuses the standard hour-drama contract — one POV, one want, one obstacle — and the refusal is structural, not coy. Four separate characters are given a full protagonist geometry inside the same 72 pages: Logan opens the episode alone in his own POV (p.1) and closes it on a ventilator (p.72); Kendall has the most screen time, the clearest want, the public defeat and the active reversal (pp.2–9, 22–24, 46–49, 65–66, 70–71); Greg has the most conventional structure of all — an inciting incident (p.15), a journey, three escalating attempts at his goal (pp.40, 54, 60) and a failure (p.71); and the sibling group functions as a collective protagonist for two extended sequences (pp.42–45, 50–52, 68–69).

What makes this hold together is that the pilot substitutes a **question** for a protagonist and then services the question with absolute discipline. "When does the old man let go?" is asked by a stranger at 13% (p.9), formalised into a legal instrument at 58% (p.42), answered *no* at 60% (p.43), tested by five separate private negotiations between 74% and 92% (pp.53–66), and made permanently unanswerable at 96% (p.69). Every scene in the episode either advances or complicates that one question. The audience is never confused about what the show is about — only about who it is about, which is precisely the anxiety the characters share. The technique is transferable: **an ensemble pilot may defer the protagonist for as long as it likes, provided it never defers the question, and provided each candidate is given at least one scene in which they are unambiguously the POV.**

## Storylines

- **A — The succession / the trust (pp.10–13, 22–24, 42–45, 50–52, 56–59, 68–69, 72).** Logan's day of buying his own children. Runs on a single document that is signed once by the wrong person (p.24), refused by three others (p.69), and left unsigned at the end (p.72). This is the only storyline that is *less* resolved at the end of the hour than at the start.
- **B — The Rapid News merger (pp.2–3, 7–9, 15–16, 18–21, 25–26, 31–34, 62, 65–66, 70–71).** Kendall's day. Structurally unusual: it *resolves*, and resolves as a **win** (p.70). Its function is not suspense but exchange-rate demonstration — it exists to be won at the precise moment winning stops mattering.
- **C — Greg's infiltration (pp.5–6, 14–15, 17–18, 22, 25, 26–29, 35–36, 40–41, 53–54, 60–62, 71).** The pilot's picaresque and its audience surrogate: the one character who has to be told what the company is. Three escalating attempts on Logan (pp.40, 54) and one on Tom (pp.60–62). Ends with nothing — a crummy hotel (p.71).
- **D — Tom & Shiv (pp.3–5, 22, 25, 28, 36–37, 58–60, 71).** A marriage conducted entirely as a career negotiation, from the gift panic (p.4) to Shiv trading Tom's promotion for her own position (pp.58–59) to Tom's joke about the watch (p.60).
- **E — Frank (pp.2, 8–9, 34–35, 64–65, 66–68, 71).** The pilot's only complete arc, and its cleanest tragedy: he gives the strategic advice (p.35), delivers the loving toast (pp.64–65), is executed in the air (pp.66–68), and is last seen drinking alone at the empty softball diamond (p.71).
- **F — Pablo & Tolly (pp.58, 62–64, 68, 72).** The world outside the family, present for exactly three scenes, given the pilot's final image. Structurally a moral chorus with no lines of commentary.

## Series engine

**The problem:** a show named *Succession* whose succession must never happen. The pilot solves this on the page, and the solution is visible in five mechanisms.

1. **The prize is withheld rather than contested.** Logan does not choose badly; he refuses to choose ("I don't know - five?… Ten?… Twenty," p.48). A contested prize is exhausted the moment it is awarded; a withheld prize can be dangled indefinitely, and every dangle is a scene.
2. **The children are priced individually, so they can never combine.** Logan works Roman in one helicopter (pp.56–58) and Shiv in another location minutes later (pp.58–59), which is why Kendall's "power of three" collapses in ten seconds at p.60. The bloc is structurally impossible: each sibling has already been given a private reason to defect. This is the renewable core — every season can restage p.60.
3. **Every offer is revocable, so nothing becomes fact.** "We can announce to the board you're in pole position. **Pending events**" (p.44). Frank is promoted into "light duties" (p.67). The papers are never signed (p.72). No state in this world is terminal, which means no plot development is expenditure.
4. **The corporate plot is renewable fuel with a fixed function.** The Rapid News merger occupies a third of the pilot and its outcome changes nothing about the family — its real job is to be a fresh referendum on the same question ("can the son do it without the father?"). Any deal, any season, does that job. This is why the deal-of-the-season structure works for years without repeating itself emotionally.
5. **The stroke converts a decision into a condition.** By removing the decider without settling the decision (p.69), the pilot creates the show's permanent state: an unanswerable question with a *live vote* attached. The last thing we are told about the trust is that the envelopes are "unsigned" (p.72). Logan can now recover partially, relapse, threaten, reverse — and each of those is a full season's premise, none of which resolves anything.

The pilot also installs the show's two inexhaustible secondary generators: the **understory** (Greg and Tom, pp.60–62 — a comic hierarchy running underneath the tragic one, immune to the stroke) and the **spousal claim** (Marcia at pp.42 and 52; Tom at pp.58–60 — outsiders whose claims are pure and therefore never disqualified by blood).

## Structural devices

**1. The opening disorientation (pp.1–2).** Black, a POV, a wall, and three "where am I" questions before the audience knows whose story this is. Three things happen simultaneously: the audience is placed in the character's condition; the most powerful man in the story is introduced as a *body* before he is introduced as a *power*; and the show's subject is stated as a physical fact — a man who has lost the map of his own house is about to spend the day refusing to give up the map. The withheld identity is also an ensemble tool: for two pages the pilot is unclaimed, so no one is yet the protagonist.

**2. Time-and-place cards, used for four beats and then abandoned (pp.2–5).** NEW YORK 3.12 AM / NEW YORK 4 AM / LONDON 10 AM / LONDON 10.15 AM. They do the work of a globe-spanning empire in four lines — and then stop permanently, the moment the story acquires a single location. A disposable device, used at exactly the length of its usefulness.

**3. The induction video as exposition (pp.5–6).** Discussed above; worth isolating as a reusable technique: **deliver world exposition as something a character is failing to absorb.** The information is complete, the scene is funny, and the POV character is characterised by his inattention. Kendall appears on the screen inside it, so the audience meets the front-runner twice — once negotiating, once as corporate iconography.

**4. The birthday party as pressure chamber (pp.28–52, 39–72%).** One apartment, one afternoon, every claimant present, and an enforced social ritual that *forbids* the only conversation anyone wants to have. Three consequences: (a) the real conversations are displaced into corridors (p.35), bathrooms (pp.35, 50–52) and terraces (pp.36–37), which gives the writer a supply of two-handers with a built-in reason to be furtive; (b) the gifts become a status ranking made of objects — Shiv's book of the family's old houses (p.36), Connor's sourdough starter (pp.37–38), Tom's Patek Philippe (p.59) — each one a complete characterisation with no dialogue attached; (c) the *surprise* is a trap for Logan (p.29) and the *announcement* is a trap for the children (p.42), so the same ritual springs in both directions.

**5. The helicopter (pp.25, 53–58, 64, 66–69).** The pilot's central structural image, and a machine whose only dramatic function is to decide who is with whom. Logan says "I don't like leftie" (p.54), refuses to allocate ("It's fine. You decide"), and Colin puts Shiv in the disfavoured aircraft (p.56). Thereafter, the second helicopter is where Frank is executed (pp.66–68), where the children refuse (p.69), and where Logan's brain gives out (p.69). A seating chart becomes a power map — and unlike a catchphrase, an image like this can be re-staged forever without wearing out.

**6. The game of catch / the softball diamond (pp.58, 62–64).** The family plays a game with money inside it. Roman offers a groundsman's child a million dollars, the child is thrown out at third by a Roy grandchild, and Roman tears the cheque into quarters and hands one over. The sequence does three jobs: it states the family's operating principle (play converts to transaction converts to humiliation converts to a joke); it supplies the pilot's only outside witnesses; and it hands the episode its final image (p.72). Note the placement — the moral nadir sits at 86–89%, immediately before the stroke, so the audience is at its least sympathetic to the Roys at the exact moment the show asks for pity.

**7. Dialogue style as structure, not texture.** Four mechanisms, all load-bearing:
- **Interruption.** The script's first spoken line begins mid-sentence ("-- I get your position Lawrence," p.2). Scenes start late and end early; there is no CUT TO in 72 pages.
- **The unfinished line.** "Marcia I -" … "(he looks at her, doesn't say it) (I love you)" (p.13). The most important content in the script is placed in parentheses or omitted.
- **Rank-coded euphemism.** "It's housekeeping" (p.23) conceals a trust amendment; "Light duties" (p.67) means dismissal; "Frank is dead" (p.68) means the paperwork went out. Power is measured by who has to be told plainly.
- **The parenthetical as unspoken text.** "(no, but -)" (p.2); "(zero interest)" (p.29); "(not excited) I'm excited" (p.11).
The structural payoff: because the important information is always *inferred*, scenes can be short and can be about nothing. That is what lets the script run 53 scenes in 72 pages (average 1.4 pp/scene, several under half a page) and still advance three plots — and it is why an ensemble this size is affordable at all.

**8. Cross-cutting as scale.** 56 scenes, some of them two lines long (sc.40, p.56: "Three helicopters take off and head up the East River"). The cut rate makes a company on four continents behave like a single nervous system, and lets the pilot hold four cities in play through its first quarter without a single explanatory line.

**9. The named roll-call ending (pp.71–72).** The action line says the quiet part out loud: *"we revisit our key players and contenders for the throne."* The montage is an explicit inventory of claimants, and the one piece of new information it carries is a negative — the envelopes are **unsigned**. The pilot's last plot fact is that the plot device is still live.

**10. The withheld object (pp.59–60 → p.72).** Tom's Patek Philippe — "Every time you look at it, it tells you exactly how rich you are" — ends the episode in its box on a table in a Queens apartment belonging to the man whose son Roman humiliated. The draft never dramatises how it got there. The rhyme is delivered by *placement alone*, which is the most economical form of authorial comment available to a screenplay.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework

- **No act architecture at all.** No teaser, no act cards, no FADE IN/OUT, not one CUT TO. Turns are located by location change plus status flip plus information release. Any structural analysis of this script has to be reconstructed from behaviour, not from punctuation — which is the defining condition of premium-cable hour analysis.
- **The central turn lands at 58–60%, not 50% or 25%.** The trust bomb (pp.42–43) sits where a four-act broadcast hour would place its third act-out. The first 40% is installation. This only works because installation scenes are simultaneously negotiations; a conventionally structured pilot with 29 pages of pure setup would be dead on the page.
- **The A-plot resolves, in the hero's favour, and it doesn't matter.** Kendall *wins* the merger at p.70 — his p.9 goal, achieved verbatim ("we're a top ten media company"). Conventional pilot craft makes the hero lose in order to create forward motion. This one makes him win and then makes winning irrelevant eleven lines later, which is a far more specific and far harder promise: *this show's currency is not achievement.*
- **The protagonist's most important scene is silent and sits at 29%.** Logan failing to sit on a couch (p.21) is the character's entire problem, played without dialogue, in the middle of the second movement rather than at a hinge.
- **No relationship exposition whatsoever.** Nobody explains who these people are. The family structure is conveyed by a hug order (p.30), a nickname ("Primo," "Romulus"), and a whispered aside (Connor: "Three wives. Three families. And counting," p.65).
- **The antagonist speaks last.** Lawrence — who has just lost — gets the final line of dialogue in the pilot and uses it to state the series premise (p.71). The winner of the A-plot ends the episode silent and holding a phone.
- **The audience-surrogate storyline ends in total failure.** Greg gets six sequences and one crummy hotel room (p.71). Standard practice gives the surrogate a foothold so the audience has somewhere to stand; here the refusal is the point, and the surrogate's uselessness is the show's opinion of merit.
- **The cliffhanger is medical, not decisional.** The episode ends by removing the agent rather than forcing a choice — formally an anti-cliffhanger, yet the most propulsive possible ending, because it converts the series question from "will he choose?" to "can they take it?"
- **Ensemble load is well above hour-drama convention:** 20+ speaking parts, 8 principals, 4 cities, 2 continents, all installed inside 30 pages, with no voiceover and no expository confidant.

### Where this draft differs from the aired episode

This is **Draft 1, dated 22 July 2016** — roughly two years before transmission — and it differs from the broadcast pilot in ways worth logging:

- **The company is "Comco"** (with "Comco-Verity Pictures" and "Comco-Verity Studio Adventure" parks), not Waystar Royco. The mascot **"Doderick" survives to air**.
- **The acquisition target is "Rapid News,"** a cable news and sports operation defending itself with a management buy-out, led by **Lawrence Delmaestro** — not the digital-media company and Lawrence of the broadcast version. More significantly, **in this draft Kendall wins**: Lawrence suspends the management bid and concedes (p.70). The aired pilot does not hand Kendall a clean victory, which changes the shape of the ending considerably — the draft's ending is a *double* reversal (win + catastrophe), the aired one is a single one.
- **Logan's brother is ROGER ROY**, a Canadian farmer (pp.17–18, 64, 72) — renamed for broadcast. Greg is "Marianne's Greg," his grandson, exactly as aired.
- **Roman has a wife (GRACE) and a six-year-old child** (pp.28, 35, 49, 63) and **Connor has a wife (CARRIE)** (pp.28, 49, 65, 72). Neither spouse survives to the series; both children/marriages are draft-only, and their removal significantly sharpens both characters.
- **Shiv is written "late 20s" and described as "Logan's youngest"** (p.3) — younger than the aired character.
- **Kendall is described as "Logan's eldest son" on p.2 and Connor is described as "Logan's eldest son" on p.28** — an unreconciled artifact of a draft still working out the half-sibling structure that the series later makes explicit.
- **Frank is fired in the helicopter** (pp.66–68), immediately after delivering the toast, rather than at the office — a tighter, crueller placement than the aired version, and structurally the better choice.
- **Marcia is coded to Singapore** (p.52) rather than to the aired character's background; the "Singapore" repetition is the mechanism of her best scene.
- **A "SANDY"** appears on Kendall's own deal team (pp.9, 16, 18) — unrelated to the later series antagonist of that name.
- **The closing scene in Queens** — Tom's Patek Philippe sitting unopened on the table of the family whose son Roman humiliated (p.72) — does not survive to broadcast. It is the draft's most novelistic gesture and its loss is understandable; the connective tissue explaining how the watch got there was never written.
- **Scene numbers 2, 6, 7, 12 and 14 are missing and two scenes are unnumbered** (EXT. UPMARKET SHOPPING STREET, p.3; EXT. THEME PARK, p.14), indicating that this "Draft 1" had already been revised in place, chiefly around the London and theme-park material.

## Lessons for the knowledge base

1. **Install an ensemble by distance from the deal, not by rank in the family.** Kendall in the room where the money is (p.2) → Shiv on another continent (p.3) → Greg inside the company mascot (p.5) → Roman crashing a room he left (p.19) → Connor already at the party like furniture (p.28). The order inverts birth order, which pre-argues the series' central claim — that primogeniture does not govern here — before any character says so. Where a character stands relative to the *plot* is a stronger introduction than where they stand relative to the *protagonist*.

2. **Give every principal one action that is simultaneously an introduction and a position on the central question.** Connor's Tupperware of sourdough starter (pp.37–38) says "eldest, out of the running, wants his father to make something rather than buy something, and is wrong about him" without a line of exposition. Greg's rote recitation — "I am Doderick… I am the best friend to all I meet!" (p.6) — is a job description, a character diagnosis and a class position in one line.

3. **Deliver world exposition as something a character is failing to absorb.** The Comco induction video (pp.5–6) hands the audience four continents, fifty countries, three divisions and six named businesses while the POV character is stoned and twenty seconds from vomiting. The audience learns everything, the scene stays funny, and the character is defined by his inattention. Exposition delivered *at* a resistant listener costs nothing in sympathy.

4. **An ensemble pilot may defer the protagonist, but never the question.** "You wanna call your Dad?" (p.9, 13%) → the trust (p.42, 58%) → "I'll stay in situ" (p.43, 60%) → the stroke (p.69, 96%). Five characters can share the POV because one question owns the hour. The test is whether every scene either advances or complicates that question; in this script, every scene does.

5. **Build the middle as a chamber piece and let a social ritual do the plotting.** Pages 28–52 put every claimant in one apartment under the rules of a birthday party — rules that forbid the only conversation that matters. The forbidden conversation is then displaced into corridors (p.35), bathrooms (pp.50–52) and terraces (pp.36–37), which supplies a whole act of two-handers with a built-in reason to be furtive. The ritual also supplies free characterisation via objects: three gifts, three complete positions on the father.

6. **Make one physical object the map of the power structure, and re-stage it.** The helicopters (p.54 "I don't like leftie" → p.56 Shiv assigned to the disliked aircraft → pp.66–68 Frank fired in the air → p.69 the stroke) turn seating into standing. A repeatable *image* outlasts a repeatable *line*, because it can be re-blocked in every episode without ever being quoted.

7. **A status-reversal engine needs a price, not just a flip — and the price should be charged to the winner, later.** Logan wins Kendall's signature (p.24) and thereby manufactures the evidence he uses to demote him (p.48); he buys Roman and Shiv separately (pp.56–59) and thereby owes two jobs he cannot both deliver. Reversals without prices generate plot; reversals with deferred prices generate series.

8. **Let the hero win the A-plot and lose the episode.** Kendall closes the merger on p.70 — hitting the exact goal he stated on p.9, "go top ten," now spoken back to him as "we're a top ten media company" — and is orphaned on p.71 by the man he just beat, in the same conversation. One page teaches the audience the show's entire exchange rate, and it is the reason a deal-of-the-season structure can run for years without the deals mattering.

9. **End a pilot by removing the decider rather than making the decision.** Logan's brain bleeds mid-negotiation (p.69) at the exact line where his children name their price ("It's not sufficiently attractive. As a proposition."), and the papers are left explicitly *unsigned* (p.72). A question with a live vote and nobody able to call it is renewable; a question that gets answered is spent.

10. **Write the unsaid as structure, not as flavour.** Dropped sentences ("Marcia I -" / "(I love you)", p.13), rank-coded euphemism ("housekeeping," p.23; "Light duties," p.67), and mid-sentence entrances (the script's first spoken line, p.2) mean the load-bearing information is always inferred rather than stated. That is what allows 56 scenes in 72 pages, several under half a page, and it is what makes any two characters in a room sufficient for a scene — which is the thing an ensemble show needs most.

11. **Show the ensemble's moral floor immediately before you ask for pity.** Roman's torn cheque (pp.62–64, 86–89%) is placed six pages before the stroke. The audience is at its least sympathetic at the exact moment the show requests sympathy — which is how a satire earns the right to be a tragedy, and vice versa, without either register cancelling the other.
