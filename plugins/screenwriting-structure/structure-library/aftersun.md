**Framework lens(es):** PRIMARY — miniplot/anti-plot (McKee's triangle): a film assembled almost entirely from subtext, in which the actual story (a father's depression, and an adult daughter's attempt to reconstruct it) is never stated. SECONDARY 1 — the memory frame (adult Sophie / the rave / the camcorder) as structure vs. punctuation. SECONDARY 2 — the information architecture of subtext: the clue inventory and its release schedule.

Page citations are the script's **printed page numbers** (Final Draft header, top right). Printed page = PDF page − 1, consistently, with no A-pages and no gaps. Percentages are of the 100-page story body.

## Logline
On a package holiday in Turkey in the late 1990s, an eleven-year-old girl films her thirty-year-old father with a camcorder while he quietly comes apart beside her — and twenty years later, at exactly the age he was, she plays the tape back and tries to reach him in a place that never existed.

## Structure overview
There is no plot. There is a **holiday of fixed length**, cut into days, wrapped inside a **tape being played**. The script's outermost container is established on page 1 (a rave; then a cassette inserted, a tape rewound past "faces, a hotel room, sea, and sky", a click, "the tape begins to play from its beginning") and closed on pp.98–99 (the same airport footage, now revealed to be playing on a television in a New York apartment). Everything between is playback plus the reconstruction the playback provokes.

Inside that container the architecture is **calendar, not causality**. Each holiday day opens on a morning slug and closes on a night; the memory frame occupies the seams between days — the place where a memory has no footage. Movements:

Where an archplot would put its turns, this script puts nothing: p.25 is a capital-cities quiz, p.50 is a boat trip, p.75 is karaoke. The turns exist, but they are in the frame — pp.1, 21, 48, 69, 84, 97 — and they turn only one variable: how close adult Sophie can get to her father.

**The release schedule.** The curve has five phases, and it is engineered so that the audience is always slightly ahead of the characters and always behind the film. (1) **pp.1–10, the frame first:** the rave is placed before any evidence, so the audience begins in mourning without knowing for whom — every subsequent ordinary detail is read as potential evidence. (2) **pp.10–35, deniable domestic clues** (money, insomnia, the bath, tai chi), each innocuous alone. (3) **pp.37–48, escalation into the body** — blood (p.39), the sinking mask (p.42), the single spoken line (p.45), the descent into darkness (p.48): the script's most explicit disclosure and its clearest image of self-erasure sit back to back at the exact midpoint. (4) **pp.53–84, the slide** — "sinking" (p.53), the spit, the rug, the pill, the railing, the karaoke refusal, the sea, unconscious — closing on the frame's own biggest disclosure (p.84), which is not about him at all but about her age. (5) **pp.85–100, the inversion:** the last fifteen pages are the happiest in the film — the birthday, the cold spring, the win, the Polaroid, the dance — with the one unambiguous confession (p.93) buried in the middle of them, unwitnessed. The script gives its confirmation only after the audience has already convicted, and then spends its final act showing what will be missed.

**Rhythm.** The intrusions land at pp.1, 21, 48, 69, 84, 97, 98, 99 — intervals of 20, 27, 21, 15, 13, then continuous. A metronome for two-thirds of the script, then an accelerando. **Placement.** Five of the eight sit at the seam between one holiday day and the next — the frame occupies the position sleep occupies, i.e. exactly the gap where a memory has no footage. The exception is #3 (p.48), which arrives when Calum physically leaves the daylight world by diving into darkness.

**Structure or punctuation?** It is punctuation by volume and structure by function, and the script resolves the contradiction by **nesting rather than intercutting**. Page 1 does not cut to the past; it presses PLAY on it. Page 98 does not cut back; it pans right and reveals the screen the past has been playing on. Between those two moves, the frame's six brief interruptions carry the only complete want–obstacle–escalation–climax arc in the film: *see him (p.1) → look for him (p.21) → doubt what you see (p.48) → look away (p.69) → recognise yourself in him (p.84) → hold him and lose him (p.97)*. In other words, Wells quarantines a conventional three-act spine inside 4.8 pages of nightclub, which then frees the remaining 95 pages to have no spine at all without the audience ever feeling adrift. That is the mechanism. Called by its function: **the frame is the plot; the holiday is the evidence.**

## Storylines
- **A — the holiday surface (pp.3–96).** Scene goals are all trivially small and mostly Sophie's: get a game on the motorbike arcade, learn the cushion shot, do the dive, buy a souvenir, do the karaoke, win the tournament. It is shot as observational comedy — Belinda, "Torremolinos", Ankara, the bread rolls, the wetsuit, the milkshake stirrers. Nothing in the A-line generates a consequence that changes another scene, with one exception: the pool coaching of pp.27–28 pays off in the tournament final on p.94. That single causal thread across 67 pages is the entire quantity of conventional plotting in the film, and it belongs to the child.
- **B — Calum's interior state (pp.4–99), never dramatized directly.** There is no scene in which he decides anything, confesses anything, is diagnosed, phones a friend about it, or is confronted. The B-line exists only as (i) **solitude** — seven private scenes with no witness (pp.9, 21, 39, 48, 63, 69, 93); (ii) **objects** — the cast, the matchbook, the mask, the rug, the books, the Polaroid, the tape; (iii) **money** — six mentions, one of them aloud; (iv) **one sentence to a stranger** (p.45); (v) **displacement** — the emotion is always given to a proxy: he weeps at a carpet (p.57), not at his life; the sobbing on p.93 comes with his face in his hands and his back to camera, exactly as it was "obscured by the shade" in the amphitheatre one page earlier. He never once says the word for what is happening to him, and neither does the script.
- **C — Sophie's adolescence and the older teens (pp.15–83).** A clean, conventional, fully-dramatized coming-of-age line running underneath: first sighting and a word she doesn't know (p.16), the toilet eavesdrop and "she's, like, nine" (pp.28–30), admission by pool skill (pp.30–32), the wet-t-shirt comparison and her own flat chest (p.63), Olly's dismissal — "Where's your dad?" (p.77), Laura's wristband as a parting gift (p.78), the kiss with Michael under six watching silhouettes (pp.80–82), and finally Toby and Alex kissing in the dark (p.83) — the slur of p.16 resolved into something tender she has no words for. **C is the film's alibi:** it supplies the forward motion, the small victories and humiliations, and the audience's attention, while B accumulates in the same rooms unobserved.
- **Convergence.** The three lines fuse in the last-night dance (pp.96–98). The A-line supplies the event (a hotel disco), the C-line supplies Sophie's refusal ("I don't dance. I never, ever dance" — the exact posture adult Sophie holds, motionless, in the opening rave), and the B-line supplies the man who is "emphatically alive" for one night. Then the intercut makes the fusion literal: child Sophie's lighthearted "Stop" and adult Sophie's inaudible scream of "Stop"; the playful push-off and the pounding fists; his spin with her head against his chest and the adult's arms round his shoulders at the same angle. One choreography, performed twice — once by a girl who doesn't know what she's looking at, once by a woman who does.

## Structural devices
- **The camcorder, diegetic and structural.** *Diegetic:* an expensive gift ("Don't get that wet. It was expensive", p.11) that gives an eleven-year-old a socially permissible way to interview her father. *Structural:* the outer container of the whole film (rewind p.1 → play → pause p.99); the license for the film's epistemology, since the tape can only contain what was pointed at, and everything else is admitted as reconstruction; a sound spine (Calum's amplified breathing behind the lens, handed over to hers on p.99); and a joke about testimony — the only time he answers a direct question about his life is when the camera is nominally off (pp.66–67), which means the answer is not on the tape adult Sophie owns. Note also that Calum uses the camera the way she will: he watches her clip and **rewinds it to play it again** on p.21, and watches the same clip without smiling on p.76.
- **The karaoke scene (pp.74–76).** The film's one true set-piece, and it is a set-piece of refusal. He won't go up; she goes anyway; she is not good; she endures the whole track; "Losing My Religion" supplies a lyric no one comments on ("life is bigger… than you and you are not me"). It ends with the only line in the script that says his situation out loud, spoken by the child as a weapon. A conventional structure would put a confrontation here; Wells puts a bad song and a true sentence.
- **Mirrors and reflections.** Sophie faint in the steamed bathroom mirror inside the DV footage (p.20); the elevator doors during the tai chi (p.23); her own reflection after the toilet eavesdrop (p.30); the car window with the hand-print sunburn (p.35); **the infinity mirrors and the spit** (pp.53–54, still dry on the glass at p.54); the wardrobe mirror with the checked dress (p.63); "I'm on TV. How do I look?" (p.65); and Sophie reflected in Calum's dive mask (p.46). Reflection is how the film renders two opposite interiors — a girl assembling a self, a man unable to look at his — in one visual grammar.
- **The withheld birthday.** It is Calum's 31st. It is announced by the daughter, on camera, as a joke ("You're 130 years old, about to turn 131… In two days", p.66). It produces a story about a birthday nobody remembered and a red toy phone (p.67). It is wished on a bus at dawn (p.86) and celebrated by a hundred strangers with his face in shadow (p.92). And it is answered by him alone, naked, sobbing (p.93). He never says the word; he receives no present; there is no private scene of him being celebrated. The birthday is the pattern of the whole film in miniature: the emotion always belongs to the scene next door.
- **The rug as time machine.** Wept over (p.57), priced (p.58), argued about (p.59), bought in silence (p.63) — and then it is the floor the camera lands on in New York (p.84). One prop carries the film across twenty-odd years and does the exposition that no line of dialogue is permitted to do.
- **Paragliders as a falling motif.** Wonder (p.13) → "a paraglider spirals downward in a controlled, but precarious looking stunt" (p.34, immediately after "we share the sky") → decoration (p.48) → a reflection in an empty pool (p.58) → "Above, a waft of paragliders **fall**" directly over Calum standing on the balcony railing (pp.69–70). The same image, five times, weighted differently each time.
- **Escalation-by-blocking.** The balcony railing three times (sit, evidence, stand). The tape watched twice (smiles / doesn't smile). The rave six times (one variable: distance). Water four times (bath, dive, sea, cold spring). The script's substitute for rising action is the same shot, worse.
- **The final airport handoff (p.99).** The last recorded image gives way to a 180° pan; the corridor's double doors open onto the rave; he walks through and the pan continues before they close. It is the only place in the film where the diegetic world and the memory space touch, and the film ends its argument there rather than stating it. The breath changing owner over the same cut ("the almost-distorted-by-proximity-to-the-microphone breaths stop. A quieter, cleaner breathing pattern can now be heard") is the actual death notice.
- **The appendix as a statement of method (p.101).** "ADDITIONAL INTERSTITIAL SHOTS TO SHOOT" — the sinking mask, the cigarette end on the roof below, the gushing hose and the lost bag, the sun going behind the mountains, and one postcard: "I love you very much. Never forget that." A shooting draft that keeps its subtext on a separate, optional page is telling you the subtext is the picture.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework
**What a conventional structural analyst would call missing:**
- **No inciting incident.** Nothing at 10% (p.10 is a lilo and suncream) disturbs an equilibrium. The holiday simply begins and simply ends.
- **No protagonist goal, no antagonist, no stakes on the page.** Nobody wants anything a reader could state in a sentence. The character with the crisis conceals it; the character with the POV doesn't know there is one.
- **No act breaks.** Nothing locks in at 25% (p.25: capital cities); nothing reverses at 50% (p.50: a rooftop cafe); nothing is lost at 75% (p.75: karaoke).
- **No climax in the story layer.** The highest-stakes action in 1998 — walking into a black sea (p.82) — happens without witness, without decision, and without resolution; the next time we see him he is unconscious and the script moves on.
- **No resolution.** The film never states that Calum died, when, or how. The word "suicide" appears once, about Cleopatra (p.90).
- **Dialogue that refuses to carry plot.** The longest exchanges are a tongue twister (p.34), capitals of the world (pp.24–26), how to pot a ball off the cushion (p.27), and a red toy phone (p.67).
- **A passive, non-transformative protagonist pair.** Calum ends where he began; Sophie's change (she can pot the black, she has been kissed) is real but tiny relative to the film's actual subject.

**The reasoned defence:**
- **The dramatic question is retrospective, not prospective.** Not "what will happen" but "what was happening" — which makes the audience, not a character, the investigator. Momentum comes from an accumulating case, not from cause and effect. Every clue is simultaneously a payoff (it confirms the dread) and a new question (it demands the next one).
- **Substitute clocks do the work of a plot.** The holiday's fixed length, "School doesn't start until Tuesday" (pp.23, 50), "It's our last few days" (p.72), "In two days" (p.66), the tournament bracket (sign-up p.27 → semi p.93 → final p.94), and "Last night. Time for a dance" (p.96). Nothing is at stake and yet everything is timed, which reads on the page as pressure.
- **The act structure exists — in the frame.** pp.1 / 21 / 48 / 69 / 84 / 97 are, functionally, opening image, break into two, midpoint reversal, all-is-lost, revelation, climax. They occupy 4.8 pages. A reader who maps the film onto the holiday finds nothing; a reader who maps it onto the rave finds a textbook.
- **The climax is the dance, and the decision in it is adult Sophie's.** She spends the film reaching for him; on pp.97–98 she reaches him, holds him, and loses him on the turn of a strobe. That is a choice, an action and an irreversible outcome — every requirement of a climax — performed by the character with the fewest lines.
- **Openness is load-bearing, not evasive.** The premise is the insufficiency of what a child recorded. Confirming Calum's death would grant the film knowledge its narrator does not have, and would convert an act of reconstruction into an act of reporting. The information is withheld from us because it was withheld from her.
- **The one genuine tension inside the draft** is that Wells clearly held explicit material in reserve and kept quarantining it: the postcard "I love you very much. Never forget that." is not a scene but an optional insert on p.101; the final beach sprint (p.100) is a fourth ending appended after the embrace, the reveal and the doors; the stage direction "he dances with a woman, **Belinda?**" (p.69) leaves an identification unresolved in the text itself; the balcony POV is "as if from a specific point of view" with the point of view unnamed (p.9). The document is visibly deciding, at the shooting stage, how much to say — and the answer, repeatedly, is "put it on the last page and see."
- **Points to verify against the release cut** (assessed from the PDF text only, not from a shot-by-shot comparison, so treat as flags rather than findings): (a) whether any of the five p.101 interstitials — above all the postcard — were shot and used; (b) whether the script's final two beats after Calum enters the rave (sc.133 apartment, sc.134 the beach sprint) survive, or whether the film ends on the closing doors; (c) whether the NOIZ kiss (sc.82) plays as written and whether the woman is identified. Each is a case where the draft is more explicit than the finished film is generally described as being, and the direction of travel — draft explicit → cut implicit — is itself the most useful thing a writer can learn from this document.

## Lessons for the knowledge base
1. **If you withhold your subject, you must lend the audience a clock.** Aftersun runs on holiday logistics — a return date, a school term ("School doesn't start until Tuesday", pp.23 and 50), a birthday two days off (p.66), a tournament bracket (pp.27 → 93 → 94), "our last few days" (p.72), "Last night" (p.96). Nothing is at stake and everything is timed; the reader feels structure where there is only calendar.
2. **Quarantine one conventional arc somewhere small, and the rest of the film can be plotless.** The only want–obstacle–escalation–climax in this script lives in about 4.8 pages of rave (pp.1, 21, 48, 69, 84, 97), advancing exactly one variable: how close she can get. Feed the audience's need for direction on a metronome, and they will tolerate ninety pages of a family on holiday.
3. **Escalate a piece of blocking instead of a plot.** Calum and the balcony railing: sitting with his legs over a three-storey drop (p.9); a binned matchbook with three matches missing (p.64); standing upright on the rail at dawn as paragliders fall (pp.69–70). One location, three body positions, no dialogue, no consequence — and a complete arc.
4. **Give the truth to the least invested listener.** The single near-confession — "Can't see myself at 40 to be honest. Surprised I made it to 30" (p.45) — goes to a dive instructor he will never see again, thirty seconds after that stranger mentions a baby on the way. Secrets travel sideways, to people with no stake, and the audience hears what the protagonist's family never will.
5. **Let the child say the adult's diagnosis without knowing what she's said.** Sophie describes depression ("you kind of crash. And feel like you're kind of…sinking", p.53), states his poverty as an insult ("you obviously don't have the money", p.76), asks the film's central question ("what did you think you'd be doing when you were 31?", p.66) and explains a suicide to him on his birthday (p.90). Ignorance in the speaker converts exposition into subtext at zero cost.
6. **Move information across a time gap with an object, never with an explanation.** The rug he cannot afford (pp.57–63) is the floor the camera lands on in adult Sophie's New York apartment (p.84). An inheritance is cheaper, faster and more devastating than any flashback or line of dialogue about what happened after.
7. **Put your most explicit scene where it cannot be used.** Calum's sobbing (p.93) arrives *after* his daughter has made a hundred strangers sing to him, is witnessed by nobody, and causes nothing. It exists to confirm what the audience has already assembled — not to advance a story — which is precisely why it can be that naked without becoming sentimental.
8. **Rhyme choreography, not dialogue, to fuse two timelines.** The climax (pp.97–98) is a match cut of body language: "Stop" said lightly and "Stop" screamed inaudibly; the playful push-off and the pounding fists; his spin with her head on his chest and her arms round his shoulders at the same angle. Cut the same physical action twice, once as play and once as grief, and no one needs to explain the film.
9. **Decide formally who is remembering, and the impossible scenes become legal.** Because page 1 presses play on a tape and page 99 reveals the television it was playing on, everything outside the recorded footage is admitted as reconstruction. That single framing decision licenses the bath, the railing, the blood, the sea and the sobbing — scenes the POV character could not have witnessed — without a single cheat.
10. **Withhold the fact that rescales the film, and let arithmetic deliver it.** Sophie is 31 (p.1). Calum is 30, turning 31 (pp.2, 66). On p.84 someone says "Hey. Happy birthday" to adult Sophie. The film never says "I am the age my father was." It makes you do the subtraction — and the subtraction is the story.
