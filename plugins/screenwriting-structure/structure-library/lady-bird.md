# Lady Bird (2017) — Structural Beat Map
**Source:** /mnt/user-data/uploads/Screenplay planning/Ressources/Script Library/Feature Misc/lady-bird-2017.pdf · **Writer:** Greta Gerwig (title page: "written by Greta Gerwig"; PDF title "LADY BIRD clean shooting script 11.8.17", A24, created 8 Nov 2017) · **Format:** feature · **Genre:** coming-of-age drama / mother-daughter chamber piece · **Length:** 113 physical script pages = printed pp.1–110 (numbers 30, 50 and 70 each appear twice); 115 PDF pages + Didion epigraph page
**Framework lens(es):** PRIMARY — episodic / character-driven indie structure with no external goal-and-obstacle spine (organised instead by the school-year calendar, the mother-daughter curve, and a serial-experiment pattern of boyfriends/friends/identities). SECONDARY 1 — the relationship arc as spine (Marion/Christine is the A-story; the college application is only its delivery mechanism). SECONDARY 2 — scene economy (measured).

Page citations use the script's **printed** page numbers. Offset: printed p.1 = PDF p.3 (printed = PDF − 2). Printed numbers stop being visible after p.103 (PDF 108); pp.104–110 are extrapolated from the offset and flagged as such. Percentages are of 110 printed pages. Note the pagination is loose — three duplicated numbers and ten pages carrying under 400 characters — so page count overstates running time (110 printed pp → a 94-minute film, ≈1.17 pp/min).

## Logline
In her senior year at a Sacramento Catholic school, a seventeen-year-old who has renamed herself Lady Bird tries on boyfriends, friends, a stolen address and an East Coast future in order to become somebody other than her mother's daughter — and only after she has escaped, alone and hungover in New York, can she say her given name and hear Sacramento as love.

## Structure overview
There is no goal-and-obstacle spine. The college application — the only thing resembling a plot — requires no antagonist, generates exactly one active decision (p.14, asking Dad to hide the financial-aid forms) and resolves offstage in a mailbox (p.104: "She opens it. She got in."). What actually organises the script is three interlocking systems: **(1) the school-year calendar**, written on the page as three literal section cards; **(2) the Marion/Christine relationship curve**, which supplies every act-level turn; and **(3) a serial-experiment pattern** in which Lady Bird adopts an identity (theatre kid, Danny's girlfriend, Jenna's friend, Kyle's girlfriend, girl-from-the-Fabulous-Forties), performs it, is exposed, and discards it. Each experiment has the identical four-beat internal shape, so the episodes rhyme rather than merely accumulate.

The calendar is made explicit by three cards — **SECOND SEMESTER (p.50)**, **SUMMER (AGAIN) (p.100)**, **COLLEGE (p.106)** — none of which survives into the release cut as a title card. On the page they function exactly as act breaks: each one lands immediately after a Marion beat and immediately before a scene in which Lady Bird has changed.

The conventional turns, translated: **break into Act Two = p.14 (13%)**, the Corolla pact with Larry, the only choice she makes that has consequences. **Midpoint = pp.50–52 (45–47%)**, the SECOND SEMESTER card plus the homeroom scene where she trades Julie's sack lunch for Jenna's table — the protagonist changes tribes at the exact centre. **Break into Act Three = pp.72–75 (65–68%)**, the suspension fight ending with "GIVE ME A NUMBER" and a hand raised and lowered without striking. **Crisis = pp.99–100 (90–91%)**, not an event but a withdrawal: Marion stops speaking and Lady Bird chases her through the house shouting "TALK TO ME! MOM! MOM! PLEASE!"

## The relationship spine
The A-story is Marion and Christine. Its shape is not a rising line but a **flat oscillation with a widening amplitude, ending in a permanent separation and a one-way repair**. Every one of the script's act-level turns is a Marion beat; the college plot's only structural job is to give the two women something to be on opposite sides of.

Two spikes carry the design. **pp.40–50 (24 scenes, mean 0.44)** is the collapse of Danny — a month of grief, a school term, a Christmas and the entire college-application plot dispatched in ten pages, six of the scenes on a single page (p.47: men's room → "Crash Into Me" → the second performance → the cast removed → cheating on finals → Christmas). **pp.70–80 (6 scenes, mean 2.08)** is the crisis — the abortion assembly (3.45 pp), the suspension fight (2.64 pp) and Kyle's bedroom (4.15 pp) back to back. Gerwig spends pages exactly where the character cannot escape, and buys them back everywhere else.

**What the short-scene style does structurally.**
1. **Transitions carry time.** With no dissolves, no fades and no inter-scene `CUT TO:`, the only signal that weeks have passed is the cut itself plus a change of costume, weather or liturgy. Time-jumps are therefore free and invisible: the script moves from a men's-room discovery to Christmas morning in nineteen lines (p.47) without announcing anything.
2. **`CUT TO:` is repurposed as an ellipsis *inside* scenes** — Jenna's party (p.64), the bathroom (p.65, twice), Kyle's bedroom (pp.78–79, three times), Julie's kitchen (p.94, twice, with the body of the scene simply not written). It removes the middles of scenes rather than the joins between them, which is why the film feels like memory: entrances and exits, no negotiations. The script even names the device in dialogue on p.79 — "Cut to 'ten years later'" / "What does 'cut to' mean to you?"
3. **Thirty per cent of scenes carry no dialogue,** so the script can state emotional facts as images and move on: Marion tailoring the dress at midnight (p.37, 0.13 pp), Kyle's father through a doorway (p.81, 0.07 pp), Marion crumpling letters (p.105, 0.09 pp), the pink walls painted white (p.105, 0.09 pp). Each of these would be a scene with an argument in a conventional draft.
4. **The mosaic makes the calendar legible.** 124 scenes across one school year averages roughly one scene every three days of story time; the audience reads the accumulation as a year passing rather than as a plot advancing, which is precisely what allows a film with no goal to feel like it is going somewhere.
5. **Short scenes make the long ones structural events.** Because the median scene is 0.72 pages, a 4-page scene reads as a stop. There are only three scenes over 3 pages, and all three are exposures: the assembly, the suspension fight, Kyle's bedroom. Length itself is being used as emphasis.

## Storylines
- **A — Christine's self-invention.** The name (pp.4, 7, 18, 20, 30, 107), the Dream House address (pp.8, 40, 54, 76), the performative streak (pp.6, 70–72), the East Coast (pp.3, 9, 14, 104). Its through-question is asked by her mother at p.88 — "the very best version of yourself" — and answered by her at p.89: "What if this is the best version?" It resolves not by achieving the goal (p.104, a quarter page) but by saying "Christine" to a stranger at a party (p.107).
- **B — Marion.** Structurally the A-story. Installed alone at pp.9–10 with the script's only direct address ("Remember this moment with Marion"), given a private virtue-scene at p.37, a private professional-grace scene at p.59, and a wordless arc-completion at pp.105–106 (drives away, circles, circles back, too late). Her love is delivered by three proxies — Shelly (p.44), Larry (p.102), and finally the letters (pp.105–107) — because she cannot deliver it herself.
- **C — Danny, then Kyle (the serial experiment).** Two boyfriends, one shape. Danny: planted p.19, peaked p.33, exposed p.47, resolved into friendship p.59 and forgiveness p.97. Kyle: planted p.43 *inside Danny's best night*, chosen p.49, peaked p.64, exposed p.79, discarded p.92, and closed at p.104 by his father's obituary. Each ends with a discovery that the boy was never the person she invented.
- **D — Julie.** The control variable: the friendship she abandons at every experiment (pp.27, 51, 65, 69) and returns to at p.92 by choosing "Crash Into Me" — the song from her own first heartbreak. Julie carries the script's own counter-argument in miniature: her Mr. Bruno humiliation (p.45), her "some people aren't built happy" (p.94), her staying in Sacramento.
- **E — Larry's depression and job loss.** Seeded as an overheard argument (p.28), used as a weapon (p.30), named by Marion (p.66), bottomed at pp.83–85 when his adopted son competes for his job, and never resolved. It is the film's proof that the household's misery is structural, not maternal.

**Convergences.** pp.38–43 (Thanksgiving: the Dream House, Danny's peak and Kyle's plant in one sequence) · p.59 (Marion checks in Father Leviatch: the mother storyline crosses the theatre storyline once and is enlarged) · pp.81–82 (Kyle's dying father glimpsed, then Marion arrives to collect her — storyline C hands the protagonist directly to storyline B) · pp.83–86 (Larry's humiliation, then Sarah-Joan's "love and attention" — the father's defeat and the film's thesis in three pages) · pp.97–99 (Danny detonates the college secret into Marion: C ignites A into B) · p.104 (Kyle's father's obituary read by Lady Bird "just like her Mom": C closes inside B's private ritual).

## Structural devices
- **The bookend car scenes.** p.1: two women in one bed and then one car, fighting about music, tuition and a name, ending with the daughter throwing herself out. pp.105–106: the same 1994 Toyota Corolla, the daughter silent in the *back* seat, Sacramento going by, ending with the mother driving away. Seven Corolla scenes in all (pp.1, 10, 14, 81, 101, 105, 105) — the car is the family's only shared room, and the arc is measured by who is driving and who is in the back.
- **The name as through-line.** Signed in quotation marks (p.7) → defended to a priest, "given. To me, by me" (p.18) → written over her own on a cast list (p.20) → refused by her mother, "Don't lie to me, Christine!" (p.30) → offered to a stranger, "Christine. My name is Christine" (p.107) → returned to her mother as a gift, "It's the name you gave me. It's a good one" (p.110). The device is also formal: the character cue reads LADY BIRD 359 times and CHRISTINE 8, switching only at p.108 — and relapsing to LADY BIRD for one drunk line ("BRUCE!") before switching back.
- **Names written on the wall.** DANNY under the window sill (p.19), KYLE beside it (p.50), both painted over in white (p.105). The romantic storyline is filed, catalogued and erased in three shots totalling under half a page.
- **Sacramento: antagonist, then object of love.** "I hate California" (p.3) → "It's soul-killing. It's the mid-west of California" (p.67) → "You clearly love Sacramento… Don't you think maybe they are the same thing? Love and attention?" (p.86) → sunrise over the Capital Bridge as a small-town *Manhattan* (p.95) → "All those bends I've known my whole life" (p.110). The city is the only antagonist that gets an arc.
- **The unsendable letters.** Planted as an image (p.105: "Keeps throwing away versions"), smuggled by a third party (p.105: Larry slips the manila envelope into the suitcase), delivered off-frame, read alone (pp.106–107), and sealed with a condition that guarantees it can never be discussed: "don't tell her I salvaged them." The reconciliation is conducted entirely through objects because neither character is capable of the scene.
- **Church as the calendar.** Six liturgical spaces mark the year: the opening mass under credits (p.5), the vestment room where the girls eat unconsecrated wafers (p.17), the second-semester liturgy (p.50), Ash Wednesday's "you are dust" (p.60), graduation in the cathedral (p.97), and a Manhattan church she chooses to enter (p.110). She takes a blessing with her arms crossed twice (pp.5, 50) and finally sings.
- **Prom as a promise/refusal/reclamation.** Announced as an administrative item (p.52) → refused by Kyle in a car (p.90) → reclaimed by choosing Julie (pp.92–95). It is the only "goal" in the film she actively fights for, and it lasts three pages.
- **Hospitals.** Marion works nights in a psych hospital (pp.9, 59) and gives away baby clothes there; her daughter is strapped into a chair and wakes up in a hospital in New York (p.109). The rhyme is never spoken.
- **The mirrored two-hander.** Thrift Town at pp.35–36 and Thrift Town at pp.87–89: same shop, same choreography (a cruelty, a deflection, a garment), fifty pages apart, with the subtext promoted to text the second time. This is how the script builds a "midpoint mirror" without a plot to mirror.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework
- **No goal, and the goal it has is deliberately underweighted.** The college plot occupies perhaps six pages of screen time in total, has no antagonist, and its climax — "She opens it. She got in." — is 0.25 of a page at 95%, upstaged in the same scene by a stranger's obituary. The script is explicitly refusing to let the plot carry the ending.
- **No classical climax.** There is no confrontation, no decision under pressure, no final test. The nearest candidates are pp.87–89 (a question asked in a changing room) and pp.99–100 (a woman refusing to speak). The film's last twenty pages contain no scene in which the two leads talk to each other.
- **The "all is lost" is an accumulation and a silence,** not an event: p.75 (a slap withheld), p.77 (a lie exposed), p.81 (a first time ruined), p.99 (a mother's silence), p.106 (a goodbye refused). None is a reversal; each is a subtraction. It lands because the script has spent 100 pages proving that speech is the only currency these two have, so withdrawing it is the maximum available violence.
- **Act breaks are calendar changes, not decisions.** SECOND SEMESTER (p.50), SUMMER (AGAIN) (p.100) and COLLEGE (p.106) are typed on the page as bare cards. This is the script's most literal admission of its own architecture — and the release cut removes all three, leaving the structure to be felt rather than read.
- **The protagonist is passive in her own plot and hyperactive in her identity.** She makes one plot decision (p.14) and roughly a dozen identity decisions. Any beat sheet that tracks "what does she do to get what she wants" will read this script as shapeless; a beat sheet that tracks "what does she pretend to be, and who catches her" reads it as rigorous.
- **The theme is stated at 78% by a character with fifteen lines,** about a document the audience never sees (p.86). Conventional practice would put "love and attention" in the protagonist's mouth at the climax; giving it to Sister Sarah-Joan means Lady Bird can go on not understanding it for another twenty pages.
- **The resolution is unilateral.** Marion never learns that her daughter read the letters, never hears the voicemail on screen, never forgives her out loud. The film resolves the *feeling* of the relationship while leaving its *facts* unresolved — a structure that would read as a broken third act in any framework requiring the antagonist to be met.
- **Draft-vs-cut differences worth logging.** (a) The three section cards are page-only. (b) The script never stages Lady Bird driving alone through Sacramento — p.105 puts her in the back seat while Marion drives, and the drive exists only as a line in the voicemail ("did you feel emotional the first time that you drove in Sacramento? I did"); the finished film dramatises it and intercuts Marion on the same streets, cashing the p.10 instruction "Remember this moment with Marion" as image rather than report. This is the clearest case in the script of a plant written for the page and paid off in the cut. (c) The scripted repainting of the pink walls over Danny's and Kyle's names (p.105) is a page-level literalisation the finished film does without. (d) Julie's kitchen (p.94) is written as `CUT TO:` / `CUT TO:` with the body of the scene missing — the draft skips its own reconciliation scene and rejoins it at the punchline.

## Lessons for the knowledge base
1. **Substitute a calendar for a plot engine, and make it explicit while drafting.** Gerwig types SECOND SEMESTER (p.50), SUMMER (AGAIN) (p.100) and COLLEGE (p.106) into the script even though no card appears on screen. The cards are load-bearing during writing: they force the act breaks to land at 45%, 91% and 96% and they tell the writer what each block must accomplish. Write the cards, then delete them in the cut.
2. **Give the ostensible plot exactly enough weight to keep two people on opposite sides of it — and no more.** The college application is worth six pages, one decision (p.14), one secret, one detonation (p.98) and one quarter-page payoff (p.104). Its only structural function is to be a thing Marion must not know. A plot used this way needs no obstacles, because it isn't the story.
3. **Build the episodes as a repeating four-beat experiment, not as a list.** Adopt → perform → be exposed → discard: theatre/Danny (pp.17, 33, 47, 59), Jenna/the address (pp.51, 54, 63, 76), Kyle (pp.49, 64, 79, 92). Because every episode has the same internal shape, the audience learns the pattern by the second iteration and can feel the third one coming — which is what "structure" means in the absence of a spine.
4. **Plant the next experiment inside the current one's best moment.** Kyle is planted at p.43, in the middle of Danny's triumphant Thanksgiving night, with the script shouting at itself: "REMEMBER KYLE, Lady Bird will." This is how an episodic script generates forward pull without a question to answer.
5. **Put the theme in a minor character's mouth at ~78%, about something offscreen.** Sister Sarah-Joan's "Don't you think maybe they are the same thing? Love and attention?" (p.86) is delivered by a supporting character, about an essay we never read, to a protagonist who does not understand it. The protagonist is then free to remain in denial for twenty more pages, and the line is available for the audience to apply to the ending.
6. **Make the "all is lost" a withdrawal of speech.** Marion's silence (pp.99–100) works as a crisis only because 99 pages have established argument as this family's love language. Rule: whatever your characters do constantly, taking it away is your dark night — and it costs nothing to stage.
7. **When two characters cannot have the reconciliation scene, reconcile them through an object and a third party.** The letters are written (p.105), discarded, salvaged by Larry, smuggled into a suitcase, read alone (pp.106–107), and sealed with "don't tell her I salvaged them." Neither woman is ever forced out of character, and the audience gets the full emotional transaction.
8. **Mirror your act-one two-hander in act three, same location, same choreography, subtext promoted to text.** Thrift Town at pp.35–36 (a cruelty, a deflection, a dress) becomes Thrift Town at pp.87–89 ("I wish that you liked me" / "But do you like me?"). A repeated location does the work a plot reversal would do elsewhere.
9. **Use scene length as your only emphasis marker.** With a median scene of 0.72 pages, the three scenes over three pages (the assembly p.70, the suspension fight p.72, Kyle's bedroom p.77) read as the film's stops. Conversely, a run of six scenes on one page (p.47) can dispose of a break-up, a term and a Christmas. Decide what you are willing to spend pages on before you outline.
10. **Give the location an arc and it can serve as antagonist.** Sacramento is hated (p.3), diagnosed (p.67), reframed by an outsider (p.86), photographed as romance (p.95), and finally named as love (p.110). A place that changes value across the script gives a plotless film its measurable movement — and lets the last line be a piece of geography rather than a resolution.
