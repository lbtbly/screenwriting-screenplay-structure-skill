# Erin Brockovich (2000) — Structural Beat Map
**Source:** /mnt/user-data/uploads/Screenplay planning/Ressources/Script Library/Feature Misc/ErinBrockovich.pdf · **Writer:** Susannah Grant (title page: "ERIN BROCKOVICH / A True Story / by Susannah Grant", dated **February 1, 1998**) · **Format:** feature · **Genre:** true-story legal drama / underdog procedural · **Length:** 135 PDF pages; this is a retyped continuous transcript with **no printed page numbers** and no CONTINUEDs, so printed pagination cannot be recovered
**Framework lens(es):** PRIMARY — Syd Field three-act paradigm (inciting incident, Plot Point I, midpoint, Plot Point II, climax) applied to a true-story legal drama with no action engine. SECONDARY 1 — McKee: progressive complications, the gap between expectation and result, controlling idea. SECONDARY 2 — dual-protagonist / mentor-partner axis (Erin + Ed Masry) with the personal-cost storyline (kids, George) braided against the case.

All citations are **PDF page numbers** (the transcript has no printed page numbers; page breaks fall mid-sentence, so PDF pages run slightly long against standard script pages). Percentages are of 135 PDF pages.

## Logline
A twice-divorced, broke, unemployable mother of three bullies her way into a job at the small law firm that lost her injury case, finds medical records buried in a routine real-estate file, and — with no legal training, no money, and no one on her side but a burned-out personal-injury lawyer — assembles the evidence that forces a 28-billion-dollar utility to pay for poisoning a desert town's water, at the cost of her health, her boyfriend, and her children's trust.

## Structure overview
The script has no chase, no fight and — until roughly the 85% mark — no clock. It manufactures act-break-scale turns out of three renewable resources: **numbers that get bigger** (dollars offered, plaintiffs counted, parts per million), **documents that change hands**, and **a recurring negotiation ritual between Erin and Ed** that prices every structural turn. Every major act break in this script is literally a transaction: Erin asks Ed for something, and what she asks for tells you where the story is.

Act One is unusually long and double-hinged. The story's *situation* (job) turns at p.13 (~10%); the story's *problem* (the file) arrives at p.19 (~14%); but the story's *question* — can these two beat PG&E? — is not asked until Ed comes to Erin's door at p.45–48 (~33–36%). In between, the script does something structurally aggressive: it **fires its protagonist at ~30% and bottoms her out before the act break** (pp.39–45), so that Plot Point I is a resurrection rather than a decision.

## Progressive complications pass
Every escalation in this script follows the same shape: Erin asks a system for something reasonable, and the system responds with contempt disguised as procedure. The gap is almost never about *information* — it is about status.

## Storylines
- **A — The case (Hinkley v. PG&E).** Runs p.19 → p.133. Its structure is *accumulation*: one file → one family → four families/eleven people (p.67) → "more'n a hundred plaintiffs" (p.87) → 600 (p.103) → 635 (p.120, 130). Its obstacles are institutional rather than personal — a fact sheet, a statute of limitations, a demur with sixty-nine counts, a settlement offer, a legal calendar of ten years. Its climax is the delivery of paper. Notably, the A-story has **no scene in which the antagonist states its own goal**: PG&E is only ever a building (p.32), a fax recipient (pp.51, 75, 86), an escalating grade of lawyer (Baum → the four sharks → Corbin), and a 1966 memo.
- **B — Home: the kids and George.** Runs p.8 → p.126. It is not a subplot but the *price tag* on the A-story, and the script keeps them physically fused: the discovery is made with Beth on her chest (p.23), the plant is photographed while the kids draw in poisoned dirt (p.50), the water board is Xeroxed with a crying baby (p.50), the Kinko's run and the signature campaign are done with three children in the motel room (pp.119–125). George's arc is a clean inversion of Erin's: he begins as noise and ends as the only adult she trusts; he is right in every argument and loses every one. His exit (p.94) removes childcare, which is why it registers as plot. The B-story resolves in two halves — George at p.121 ("Think you could learn?") and Matthew at p.125 ("You want eggs?") — **both before the climax**, so that the win at p.130 is not the reward for family repair.
- **C — Ed Masry.** Runs p.2 → p.135. A man whose "passion… has long since dissipated" is re-lit by a case he never read. His arc is measured by five transactions with Erin (p.13 job/"no benefits" → p.48 rehire, raise, dental → p.68 raise + cel phone → p.97 paycheck, $5,000, Blazer → p.135 $2,000,000), and by one repeated line ("I'm drawing the line," p.48 → p.135). His mid-film peak is "I like this case… It's been a long time since I had a case I cared about" (p.81); his betrayal at p.109 repeats Erin's two intolerables from p.12 verbatim in behaviour ("being ignored, and being lied to"); his resolution is dictated to him at the elevator (p.131). The script deliberately kills the romantic option at the motel (p.91) so this axis can end in respect.
- **Convergence points.** p.45–48 (Ed crosses town with the science: A + C fuse); p.68 (midpoint: Ed's conditional yes sets the terms of the climax and buys the cel phone that detonates at p.110); p.94 (George's ultimatum forces Erin to name what the case gives her: A + B fuse); p.109–112 (Ed's rejection and Matthew's rejection land within three pages, in the same vocabulary of neglect); p.121–125 (George minds the children *so that* the signature campaign can happen — the B-story becomes the A-story's logistics); p.130–131 (the box and the memo settle both the case and the partnership).
- **Donna Irving as the case's face.** She is the A-story's viewpoint character: the first witness (p.28), the first convert (p.52, "OUT OF THE POOL!"), the one who extracts the promise (p.86, "You gotta promise me we're gonna get them"), the co-canvasser in a chemo scarf (p.122), and the recipient of the verdict on the beach (p.133). Any beat the abstraction needs to be made human is given to Donna.

## Structural devices
- **The negotiation ritual as act-break marker.** Five times, a turn is priced: p.13 (a job, no benefits), p.47–48 (rehire + 10% + dental), p.68 (raise + cel phone), p.97–98 (paycheck + $5,000 + a Blazer), p.135 ($2 million). At the climax the ritual is performed with words instead of money — "Say you were wrong" (p.131). If you want to know where you are in this script, look at what Erin is asking Ed for.
- **Numbers as escalation.** Two ladders run in parallel and cross at the midpoint: money climbs ($74 → $100 loan → $16 in the bank → $65,000 comps → $250,000 → $2M → $20M → $50–400M → the maximum → $5M to the Irvings → $2M bonus), and plaintiffs climb (1 family → 11 people → 100+ → 600 → 635). Toxicity supplies a third: 0.58 ppm found (p.39) against a legal limit of .05 (p.46). The film's biggest reversals are ratios read aloud.
- **Documents as physical objects with a location.** The box Ed trips over (pp.11, 18) → a single Xerox (p.39) → the TOXICOLOGY and PLAINTIFFS binders (pp.71, 75) → the crematory urn on her desk (p.75, "She lived on the plume. You never know.") → PG&E's retaliatory "slew of paper" (p.129) → the shredded warehouse (p.128) → an inch-high stack in Embry's garage (p.129) → a box of 635 forms and one envelope (p.130). Paper is the film's weapon, its wound and its scoreboard.
- **The fax-machine LED as a repurposed motif.** The same insert shot appears at p.51 (PG&E CLAIMS DEPT — attack), p.75 (again), p.86 (three times in three cuts — pressure), and then p.105–106, where the recipient reads **POTTER, HUGHES, ROSEWOOD** — the identical image now meaning Erin has been replaced. One shot, three meanings, no dialogue.
- **The two boxes in the truck.** Act III's suspense is carried by an inventory: unsigned forms shrinking, signed forms growing (pp.122, 124, 126). A progress bar substituting for a ticking clock.
- **Water, pools, and the ocean.** Donna's backyard pool (p.28) → "OUT OF THE POOL!" (p.52) → photographs of the green community pool "that PG&E built" (p.75) → Annabelle "wanted to be a synchronized swimmer, so she spent every minute she could in the PG&E pool" (p.104) → Donna's daughters boogie-boarding in clean surf while she learns the number (p.132–133).
- **The water bug as a powerlessness meter.** Crushed on the stoop (p.9), crawling across the crossed-out want ads (p.11), crushed again (p.20), and then the uncatchable one that triggers her breakdown (pp.42–43). Once she has the case, the bugs vanish from the script entirely.
- **Long-distance plants.** The Jaguar (p.2 → p.77, Laura Ambrosino's "I always thought I wanted outta life… a Jaguar"); the tequila from the drug-dealer client (p.3 → p.80); "Erin Pattee Brockovich" signed at the water board (p.37 → p.73, "No one calls me Pattee"); her memory for numbers (p.17 → pp.103–104); dyslexia (p.14 → the laborious read-aloud at p.39 → the memorized phone numbers at p.103); the cel phone won at the midpoint (p.68 → thrown at p.110); "I'm drawing the line" (p.48 → p.135).
- **Beth's development as the calendar.** In a script with no title cards and no seasons except one Christmas party (p.82), time is measured by a baby: nine months (p.4), ten months (p.17), first word heard by telephone (p.74), toddling in the motel room (p.124). The film's most devastating personal beat is also its clock.
- **Illness as the missing deadline.** No ticking clock exists until p.115 ("five years, maybe, for a trial. Double that for the appeal"), so the script installs a biological one at p.85 and escalates it in five increments (pp.85, 88, 105, 106, 109) to collapse and meningitis at pp.112–113. Erin's body converts an abstract legal delay into something an audience can watch.
- **How the legal exposition is dramatized.** Four techniques, each used once so none wears out: (1) *Exposition under a fee negotiation* — the statute-of-limitations lecture at p.61 is smuggled in under Erin's improvised outrage about Ed's 40%; (2) *exposition as humiliation* — Paulsen mistaking her for a prank (p.25), Frankel barely noticing her (p.35); (3) *exposition as an interruption war* — Potter's rapid-fire "demur / counts / who's the judge" over Erin's unanswered "What's a demur?" (p.102), where the content matters less than the fact that she can't get a word in; (4) *exposition read aloud badly* — the abatement order sounded out syllable by syllable at p.39.
- **Antagonism distributed instead of personified.** The corporation is architecture (p.32), a fax number, a hierarchy of lawyers, and a memo from 1966. Every scene that a conventional draft would give to a villain is given instead to a well-meaning obstacle: Anna, Jane, Brenda, Baum, Theresa, Potter, and finally Ed himself.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework
- **Act One runs to ~36% and hinges twice.** The situation turns at p.13 (~10%), the problem arrives at p.19 (~14%), and the dramatic question isn't asked until p.48 (~36%). Field's paradigm wants one Plot Point I; this script has a soft one and a hard one, and the twenty pages between them are spent making the audience need money as badly as Erin does. It works because the *investigation* is already engaging before the *case* exists.
- **The all-is-lost beat is placed before the act break, not before the climax.** Getting fired (p.40), the bug meltdown and the tiara speech (pp.42–45) form a full dark-night sequence at 29–33% — and then Plot Point I is a *rescue*: Ed comes to her door with the science. This inverts the conventional order and buys the script a protagonist who has nothing left to lose entering Act II, which is what licenses her recklessness for the next sixty pages.
- **The second act is 46% of the script and is structurally an accumulation, not a duel.** There is no reversal-counter-reversal engine because the opponent never engages: PG&E's moves are a lowball, a fax exchange, a better lowball, a chase, and an arbitration offer. What sustains the act is (a) new plaintiffs arriving on a referral chain, (b) escalating dollar figures, and (c) the B-story steadily charging Erin for her progress. Removing the personal-cost storyline would leave Act Two structurally inert.
- **The only personified antagonist turns out to be an ally (p.127).** The Man in Dark Glasses gets a stalk, a chase, and a face-to-face confrontation — and is then revealed as Charles Embry, the shredder operator who kept the documents. This is the film's boldest structural gamble: it spends its entire supply of physical menace on a red herring, and pays it back by making the climactic evidence a moral act by a stranger. The cost is that the story never gets a villain to defeat; the compensation is that the film's thesis (people are not what they look like) is proven on the audience, not just on Erin.
- **The climax is a delivery, not a confrontation.** Erin walks into a room and puts down a box. The dramatic charge comes entirely from setups paid in sequence: the firehouse failure (p.118) is answered by 635 signatures, and "show me the document that proves that" (p.81) is answered by a memo stamped March 1966. This is the strongest available argument that in a procedural, **your climax's power is manufactured 50 pages earlier by stating the impossible condition out loud.**
- **The verdict is reported, not staged.** No arbitration hearing, no courtroom, no reaction shots of the plaintiffs. The result is delivered in conversation on a beach (p.133), and — remarkably — **the total award is never stated as a number.** "He's making them pay the maximum" (i.e. the top of the 50–400 million range named at p.108) and "five million of it to you all." A draft this obsessed with numbers withholds the headline figure at the exact moment the audience wants it, keeping the resolution about Donna's face rather than the score. (As released, the film is much more explicit about the total; this draft's restraint is a genuine structural difference.)
- **Two protagonists, one arc apiece, deliberately un-romanced.** Erin's arc is from "how'd I end up so nothing?" (p.44) to dictating her boss's apology (p.131); Ed's is from "you shouldn't judge, Brenda" said reflexively (p.4) to "Because that's how people treat each other!" said furiously (p.104) to "I was wrong. … Both." (p.131). The motel scene (p.91) explicitly raises and closes off the romance in nine lines so the partnership can resolve in respect, not a kiss.
- **True-story sprawl shows in the seams.** This February 1998 draft carries visible unfinished work: Mandy Brown's husband is **Tom** at p.57 and p.123 but **Roy** at p.61; the Ambrosino scene at p.64 mis-slugs Mike's dialogue as **PETE**; PG&E's team is "Ms. Cooper" in dialogue and "MR. COOPER" in the description at p.86, and Sanchez is "MS." then "MR." then "Miss"; at p.108 the senior attorney's second speech is assigned to **HEAD COUNSEL** rather than **CORBIN**. The plaintiff family is named **Irving** here (the released film uses a different surname for the lead family), and Mario, the office boy, is characterized in terms the finished film softened considerably. None of these are structural, but they confirm this is a working draft, not a shooting script — and the structure is nonetheless essentially the film's.
- **What is genuinely missing from the paradigm, and why it doesn't hurt.** No ticking clock for 85% of the script; no physical jeopardy for the protagonist except a chase that turns out to be a misunderstanding; no scene between the hero and the antagonist. The script survives all three because it substitutes a *body* for the clock (the cough), a *relationship* for the duel (Erin vs. Ed), and *arithmetic* for the villain (28 billion, .58 vs .05, 635, ten years).

## Lessons for the knowledge base
1. **Give the protagonist a recurring transaction with one other character and use it to mark every act break.** Erin's five negotiations with Ed (pp.13, 47–48, 68, 97–98, 135) let a sprawling true story keep a legible pulse: the audience always knows what stage the story is at because they know what she's currently worth. The climax then lands by *breaking* the pattern — at p.131 she demands words, not money.
2. **State the impossible condition out loud at the midpoint, then pay it at the climax.** "If, and only if, you find me the evidence to back all this up" (p.68) and "Show me the document that proves that" (p.81) are the two locks; Embry's unshredded memo (p.129) and 635 signatures (p.130) are the two keys. In a story without physical stakes, a *specified* impossibility is the most reliable way to generate act-break-scale tension.
3. **When there is no villain, escalate by seniority, by arithmetic, and by referral.** PG&E's contempt is dramatized by who it sends (a squeaking mail clerk at p.54 → four sharks at p.86 → Corbin's private plane at p.107), its power by a single figure ("a 28-billion dollar corporation," p.55), and the case's growth by a chain of neighbours vouching for each other (pp.57–65). None of these requires the antagonist to appear.
4. **Bottom out the protagonist *before* the act break, and let the mentor cross town to restart the story.** Fired at p.40, unable to kill a bug at p.43, "how'd I end up so nothing?" at p.44 — then Ed knocks on her door at p.45 with the ratio that makes the case real. Placing the dark night at ~30% produces a hero who enters Act Two with nothing to protect, which is the only reason her recklessness reads as courage instead of ego.
5. **Make the cost storyline structurally load-bearing, not decorative.** George is childcare (p.21) before he is a boyfriend, so his departure (p.94) is a logistics crisis; Beth's development is the calendar (pp.4, 17, 74, 124), so "Beth started talking" is simultaneously the story's most painful beat and a time-stamp. If the B-story can be cut without breaking the A-story's schedule, it isn't braided — it's parallel.
6. **Dramatize exposition by attaching it to a status humiliation.** Every block of legal or scientific information in this script arrives with someone treating Erin as stupid — Paulsen assuming a prank (p.25), the statute lecture buried under Ed's fee embarrassment (p.61), Potter talking over her four unanswered questions (p.102). The audience absorbs the content while watching the conflict; the information is never the scene's subject.
7. **Install a biological clock when the plot refuses to supply one.** The cough at p.85 escalates in five beats to collapse at p.112 and meningitis at p.113, arriving just before the story's real deadline is finally named ("ten years… a whole bunch of them are gonna be dead," p.115). A protagonist's body is the cheapest available deadline in a story about paperwork.
8. **Let one recurring insert shot change meaning to mark a reversal.** The fax LED reads PG&E CLAIMS DEPT while Erin is winning (pp.51, 75, 86) and POTTER, HUGHES, ROSEWOOD when she has been replaced (p.106). Repetition builds the vocabulary; substitution delivers the turn — with no dialogue and no new scene.
9. **Spend your only menace on a misdirection if the reveal proves the theme.** The stalker sequence (pp.74, 76, 88–91) exists so that Embry's confession (pp.127–129) can land: the man everyone — including the audience — read as a thug is the only person in the film who acts purely on conscience. In a story whose subject is being misjudged on appearance, the structure itself must misjudge someone.
10. **Withhold the number you have spent the whole script escalating.** After ninety pages of dollar figures, the final award is given as "the maximum" and one family's share (p.133). Refusing the headline figure at the resolution keeps the last movement about Donna's face and her daughters in clean water — and demonstrates that a payoff can be *emotional* even in a story whose entire escalation engine was numerical.
