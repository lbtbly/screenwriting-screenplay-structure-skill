**Framework lens(es):** PRIMARY — Save the Cat 15-beat sheet (this script is the KB's canonical STC exemplar). SECONDARY 1 — Snyder's story-DNA genres, argued as **Fool Triumphant** with a Golden Fleece prize-swap. SECONDARY 2 — rom-com beat canon and this script's systematic subversion of it.

Page citations are the script's **printed** page numbers (PDF page = printed page + 1; the two revision inserts 108A and 109A sit at PDF 110 and 112). Percentages are of 117 printed pages.

## Logline
A Bel Air sorority queen dumped for being "too blonde to be serious" talks her way into Harvard Law to win her ex-boyfriend back — and discovers, defending a fitness mogul accused of murder, that the frivolous expertise everyone mocks her for is the only thing in the building that can crack the case, and that the man she came for is not the prize.

## Structure overview
The script runs a clean four-block architecture whose front half is close to textbook Snyder and whose back half is unusually compressed. Act One is a **Rites of Passage** opening (a heartbreak, a mourning montage, a grief-driven plan) that is abandoned the moment the Boxster hits Harvard Yard at p.25; from there the DNA is **Fool Triumphant** — an underestimated outsider against an establishment — and the story stops being about a life problem and starts being about an institution.

The engine that makes the whole thing hold is a single design decision: the murder plot is built so that **only the heroine's despised expertise can solve it**. The alibi is a beauty secret (liposuction, p.75); the murder weapon is a beauty fact (perm maintenance, p.110); the false witness is exposed by a fashion observation (p.94). The A story and the theme are the same object, which is why a comedy about hair can carry a homicide trial without changing register.

Two structural oddities are worth flagging up front. First, the "Bad Guys Close In" stretch (54–83%) is a **winning streak** — Elle gets the internship, gets the alibi, out-argues the class, cracks the pool boy — so the section Snyder designs as descent is played here as ascent, and the crisis when it arrives is one scene long. Second, the **All Is Lost lands at 85%**, seventeen points later than prescribed, which leaves a 12-page third act where Snyder budgets roughly a quarter of the script.

| Block | Pages | % of script | Function | Governing question |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Act One — the wrong plan** | 1–25 | 1–21% | Sorority world, the break-up, the mourning, the LSAT campaign, admission, arrival | Can she get him back? |
| **Act Two-A — the Fool in the establishment** | 25–61 | 21–52% | Harvard as antithesis; sorority tactics fail (25–44%); humiliation flips to resolve; study montage; first legal victories; the internship | Can she survive here? |
| **Act Two-B — competence under load** | 61–100 | 52–85% | The Brooke Windham case; the withheld alibi; the ethical test; Emmett; the pool boy cracked; then Donovan's hand on her thigh | Is her competence real, or is she being humoured? |
| **Act Three — the Fool triumphant** | 100–117 | 85–100% | Quit → salon resurrection → Brooke fires Donovan → the perm cross → Warner refused, Emmett accepted, the club table | What is she actually worth, and to whom? |

Key turns: **catalyst p.8 (7%)**, **break into Two p.25 (21%)**, **B story p.33 (28%)**, **midpoint hinge pp.51–61 (44–52%)** with the fulcrum at **p.58–60 (50–51%)**, **All Is Lost p.100 (85%)**, **Break into Three p.103 (88%)**, **finale pp.105–116 (90–99%)**, **final image pp.116–117 (99–100%)**.

**Overall verdict.** Front half: near-textbook, with three beats (Break into Two, B Story, Final Image) landing within a point or two of Snyder's prescription and the midpoint landing at 50.4%. Back half: **the crisis apparatus is displaced ~15 points late and compressed to roughly a third of its prescribed length**, buying that space for an inverted, ascending "Bad Guys Close In." The script is a legitimate STC exemplar, but the KB should teach it as an exemplar of *how much back-half compression a well-built front half can afford*, not as a beat-by-beat metronome.

## Storylines
- **A — the competence spine (law school → internship → the Windham case).** Owns the clock (the 1L year, then the trial) and every reversal of status. Its arc is measured in what she is trusted with: kicked out of class (p.33) → a right answer (p.53) → a resume request (p.56) → an internship (p.61) → a client's secret (p.75) → a witness (p.107) → a club table (p.116). The Windham case is not a separate storyline but the A story's final container, and it is designed backwards from the theme: a blonde accused, a beauty secret for an alibi, and a beauty fact for a murder weapon.
- **B — the Warner→Emmett romance.** Warner is the *stated* object of desire, and the script gives him all of the rom-com apparatus: the near-proposal (p.6), the break-up (p.8), the pursuit (pp.40–49), the jealousy triangle (p.36), and the third-act reconciliation speech (p.113). He is refused at 97%. Emmett is the *actual* object and is given almost none of that apparatus — no meet-cute (a bench, a kindness), no obstacle, no jealousy, no breakup, no grand gesture. He advances through argument (p.79–85), professional risk (p.95, p.106) and the returned line "have a little faith" (p.80 → p.109). His romantic climax is 1.5 pages at 98%; the *emotional* climax of the pairing is the two-shot at p.112 ("Oh, my God!" / "Oh, my God!!") over a murder confession.
- **C — Paulette and the manicure world.** The class mirror and the theme's proving ground. Introduced as a woman who reads her own abandonment as evidence of her worthlessness (p.38–39), she becomes Elle's first client (p.58), first student (p.69), first success story (p.92–97), and the first person to say the film's thesis out loud to Elle's face — "You're going to make a great lawyer" (p.98), one page before Donovan's assault and five before Stromwell's resurrection line. The salon is also the film's true sanctuary: Elle goes there after every defeat (pp.37, 52, 67, 89, 102), and the Break into Three happens in it.
- **D — Sarah Knottingham, rival to partner.** The arc that quietly receives the complete rom-com beat sheet the Emmett romance is denied: hostile meet (p.33, p.36) → escalation (p.43, p.50) → grudging respect (p.78) → olive branch (p.89–92: "We'll drink it after we win") → betrayal misread (p.100–101) → apology and declaration (p.114–115: "I'm a bitch." / "Yes, you are.") → union (pawning the ring for Jamaica) → visible transformation in the final image (newly blonde).

**Where they converge.** p.58 — A meets C when Elle lawyers for Paulette, at exactly 50%. p.63 — A absorbs the Windham case. p.75 — the A-story secret turns out to be a C-world secret (liposuction), fusing plot and theme. p.77 — the sorority creed becomes attorney–client privilege, fusing the opening world with the antithesis world. pp.94–97 — A meets B when Emmett spends his credibility on her insight. p.103 — C delivers A's Break into Three (Stromwell in the salon chair). pp.105–107 — total convergence: Paulette and the UPS Guy, Serena and Margot, Dorky David, Sarah, Emmett and Brooke are all physically in the courtroom, and each is used. p.109 — B's line comes back into A ("Have a little faith"). p.116 — a B-story car-ride joke (the Blonde Legal Defense Fund) becomes A's institutional endpoint, staffed by D.

## Structural devices
- **Costume and colour as a status ledger.** Pink Prada pedal pushers (p.2) → a white Hervé Léger mini, "bridal... but not like I suspect anything" (p.4) → Warner's oversized Sigma Chi t-shirt in mourning (p.14) → skimpy Gucci plus fake wire-rimmed glasses, "I totally look the part!" (p.28) → a Playboy Bunny costume among Hester Prynnes (p.47) → the bunny tail ripped off and thrown (p.51) → ponytail, sweats, a nail broken and typed through (p.51, p.89) → a cashmere twinset and a leather briefcase, "You look — nice" (p.62) → **a Prada mini-dress and bubble gum for the climax (p.105)** → a red 2L t-shirt (p.116). The arc: dressing for a man → dressing for an institution → dressing as herself with authority. The finale deliberately reverses the p.62 assimilation, and Sapphire's "What happened to her tan?" (p.112) is the joke that measures the distance travelled.
- **The Bend & Snap as a three-stage payoff.** Taught at pp.69–71 with zero plot content; detonated as a disaster at pp.92–93 (a broken nose); resolved as a romance at p.97; and paid a fourth time visually — the UPS Guy sitting in the courtroom gallery in a nose brace at p.107. A comic set-piece that costs a page and a half at 60% is only affordable if it is cashed more than once.
- **Plant the domain, not the clue.** The perm rule (p.110) is never mentioned before the scene in which it wins the case. What *is* planted, from page 5, is that Elle's beauty expertise is technical, evidentiary and correct under pressure (the rayon takedown, p.6; "silicone is so 90's," p.47; "gay men know designers," p.94; the "Delta Gamma would never sleep with a man in a shiny shirt / a thong" chain, pp.72, 88). The audience is trained to accept the *category* of knowledge, so the specific fact can arrive cold and still feel fair.
- **The pool boy as a snobbery-to-evidence conversion.** Enrique's shiny unbuttoned shirt (p.72) → "no Delta Gamma would sleep with a man in a shiny shirt" (p.72) → the thong exhibit (p.88) → "Don't stomp your little Prada sandals at me, Miss Thing" (p.94) → the Cher tape (p.95) → "And what is your boyfriend's name?" (p.96). Every rung of the ladder is a joke about taste; the last one is testimony.
- **Kindness as plot currency.** Amy the pledge (p.3), Dorky David's step-stool (p.52 — he helps *her* first), the public slap that gets David a date (pp.85–87), the goodie basket for Brooke (p.74), the Bend & Snap for Paulette (p.69). David repays it with Rule 3:03 at p.105; Brooke repays it by firing her own attorney at p.105; Paulette repays it with the thesis at p.98. The Save the Cat beat is not a one-off likeability tax here — it is the script's economy.
- **"Sisters forever" as a migrating creed.** A charm bracelet in the opening shot (p.1) → the sisters' goodbye (p.13) → "I can't break the bonds of sisterhood!" as a legal position (p.77) → "Sisters forever?" / "Sister forever" as Elle chooses the case over the limo (p.92) → "Don't fuck with a Delta Gamma" in the courtroom (p.107). The sorority's silliest slogan is revalued into professional ethics without ever being renounced.
- **The Rock as a transferable object.** Anticipated (p.3) → on the rival's finger (p.36) → the hot-tub jab (p.61) → grabbed and appraised by Margot (p.92) → pawned to fund a study trip between women (p.115).
- **Dogs measure who has power.** Underdog is a purse accessory (p.1), then a courier (p.42), then a witness "intrigued by her line of questioning" from the counsel table (p.108A), then sitting on the club flyers (p.116). Rufus is the property Elle wins back with the law (p.59). The animals track the heroine's shift from ornament to agent more economically than any dialogue does.
- **Comedy set-pieces that carry plot.** Test each one: the Halloween party (pp.47–51) delivers the humiliation that flips the story; the sperm-donor argument (pp.54–56) wins the internship; the Dewey confrontation (pp.57–59) is the midpoint; the Bend & Snap (pp.69–71) delivers Paulette's romance and a gallery seat; the spa mud-room nudity gag (pp.81–82) delivers the pool-boy motive; the David slap (pp.85–87) delivers Rule 3:03. **The only set-piece that does not advance the plot is the Dylan McDermott car chase (p.22)** — the exception that proves the rule, and the one gag the released film also treats as garnish.
- **Two montages that answer each other.** pp.19–23: three months of performed competence, built around a video made for an audience, with a practice score of 143 in the middle of it. pp.51–54: the same duration of real competence, built with nobody watching, with a broken nail typed through. The first gets her admitted; the second gets her respected.
- **Bookended orientation quads.** p.26 ("Class of 2003" banners, club tables, a red-shirted 2L handing her a packet, "Malibu Barbie lives") and pp.116–117 ("Class of 2004" banners, the same pan across the same club tables, Elle now in the red 2L shirt behind her own). The Act Two entrance and the Final Image are the same location one year apart, which is why the ending needs no thesis statement.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework
- **The crisis apparatus is late and tiny.** All Is Lost at 85% (prescribed 68%), Dark Night two pages long (prescribed ten), Break into Three at 88% (prescribed 77%), Finale 12 pages (prescribed 25). The script gets away with it on three conditions, all of which are worth generalising: the crisis is **single-cause** (one man, one gesture); the **solution is pre-staged** in the scene immediately after (Brooke's loss of faith in Donovan, pp.103–104); and the audience has already watched the hero exhaust her options across Act Two, so the "no way out" work is done before the beat arrives.
- **"Bad Guys Close In" is a rising-success sequence.** From 54% to 83% Elle is winning almost continuously. Snyder's model expects deterioration here. The film substitutes a different pressure — *nobody in authority credits the wins* — so the section builds resentment rather than jeopardy, and the trapdoor at p.100 converts all of it retroactively. This is a legitimate variant the KB should name: **poison the achievement rather than remove it.**
- **Theme Stated is dramatised on page 5 and not spoken until page 60.** A defensible violation. The audience is given the proof at 5% and the thesis at 51%, which is precisely when they need language for what they have been watching.
- **No literal whiff of death.** The death is professional and identity-level: the mentor dies, the alliance dies, and the heroine declares her own competence a fraud ("It turns out, I am a joke," p.103). The KB's "whiff of death" rule should be read as *something the hero built must be seen to die*, not as a corpse quota.
- **The rom-com's reconciliation beat is given to the wrong man and then refused.** Warner delivers the canonical Act Three declaration at p.113 — "I'm an idiot... I want to be with you, Elle. Forever" — the script has Elle *melt* on the page ("a trace of the old love in her eyes"), lets her say the line back, and only then converts it into a wrist-lock: "In court. On opposing sides." Playing the beat straight before destroying it is what makes the refusal a payoff rather than a punchline.
- **The real love object is her own competence, and the script staging proves it.** The union beat is the reaction two-shot at p.112 over Chutney's confession; the kiss at p.116 is smaller, shorter, and comically framed ("I'm not just some man-toy you can show off like a trophy"). A rom-com that spends 1.5 pages on its romantic climax and five on its cross-examination has told you what it is.
- **The rival gets the full romance.** Sarah receives every canonical beat — meet-hostile, escalation, respect, olive branch, misunderstanding, apology, union, transformation — while Emmett receives almost none. If you were beat-sheeting this script blind, you would identify Sarah as the love interest.
- **The heroine does not change, which is the genre test.** She wins in pink, with bubble gum, using sorority knowledge, and the final image measures the *institution's* conversion (Sarah blonde, a queue of 1Ls signing up). That disqualifies **Rites of Passage** (whose resolution is acceptance of a life change) and confirms **Fool Triumphant**: the Fool (Elle), the Establishment (Harvard Law and the East Coast blue-blood class), the transmutation/new name (the navy suits at p.62, and "Delta. Gamma." as her jail alias at p.74), and the Insider who knows her from before and sells her out (Warner: "You're not smart enough," p.49). The **Golden Fleece** claim is real but partial: the road ends at 21%, and what survives of the genre is its prize-swap — the stated fleece (Warner) is not the fleece she brings home. The first 25 pages *are* Rites of Passage (heartbreak, mourning, denial); the graft is deliberate and the seam is the Boxster on p.25.
- **Draft-vs-film differences (significant).** This shooting draft is **not** the release cut, and the divergences are all in the third act: the rival is **Sarah Knottingham**, not Vivian Kensington; Emmett is **Kerrigan**, not Richmond; the professor who assaults her is **Donovan**, not Callahan; and there is **no graduation sequence, no valedictorian speech and no epilogue title cards**. The film's final image — Elle at a podium articulating the theme ("you must always have faith in yourself") plus cards resolving Paulette's marriage, Vivian's friendship and Emmett's proposal — replaces this draft's ending, which is a courthouse-steps kiss followed by a wordless institutional rhyme (the Blonde Legal Defense Club table). **The draft's ending is structurally tighter and thematically mute; the film's is structurally looser and thematically explicit.** Worth teaching as a straight trade-off: the released version buys clarity and ensemble closure with an added scene and four title cards, and pays for it by stating aloud what the club table shows. This draft also leaves Donovan entirely unpunished beyond "pathetic asshole," and has Sarah *witness and misread* the assault rather than be told about it — a better mechanism, since it manufactures the p.114 apology.
- **Loose ends in this draft.** Margot's "lucky scrunchie" (p.18) is planted and never paid. Elle's parents disappear from p.25 to a single TV cutaway at p.112. Enid is a running gag rather than an arc. And the script carries two alternate "(TV VERSION)" dialogue substitutions in the body text (pp.13 and 25) — a shooting-draft artefact, not a structural feature.

## Lessons for the knowledge base
1. **Build the plot's lock to fit the hero's despised key.** The Windham case is reverse-engineered from Elle's disqualification: the alibi is a beauty secret (p.75), the false witness is exposed by a fashion tell (p.94), the murder is solved by perm chemistry (p.110). If the climax can be won by anyone else in the cast, the plot is not yet the theme.
2. **Dramatise the theme on page 5; state it at the midpoint.** The rayon takedown (p.6) proves the thesis before the audience knows there is one; Stromwell's "an image and a good hook can get you into a room, but something has to keep you in that room" (p.60) gives them language for it exactly when they need it. Snyder's page-5 rule survives this violation intact — what he actually requires is that the theme be *present* by page 5, not spoken.
3. **The Save the Cat beat should use the same faculty as the climax.** Elle rescues Amy at p.3 with social engineering; she wins the trial at p.110 with the same faculty. A likeability beat that draws on an unrelated virtue is a tax; one that draws on the climactic skill is a setup.
4. **Poison the achievement rather than remove it.** The All Is Lost (p.100) leaves every success intact and destroys her belief that she earned any of it. This is why "Bad Guys Close In" can be played as a winning streak: nothing has to be taken away, because one gesture can retroactively re-price forty pages.
5. **A late, compressed crisis is affordable if it is single-cause and pre-solved.** All Is Lost at 85%, Dark Night of two pages, Break into Three of one line — survivable because the cause is one man's hand and the solution (Brooke's distrust of her attorney) is planted in the very next scene. Compress the crisis, never the setup for its solution.
6. **Put the resurrection in the least prestigious room in the movie.** Stromwell delivering the Break into Three from a nail-drying chair in a strip-mall salon (p.103) collapses the film's two worlds into one image, and does in eleven words what a mentor speech would need a page for. Choose the *location* of the turn as carefully as its content.
7. **When the goal changes, don't announce it.** Warner is silently replaced by self-worth at p.51 ("You're about to see just how valuable Elle Woods can be") and never argued about again. The audience revises the goal automatically; a scene explaining the revision would flatten it, and would make the p.113 refusal predictable.
8. **A comedy set-piece must be cashed at least twice to pay for its page count.** The Bend & Snap (pp.69–71) costs a page and a half of pure spectacle and returns a broken nose (p.92), a romance (p.97) and a visual gag in the courtroom gallery (p.107). One payoff would not have justified the detour at 60%.
9. **Give the rival the complete romance beat sheet.** Sarah gets meet-hostile → escalation → respect → olive branch → betrayal → apology → union → transformation (pp.33–117), while the actual love interest gets a bench, an argument and a kiss. In a story about a woman's competence, routing the emotional architecture through the female antagonist is what keeps the ending from being about a man.
10. **In a Fool Triumphant, the Final Image measures the world, not the hero.** Elle is unchanged — pink, blonde, sorority-fluent, chewing gum in court. What has changed is the institution: the same quad, the same club tables, one year later, with her banner among them and the rival gone blonde (pp.116–117). If your protagonist's virtue is that she was right all along, your last image must show the room admitting it.
