# Raiders of the Lost Ark (1981) — Structural Beat Map
**Source:** /mnt/user-data/uploads/Screenplay planning/Ressources/Script Library/Feature Misc/raiders-of-the-lost-ark-1981.pdf · **Writers:** "Screenplay by Lawrence Kasdan / Story by George Lucas" (title page — this draft credits **Lucas alone** for story; Philip Kaufman's co-story credit is not on it) · **Format:** feature · **Genre:** action-adventure (serial pastiche / archaeological quest) · **Length:** 104 printed pp (105 PDF pp; title page unnumbered) — **REVISED THIRD DRAFT, AUGUST 1979**, Medway Productions; 160 numbered scenes of which 10 are marked OMIT
**Framework lens(es):** PRIMARY — the eight-sequence method (Frank Daniel / Gulino), Raiders as the canonical action/adventure sequence exemplar. SECONDARY 1 — set-piece architecture (goal / escalating obstacle / reversal / cost, and how location–tempo–antagonist rotation defeats monotony). SECONDARY 2 — the passive-protagonist critique, stated fairly and tested against the page.

Page citations are the script's **printed** page numbers (top-right of each page). Offset: printed page = PDF page − 1. Percentages are of 104 printed pages. **Note:** printed p.53 is absent from this PDF — scenes 79–80 are replaced by a placeholder reading `PAGE 53 MISSING`, so roughly one page (the arrival at Sallah's and the introduction of the old scholar Amir) is unreadable here.

## Logline
In 1936, a mercenary archaeologist is hired by Army Intelligence to beat Hitler to the Ark of the Covenant — a race that forces him back to the woman he ruined ten years earlier, and toward a prize that no human being, himself included, is equipped to control.

## Structure overview
This is a **relay structure**: the film is a chain of self-contained set-pieces, each with its own terrain, antagonist and tool, strung on one object that changes hands six times. The second act is nearly wall-to-wall action, and the script's answer to monotony is not variation of intensity but **rotation of four variables at once** — where (jungle / museum / bar / bazaar / tomb / airstrip / mountain road / open sea / cavern), against whom (booby traps / a samurai / a torturer / a mob / snakes / mechanics / a convoy / the ocean / God), at what tempo (the map room and the periscope ride are near-silent), and with what implement (whip / gun / brains / torch / fists / horse / bazooka).

The engine is possession. Track the Ark-shaped object and you have the architecture: Indy has the idol (p.8) → Belloq takes it (p.10) → Indy has the headpiece halves (pp.25, 41) → Indy has the Ark (p.66) → the Nazis take it (p.68) → Indy takes it back (p.77) → the Nazis take it at sea (p.84) → Indy takes it back (p.96) → the U.S. government takes it (p.103). Nine transfers, no repeats of method.

Major turns: **Act One curtain p.20 (19%)** — "They want you to go for it. And they'll pay." **Midpoint p.52 (50%)** — Indy drunk opposite Belloq, believing Marion dead, told "I am a shadowy reflection of you." **Act Two curtain p.85 (82%)** — the Nazis take the Ark and Marion off the Bantu Wind and sail away; Indy is officially dead. **Climax p.94 (90%)** — the Ark is opened and Belloq is destroyed, *with Indy off-screen*.

## Eight-sequence pass
Total 104 pp. Act One = sequences 1–2 (pp.1–20). Act Two = sequences 3–6 (pp.20–85). Act Three = sequences 7–8 (pp.86–104). Sequence lengths run 9–19 pp — deliberately uneven, because this script's units are tension units, not page counts.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

The audience now knows the hero can lose, knows his rival, and has been handed the phobia that the treasure will be buried in |

The hero wins the woman and loses the object to his own government |

**What the pass shows.** Sequences 1, 3, 4, 6 and 7 all end with the hero holding *less* than when he started; only 2, 5 and 8 end up. The film's forward drive comes not from accumulating wins but from a metronomic **grab / lose / re-grab** pulse applied to one object, with the interval shortening as the script proceeds (idol held 2 pp, headpiece held 20 pp, Ark held 2 pp, then 8 pp, then 5 pp). Every sequence also ends on a *physical handover* — an object changing hands, a door sealing, a vehicle leaving — never on a decision. That is what makes an eight-sequence structure feel like a serial rather than a drama.

**How repetition is avoided.** Eleven major sequences and no two share a *combination*. Terrain rotates jungle → interior museum → interior bar → open-air maze → sealed puzzle room → tomb → airfield → mountain road → open ocean → cavern → tunnel. Antagonist rotates architecture → a swordsman → a torturer → a crowd → the clock → animals → machinery → an army → the sea → a deity → fire. Tempo rotates: two of the eleven (map room, periscope) have almost no cutting and no dialogue, and both are placed immediately *before* the two biggest kinetic sequences, so the film's loudest moments are preceded by its quietest. And Indy's tool is different every time — whip, revolver, brain, torch, fists, a horse, a bazooka he never fires, a bayonet, a stolen machine gun.

## Storylines
- **A — The Ark hunt (pp.14–104).** Objective, external, and deliberately handed off between two teams rather than pursued by one. It is structured as a **relay of partial information**: the Nazis have the site, Indy has the medallion's extra line, Belloq has the map-room calculation, Sallah has access, Amir has the language. Nobody can proceed alone, which is why the two sides keep colliding rather than diverging.
- **B — Indy & Marion (pp.26–41, 60–62, 68–71, 81–83, 95–96, 103).** A repair story, not a courtship: the debt is stated on p.30 ("This is your handiwork" / "I was a child"), converted into a business partnership on p.41, tested by the choice to re-gag her on p.61, audited explicitly on p.82 ("Maybe we should consider all past accounts closed" / "No. Not yet."), consummated on p.82–83, and settled on p.103 when she out-ranks the MacGuffin. **This draft is materially warmer and franker than the film**: they sleep together, and Marion — not Indy — is the one who insists on taking the Ark out of the burning cavern (p.96).
- **C — Belloq as dark mirror (pp.10, 43, 48–52, 63, 67–69, 84–85, 91–94).** The most rigorously built line in the script. He is introduced *winning* (p.10), named as the Nazis' asset by Sallah (p.43), given the thesis speech at the exact midpoint (p.50: "I am a shadowy reflection of you… it would have taken only a nudge to make you the same as me"), shown as an outsider inside his own faction (p.63), given the one flicker of decency that separates him from the Nazis ("The girl was mine!", p.68; "Indiana Jones… adieu!", p.69), allowed to claim Marion as spoils (p.85), and finally destroyed by the thing he articulated on p.50 — the transmitter he wanted to answer. His death is written as an ecstasy, not a punishment: "If a death's-head can smile and look satisfied…" (p.94).
- **D — Sallah and the Egyptians (pp.41–44, 51–56, 60, 75–76, 80).** The comic/moral counterweight: a family, nine children, food, a wife who outranks him. Structurally he is the film's rescue mechanism — the children extract Indy from Belloq (p.51), Sallah supplies the flag-rope (p.60) and the vanishing garage (p.80) — and the voice that states the theme the hero refuses to hear (p.44).

**Convergence points.** p.44 (A meets C: the completed headpiece and the news that Belloq is at Tanis). p.52 (A, B and C in one scene: the Ark, Marion's death and Belloq's mirror). p.68 (all four: Indy and Marion in the pit, Belloq and Sallah above, the Ark on a crane). **p.85 is the total convergence** — Belloq takes the Ark *and* Marion *and* pronounces Indy dead in eleven lines. p.94–96 resolves C and B in successive scenes; A is resolved off-screen by a bureaucrat.

## Structural devices
- **Cold open as thesis (pp.1–11).** Not a teaser — a complete eleven-page film. It contains the hero's tool (p.4), his ethics (p.1), his expertise (p.7), his phobia (p.11), his rival (p.10), the shape of every trap in the movie, and a loss. Everything after p.11 is this sequence at larger scale: a booby-trapped sacred object, a partner who may betray, and a Frenchman waiting at the exit. The idol-for-sandbag switch (p.8) is a rehearsal of Belloq's ivory rod (p.94) — both men mis-price what they are lifting.
- **Travel as a single wipe.** The script's connective tissue is austere: `WIPE TO:` → one shot of an aircraft (p.20 Pan Am Clipper; p.26 DC-3) → `WIPE TO:` → arrival. There is no map, no red line, no montage in this draft — the famous animated route is a production answer to a script that simply refuses to spend pages on transit. Transitions are also used as punctuation: the only `FADE OUT / FADE IN` inside the body of the script is at p.88, marking the passage of a night on the periscope.
- **Exposition inside motion or inside vanity.** Two big information dumps, neither static. The lecture hall (pp.15–19) is a man showing off to an audience he wants to impress, with a prop, a blackboard sketch and a payoff image. Amir's translation (pp.53–55) is intercut with a monkey eating poisoned dates — so the scene that delivers the plot's key fact is simultaneously an assassination attempt and a running gag.
- **Planted / paid setups.** The two-piece key (p.16 briefing → p.25 Shanghai half → p.34 medallion reveal → p.43 fitted together → p.54 the line only the top half carries → p.58 the beam of light). The medallion burn (pp.39–40 → p.54 "their top section was blank" → p.63 the scar displayed in a Heil Hitler). "I can't stand snakes" (p.11 → p.64 → p.67). The whip: killing (p.4), bridge (p.6), duelling weapon (p.23), disarm (p.38), climbing rig (p.70), safety line (p.87), suspense object hanging in plain sight (pp.89–90), and finally a **cargo harness for the Ark** (p.96). "If it's God you want to talk to, maybe I can arrange it" (p.51) → "If you talk to Him, tell Him I'm on my way up" (p.93) → p.94.
- **The two-line cross-cut with a comic B-line.** The map room (pp.56–60) is the model: silent A-line (a man solving a puzzle correctly) against a B-line whose only job is to remove his exit, played entirely for laughs. The suspense is generated by the *comedy* — every joke costs the hero a resource.
- **The object as the clock.** No countdown is ever stated. Urgency is produced by possession changing hands, which is why the film never needs a bomb timer to feel fast.
- **The antagonist bloc split three ways.** Belloq (mysticism), Shliemann (career self-preservation), Belzig (sadism). Each is killed by a different agency in a different sequence — Belzig by physics (p.78), Belloq by the divine (p.94), Shliemann by fire (p.99) — so the finale doesn't have to service three deaths at once.
- **The hero stripped before the climax.** Gun taken (p.10 and again p.93), money burned (p.40), plane destroyed (p.74), hat lost "once and for all" (p.86), whip left hanging on a periscope (p.89), bazooka knocked away (p.93). The film systematically disarms its own icon so the last act is fought by a man rather than a costume.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework
**Draft vs. release film** — this Revised Third Draft differs from the finished picture in ways that matter structurally:
- **An entire missing sequence: Shanghai (pp.20–25).** General Tengtu Hok, the samurai duel, the gong. The film compresses the key into a single object held by Marion; here it is two halves, which pushes Marion's entrance to p.27 (26%) and gives Act Two-A a second set-piece.
- **The bazooka standoff is at the climax, not mid-film (pp.92–93)** — and Indy is simply overpowered rather than making the choice not to destroy the Ark. The release film's version is the more active writing.
- **The Ark is opened inside a silk Tabernacle in the Nazi cavern, and Indy is not there (p.94).** No mountaintop, no Marion tied to a post beside him, no "shut your eyes." Marion is discovered *afterwards*, spread-eagled behind the burning tent (p.95). The single most famous piece of protagonist agency in the film — the one line of knowledge that saves them both — does not exist in this draft.
- **A whole additional climax: the mine-car chase (pp.97–100)**, subsequently recycled as the centrepiece of *Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom*.
- **Toht is "Belzig" and dies in the truck chase (p.78)**, so the man branded by the medallion never reaches the finale; Dietrich is "Shliemann" and burns in a tunnel (p.99). Neither is melted.
- **Indy and Marion sleep together (pp.82–83)**; there is no drinking contest and no white dress in Belloq's tent — the white garment here is a nightgown on the Bantu Wind.
- **Missing gags:** the swordsman is kicked in the groin, not shot (p.47); "It's not the years, honey, it's the mileage" is absent; Indy is never dragged beneath the truck or shot in the arm.
- **Continuity artefact:** the hat is crushed by the boulder (p.9) yet recovered in the Raven fire (p.40).
- **Framework friction:** sequences 7 (17 pp) and 8 (19 pp) are oversized because each contains three movements under one question. A strict eight-sequence reading would want the midpoint at the end of sequence 4; here the midpoint (p.52) falls inside sequence 5. The script's units are tension units, not fifteen-page units — a useful warning against applying the method arithmetically.

**The passive-protagonist critique.**

*Stated fairly:* Indiana Jones has no effect on the outcome of his own film. Remove him and the Nazis still find Tanis, still excavate the Well of the Souls, still open the Ark, and are still annihilated by it. Every "win" he scores is cancelled within pages: he takes the idol and loses it (pp.8–10), lifts the Ark and hands it to Belloq (pp.66–68), takes the truck and loses the cargo at sea (pp.80–85), threatens the Ark with a bazooka and is disarmed (p.93). His expertise is not merely useless to the Allies — it is *actively useful to the enemy*, a point the script makes explicitly and gleefully in Belloq's line at p.68: "After all these years, it is most considerate of you to aid me in this way."

*Tested against this draft, the critique gets stronger in one place and collapses in three.*

**Where it gets stronger.** In the release film, Indy is present at the ceremony and survives by an act of knowledge ("shut your eyes"). In this draft he is being marched to a wall to be shot when the Ark opens (pp.93–94). He does not witness the climax, does not cause it, and is not endangered by it. At the film's decisive moment the protagonist is off-stage. That is the critique's best evidence anywhere in the franchise, and it is on this page.

**Where it collapses.**
1. **It measures the wrong dramatic question.** After p.85 the operative question is not "will the Ark be found" but "will Marion live." On that question Indy is 100% causal: he rides a submarine across the Mediterranean (pp.86–89), infiltrates the base, and cuts four bindings with a bayonet with a river of burning oil "only a foot from Marion" (p.95). Remove him and she burns. The script also gives him a second, earlier, purely physical save — he catches her when she is thrown thirty feet into the snake pit (p.68).
2. **He determines the time and place of the climax.** Because he burns the Flying Wing (p.74), the Ark travels by road. Because he hijacks the truck (pp.77–80), it travels by sea. Because he stows away, the Wurrfler makes for the island. Without Indy, Belloq opens the Ark **in Berlin, in front of Hitler**, in a controlled setting, having verified its contents in advance — the exact scenario Belloq talks Shliemann out of at p.90. The Ark's destructive power is a constant; where and on whom it discharges is entirely Indy's doing, and the difference between "it kills a field team on a Greek island" and "it kills the German high command" is not a rounding error.
3. **In this draft he is necessary for the Ark's recovery.** The Ark ends the film in Washington only because Indy runs back into a burning tent, lashes it to his whip and drags it into a mine car while the island detonates (pp.96–100). Left alone, it is buried under the cliff with everything else. The finished film weakened this (the Ark is simply recovered off-screen); the draft has him physically carry the object out.
4. **Passivity is not the same as non-causality.** Indy initiates every sequence in the script: he chooses Shanghai, chooses Nepal, chooses to buy rather than steal from Marion, chooses to re-gag her and leave her (p.61 — a decision with real consequences), chooses to dig in a different dune, chooses to make it up as he goes (p.77). There is not one scene in 104 pages where he waits.

*Verdict.* **Partly true at the climax; false about the film.** The accurate diagnosis is not a passive protagonist but a **decoupled climax** — the story's outcome is settled by an agency above the protagonist's, deliberately, because Sallah's warning on p.44 ("It is not something man was meant to disturb… It is not of this earth") is the film's actual thesis and Belloq's incineration is its proof. A hero who could control the Ark would refute the movie. The genuine craft failing in *this draft* is narrower and worth naming: having removed Indy from the room, Kasdan gives him nothing to *decide* at the decisive moment — the bazooka standoff, which should have been that decision, resolves by a man grabbing him from behind. The release film fixed it with two beats (put him on the mountain; give him the one piece of knowledge that saves them) at a total cost of about forty seconds of screen time. That is the transferable lesson: when your climax belongs to a force larger than your hero, you must buy his agency back somewhere inside the same scene.

## Lessons for the knowledge base
1. **Open with a complete miniature of the film that ends in defeat.** The Peru sequence (pp.1–11) demonstrates tool, ethic, expertise, phobia and rival, then takes the prize away. Losing the prologue purchases two hours of credible jeopardy; a hero who has already been beaten once can be threatened forever.
2. **Rotate four variables, not one.** Monotony in action isn't caused by too much action; it is caused by repeating a *combination*. Across eleven set-pieces this script never repeats terrain + antagonist + tempo + tool (see Set-piece anatomy). Two of the eleven — the map room (pp.56–60) and the periscope ride (pp.86–89) — have no antagonist and almost no dialogue, and both sit immediately before the loudest sequences.
3. **Make possession, not a countdown, the clock.** The object changes hands nine times in 104 pages and the intervals shorten. No deadline is ever stated aloud, yet nothing sags — because the audience always knows who is holding the thing and how recently that changed.
4. **Put exposition where a character is either showing off or in motion.** Five pages of Ark history survive at pp.15–19 because Indy is performing for an audience he wants to impress; the plot's single decisive fact (pp.53–55) survives because it is intercut with a monkey being poisoned. Load everything the audience will need *before* the set-piece that uses it, so that set-piece can play silent (p.58).
5. **Cross-cut a silent puzzle against a comic disaster.** The map room is the template: A-line, a man succeeding in silence; B-line, a friend being comically conscripted into removing the A-line's escape route. Each laugh costs the hero a resource, so comedy and suspense are the same curve.
6. **Bury the treasure in the hero's specific fear.** "I can't stand snakes" (p.11) is a throwaway laugh 53 pages before the Well of the Souls (p.64). The vault of the object should be the vault of the man — it converts a physical obstacle into a character test at zero narrative cost.
7. **Let the antagonist say the theme, then be killed by it.** Belloq states the film's thesis at the exact midpoint ("I am a shadowy reflection of you… It's a transmitter. A radio for talking to God!", pp.50–51) and is destroyed at p.94 by precisely the thing he described. A dark mirror that articulates the argument spares the hero from having to.
8. **Split the antagonist bloc into competing appetites.** Belloq wants transcendence, Shliemann wants Berlin's approval, Belzig wants to hurt people (pp.62–63). They obstruct each other (p.68: "The girl was mine!"), they can be killed in three different sequences by three different agencies, and the hero gets to exploit the seams.
9. **Strip the hero of his icons before the finale.** Gun, money, plane, hat "once and for all" (p.86), whip left dangling on a periscope (p.89), bazooka (p.93). The last act should be fought by the person, not the costume.
10. **When the climax belongs to a force above the protagonist, buy his agency back inside the same scene.** This draft is the negative proof: remove Indy from the room where the Ark is opened (p.94) and the whole passive-protagonist critique becomes fair comment. Two small beats — presence and one piece of decisive knowledge — are the entire difference between a divine climax and an absent hero.
