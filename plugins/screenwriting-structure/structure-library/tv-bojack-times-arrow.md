# BoJack Horseman 4x11 "Time's Arrow" (2017) — Structural Beat Map
**Source:** /mnt/user-data/uploads/Screenplay planning/Ressources/Script Library/TV Misc/bojack-horseman-411-times-arrow-2017.pdf · **Writer:** Kate Purdy (title page: "Written by Kate Purdy") · **Format:** animated TV episode (Netflix half-hour, adult animation), season 4 episode 11 of 12, production #BJH 411 · **Genre:** tragedy / character study in comic register — a dementia POV piece inside a satirical animated comedy · **Length:** 39 printed pp (40 PDF pp; unnumbered title page = PDF p.1, so printed p.N = PDF p.N+1); **TABLE DRAFT, November 30, 2016**; Final Draft 9
**Framework lens(es):** PRIMARY — subjective/unreliable structure in animation: the episode is staged from inside Beatrice's dementia, and the medium's licence to dissolve a body, a room or a person mid-shot is used as load-bearing architecture. SECONDARY 1 — the spotlight/POV episode as a late-season form that retroactively re-prices the whole series. SECONDARY 2 — non-linear memory architecture: the actual chronology of a life versus the order a failing mind serves it in, and the associative rule governing each jump.

Page citations use the script's printed page numbers, which sit top-right in a running header on every page ("BoJack Horseman / 'Time's Arrow' — Table Draft — #411 — 11/30/16 — N."). Percentages are of 39 printed pages.

**Format conventions found.** This is a **master-scene script indistinguishable in layout from live-action** — no panel numbers, no shot lists, no storyboard or animatic notation. Animation shows up in four places only: (1) species designations on character introduction — `CLEMELIA BLOODSWORTH (a goose)`, `CORBIN CREAMERMAN (24, white goat)`, `JAN BRONSON (a Swedish human)`, `A COCKATIEL IN A CHEAP SUIT`; (2) action lines that state physical impossibilities as flat fact ("her corset turns into a beautiful ball gown and the background changes to:"); (3) `EXT. WHITE SPACE` as a legitimate location; (4) one writer-to-reader aside inside an action line ("The baby looks like Hollyhock, because it is.", p.35). Act architecture is the Netflix-animation four-part shape, all breaks printed: **COLD OPEN** (ends p.3) / **ACT ONE** (pp.4–22) / **ACT TWO** (pp.23–31) / **ACT THREE** (pp.32–39), closing on `END OF EPISODE`. Sluglines carry a **mandatory year stamp** — `INT. BEATRICE'S BEDROOM - 1947 - NIGHT` — which is the episode's entire orientation system, and its most important exception is the very first slugline, `INT./EXT. BOJACK'S CAR - DAY`, which has no year at all. In 39 pages there are exactly **two formal transitions**: one `CUT TO:` (p.17) and one `TIME DISSOLVE TO:` (p.24). Every other cut — including jumps of 53 years — is unmarked, because inside Beatrice there is no such thing as a transition.

> **Draft caveat, stated up front.** This is the **table draft**, roughly ten months before broadcast. Several devices the aired episode is famous for are **not in this document**: there are no scribbled-out faces, no on-screen name-substitution supers, no lima beans, no "Honeydew," no "I see you" refrain, and no Beatrice voice-over. What the draft contains is the *logic* those devices were later built from — a distortion system already fully architected in prose, and a character list in which people Beatrice does not value are stored as job titles. Every claim below is cited to this document; where the aired version diverges it is flagged rather than assumed.

## Logline
On the drive to the nursing home where her son is dumping her, a woman with dementia falls out of the present and back through her own life — a scarlet fever at seven, a debutante ball at twenty-three, a marriage she talked herself into, a son she resented, and a maid whose baby she took — until the memory she has spent seventy years refusing surfaces and explains all of it, and her son, at the door, decides to lie to her kindly.

## Structure overview
The episode has no goal-obstacle spine and no dramatic question in the ordinary sense. Its spine is **a subjective transit**: Beatrice enters a car in 2017 (p.1), leaves it as a seven-year-old (p.3), and does not return to the present for thirty-four pages. What organises those pages is not plot but **the escalating failure of the machinery that separates one memory from another** — first a name is wrong, then the image agrees with the wrong name, then her body is twenty years younger, then rooms stop existing between scenes, then a sentence finishes itself six years later, then a face in 1947 and a face in 2017 are the same drawing.

Three things make it hold. First, the chronology is **overwhelmingly forward**: pp.3–35 run 1947 → 1963 → 1964 → 1970 → 1999 → 2000 in strict order, with exactly one puncture (2017, pp.15–16). Beatrice's own thesis line — "Time's arrow neither stands still nor reverses, after all. It merely marches forward" (p.2) — is literally true of the episode's architecture for 32 of its 39 pages. Second, the arrow **reverses exactly twice, both inside the last four pages** (the 1964 birth intercut, p.35; the 1947 doll-burning, pp.36–37), and both reversals do the same job: they supply the cause of the act the audience has just watched. Third, the present-day frame is rationed to **5.5 pages of 39 (~14%)**, split into three appearances — the drive (pp.1–3), the check-in (pp.15–16), the goodbye (pp.37–39) — and BoJack's entire arc across those five pages is cruelty → transaction → mercy.

The act lengths are conspicuously lopsided: Act One is 19 pages (49% of the episode), Acts Two and Three are 9 and 8. The imbalance tracks **novelty, not duration**. Material the series has never shown (1947 childhood, the 1963 courtship) gets scenes; material the series has already mined for four seasons (BoJack's childhood, the loveless house) gets a photo montage (p.23), a time dissolve (p.24) and a time-lapse (pp.26–27). Beatrice's memory allocates screen time by damage, not by calendar.

## The distortion ledger
Escalation scale used below: **L1 — elision/compression** (memory doing what memory normally does: skipping, dissolving, flashing); **L2 — substitution** (a person, place or line replaced by another); **L3 — dissolution** (space, body or identity fails outright); **L4 — fabrication** (a memory authored from outside her).

**What the shape means.** Strip out the three present-day segments and the remaining 33.5 pages are in **perfect chronological order** from 1947 to 2000 — except for two reversals, both in the final four pages. The episode is not a shuffle; it is a straight line with a frame around it and two deliberate breaks at the end. Beatrice's thesis ("It merely marches forward," p.2) is architecturally *true* for 85% of the running time, which is exactly what makes the two reversals land: the arrow bends only when the story needs to explain itself, and it bends backwards twice in a row — 2000 → 1964 (p.35), then 2000 → 1947 (p.36) — each time to supply the cause of the act just witnessed. The pattern is: **watch the effect, then travel to the cause.** The last reversal reaches the furthest back and explains the most.

## Storylines

**A — Beatrice's life (pp.3–37, ~87% of the episode).** 1947 to 2000, in order. Its through-line is not an ambition or a relationship but **an object**: the doll, which enters unexplained on p.3, is tucked under her arm on p.5, dangles from her fist as she is carried in on p.7, is hugged in front of the fire on p.8, flashes on p.21 at the moment she refuses an abortion, and is finally thrown into the fire by her father on p.37. The A-story is the answer to "why did this woman become this?" and its answer is a single scene, withheld until 92%.

**B — the present-day frame with BoJack (pp.1–3, 15–16, 37–39; 5.5 pp, ~14%).** Three appearances and a complete arc: **cruelty** ("a glorious magical place where they'll lock you in a room by yourself so you can't hurt anyone ever again," p.2), **transaction** ("Can I pay for the next five years in a lump sum now so I don't have to think about her again?", p.16), **mercy** (the Michigan speech, pp.38–39). The B-story never has a scene to itself longer than two pages; it is heard rather than watched — a pre-lap (p.28), a line in someone else's mouth (p.15), a voice behind a door. Its function is to be the pressure that keeps forcing the seam in Beatrice's memory open.

**C — Henrietta (pp.27, 30–36; ~7 pp).** She is introduced twice, and the first introduction is the cruellest structural joke in the script: she arrives as **the last item in a montage of interchangeable maids** (p.27), i.e. as furniture. Then she is named, then she is fired, then she is pregnant, then she is a mirror. Her scenes contain no interiority — Beatrice never asks her a personal question that isn't instrumental. She is the only character other than Beatrice who is shown giving birth, and the only one whose child is taken.

**Convergence.** All three lock in a four-page window, pp.35–39. C completes (Hollyhock handed to a nameless woman, p.36) → A's origin is revealed one paragraph later (the doll burned, pp.36–37) → the match-cut on a haunted expression drops us into B (p.37) → and B resolves by taking over A's own machinery (pp.38–39). The convergence is engineered so that each storyline's ending *causes* the next storyline's ending: Beatrice loses Hollyhock, which retrieves the doll, which produces the expression, which is the cut, which delivers her to her son.

The cross-storyline rhyme that carries the whole design: **"This is for your own good"** (Beatrice to Henrietta, p.35) → **"It all must be destroyed - for your own good"** (Joseph to Beatrice, p.36). Storyline A supplies the source code for storyline C, one page after C has finished running it.

## Structural devices

**Names as attention.** The draft has no scribbled faces, but it has the same idea implemented in the cast list. People Beatrice values get names (Joseph, Honey, Clemelia, Corbin, Butterscotch, Henrietta, BoJack). People she doesn't are stored as **functions**: `MAID`, `ORDERLY`, `DOCTOR`, `HOUSE STAFF`, `A SERVANT`, `GIRL #1`, `GIRL #2`, `A MAN IN A TUXEDO`, `A COCKATIEL IN A CHEAP SUIT`, and — at the exact moment she gives away her husband's daughter — `A NICE, OFFICIAL LOOKING WOMAN` (p.36). Naming policy is characterisation of the point-of-view character, and it is what the aired episode later converts into a visual: a face is scribbled out for the same reason a character is called "a nice, official looking woman." The one genuine name-substitution in the draft runs the other way — a person she *does* know is overwritten by another person she knows (BoJack → Henrietta, p.2), which is worse than anonymity: it is misfiling.

**Books as chapter cards, and the cards burned in front of us.** The episode's three time-jumps are announced by the titles of books Beatrice happens to be reading: "**THE NEXT MORNING**" (p.5), "**Two Weeks Later**" (p.17), "**Several Months Later**" (p.32). Each is literally accurate to the elapsed time. On p.36 a servant "picks up a pile of books: 'The Next Morning', 'Two Weeks Later', etc." and throws them into the fire. **The episode's editorial grammar turns out to be made of the objects that were taken from her.** The device is set up three times across 27 pages before the reveal, and it costs nothing — it is hidden inside props.

**Fire as the retrieval index.** Fire appears in the preview flash (p.2), smoulders in the 1947 bedroom (pp.5, 7), is the transition vehicle from 1947 to 1963 (p.8), intrudes on the abortion conversation (p.21), appears three times in the seven-image storm (p.31), and is the site of the withheld memory (pp.36–37). Nothing else in the episode recurs at that density. It is the mind's file index — every path back to the original wound runs through a flame.

**Ice cream (the taste of the life not lived).** The chain is three links long and entirely load-bearing. Joseph pitches the Sugarman/Creamerman merger and "all the free iced cream you could... uh, serve to other people" (p.9). Corbin — the man who shares her father-problem and would have been kind to her — explains that he wants to prove "**nature's way is actually the best way**" (p.19), a sentence away from being the only real conversation in the episode, before the pregnancy interrupts it mid-line. Thirty-seven years later, in a memory that never happened, the last thing BoJack gives her is "**Vanilla ice cream**" (p.39) — all-natural, Corbin's ambition, the flavour of the marriage she refused. "Can you taste the ice cream, Mom?" is the episode's final question, and the fact that she can is both the mercy and the diagnosis. *(No lima beans and no "Honeydew" appear anywhere in this draft; Honey Sugarman is present as an unspeaking figure on pp.6, 7, 16 and 29 only.)*

**Stories, and who is allowed to tell them.** The episode's real spine. Beatrice is talked into her entire life by a story told in the third person — "Did you ever hear the story of the couple who moved to California?" (pp.21–22) — and accepts it by joining in ("Isn't that how the story goes?"). At six, her son is ordered to produce one on demand: "Mommy's tired, BoJack. Tell me a story" (p.25). In 1999 she renounces the form: "It's just a bunch of silly stories... I, for one, am through with silly stories. Lot of good they ever did" (p.30) — the same conversation in which BoJack defends them ("Some people like silly stories. They find them comforting"). And on pp.38–39, he tells her one. The four beats are spaced across 18 pages and constitute the episode's actual argument.

**Recognition, in place of the aired "I see you."** This draft's recognition motif runs on *seeing* and then on *tasting*. Butterscotch: "You will see. I'm writing the next Great American Novel" (p.14, answering her "I see"). BoJack: "Best of luck. **See ya never**" (p.37) — the line that, spoken as he leaves, is what finally wakes her. Then "... Who is that?" / "**BoJack? Is that you?**" (pp.37–38). And when sight is exhausted, the last verification offered is a taste: "Can you taste the ice cream, Mom?" (p.39). The character who has spent an episode unable to recognise anyone is asked, in her last line, to confirm a sensation instead of a fact — and does.

**The final reveal and the last line.** The Hollyhock reveal is delivered in an action line, not a performance: "The baby looks like Hollyhock, **because it is**" (p.35) — flat, to the reader, over Beatrice's head. That deliberate POV break is what lets the *emotional* climax stay hers one page later. The last line — "**Oh, BoJack. It's so delicious.**" — is the only moment in 39 pages where Beatrice is happy in the present tense, and it is false. The episode ends by giving its protagonist exactly what it has spent the whole running time proving she cannot have.

**The double bookend.** p.2: "Are we going to the lake house?" / "No, we're going to a glorious magical place where they'll lock you in a room by yourself." pp.38–39: "Where am I?" / "You're in Michigan. At the old vacation house." Same question, same speaker, opposite answer. The 36 pages in between are what changes his mind, and he never says so.

## Deviations & tensions with the framework

**This is a subjective structure, not an unreliable one.** The distinction matters for the knowledge base. Nothing Beatrice shows us is later contradicted; there is no Rashomon effect, no second version, no revelation that a scene was false. What the episode distorts is **form** — transitions, geography, casting, chronology — while leaving **content** intact. The audience never doubts that Joseph burned the doll or that Beatrice took Hollyhock. Compare the KB's other unreliable-POV entry, *The Father*, which distorts facts to induce the audience's confusion; "Time's Arrow" distorts *editing* to induce the audience's empathy. Subjective ≠ unreliable, and the two produce opposite audience states.

**The POV is not airtight, and the leaks are deliberate.** Three places break Beatrice's perspective: the p.35 action line "because it is" (an authorial aside to the reader); the two-way intercut of the births (pp.35–36), which requires an editorial intelligence Beatrice does not have; and the 1999 living-room scene (pp.28–30), where BoJack's jokes land normally ("That actually is a legitimate reason to get a divorce") rather than degrading the way the cold open's dialogue does. The episode also spends its first full page in **BoJack's** POV — his fury, his car, his reaction shots — and only defects on p.2. That page is the price of admission: the audience is handed the familiar viewpoint before it is taken away, and the pan to Henrietta is felt as a theft rather than a premise.

**What a viewer without series context loses.** A great deal, and all of it at the ends rather than the middle. (1) "Henrietta" in the cold open is just a senile misnaming — a newcomer cannot know it is a confession, and the payoff at p.27 lands as a coincidence rather than a return. (2) "because it is" (p.35) is inert without Hollyhock, introduced in 4x01; the season's central mystery is the only reason that line is a bomb. (3) The 1999 living-room scene (pp.28–30) is a replay of a dynamic built over three seasons; cold, it reads as a generic bad-mother scene. (4) Honey's lobotomy is one clause of one joke (p.6) and assumes 4x02 entirely. (5) The Michigan lake house (pp.38–39) assumes the audience has seen it. (6) "You'd better be worth all of this" / "Well, you're not" (pp.24–25) is a *quotation* of the series' most notorious moment; a newcomer gets a good cut where a viewer gets a detonation.

**Is it self-sufficient?** Partly — and the split is instructive. Pages 3–37 work as a standalone short film: a girl is humiliated, told not to cry, has her comfort object burned "for her own good," grows into a woman who does the same thing to another woman's child. That thirty-four-page arc needs no context. What is **not** self-sufficient is the frame and the reveal: the cold open, the check-in, the Hollyhock line and the ending all draw on series capital. The episode is therefore best classified as **a self-contained tragedy wearing a series-dependent frame** — which is precisely what a spotlight episode should be, since the frame is what makes it an episode rather than a short.

**Why 4x11 and not earlier.** Four preconditions, none optional. (i) **The season asked a question this episode answers** — Hollyhock's parentage is the 4x01 setup; the episode is the answer, so it cannot precede it. (ii) **The dementia must already be established** as comedy before it can be revealed as architecture; the audience has laughed at Beatrice's confusion for ten episodes. (iii) **Accumulated contempt is the raw material** — the sympathy generated here is a payoff on three and a half seasons of the audience hating this woman; run at 1x11 it would be a sad backstory, run at 4x11 it is a reversal of the audience's own judgement. (iv) **Placement at 11 of 12** hands the finale an audience that knows something BoJack doesn't.

**What it changes about every prior episode.** Retroactively, Beatrice stops being a source of cruelty and becomes a *conduit* for it: her signature lines acquire origin scenes ("I should have married Corbin Creamerman," p.26; "You'd better be worth all of this," p.24), and her register — the ornate, Latinate contempt — is revealed as inherited twice over, from Clemelia's playground diction (pp.4–5) and Joseph's rhyming couplets (p.36). BoJack's childhood flashbacks across four seasons are re-priced as **peripheral vision**: the time-lapse on pp.26–27 shows his entire boyhood as background motion in a room where a different destruction was happening. And the show's governing question — whether people can change — acquires a new, worse form: Beatrice does not choose to be her father, she is *quoting* him, and the episode's structure is the proof, because the quotation (p.35) is shown before its source (p.36).

**Where the table draft diverges from the aired episode.** Flagged rather than smoothed over: no scribbled faces, no on-screen name substitution, no Beatrice narration, no "I see you," no lima beans, no "Honeydew." Everything in this document is prose architecture; the aired version's visual signature was layered on top of a structure that was already complete. That is itself a finding — **the distortion system is fully specified at script stage in transitions, sluglines and character names, before any drawing exists.**

## Lessons for the knowledge base

1. **Make the POV handover an event, not a premise.** Spend a page in the familiar viewpoint first. "Time's Arrow" opens in BoJack's fury and BoJack's car (p.1), then defects mid-sentence on a pan (p.2). The audience must *feel* the camera change hands, or the subjective world reads as a stylistic given rather than a violation.

2. **Give the subjective world exactly one control shot.** The rearview mirror (p.3) is the only unmediated view of Beatrice's real body in 39 pages, and she strikes it away. One shot of the truth calibrates every distortion after it and pre-empts the audience's "is any of this real?" — which is the question that kills subjective structures.

3. **Escalate distortion in stages, and make each stage a different faculty.** Word (p.1, she misnames him) → image (p.2, the frame agrees) → body (p.2, she is twenty years younger) → space (pp.3–4, rooms render on demand) → grammar (pp.24–25, a sentence spans six years) → identity (p.37, one face for two eras). Escalation by *category* rather than by intensity keeps a subjective episode from plateauing at minute ten.

4. **Build your transition grammar out of diegetic props, then destroy them.** The three chapter cards are books she is holding (pp.5, 17, 32); on p.36 they are thrown in the fire. When the mechanism the audience has been using to navigate turns out to be an object from the trauma, form becomes content at zero narrative cost.

5. **Cut on cause, never on time.** Every one of the ~23 jumps in this episode has a stated trigger — a word (p.15), an object (p.24), a gesture (p.10), an emotion (p.33), a phrase repeated verbatim (p.36). Associative structure without a legible trigger per jump reads as randomness; with one, it reads as a mind.

6. **Reverse chronology only where reversal explains.** 32 of 39 pages run strictly forward. The arrow bends exactly twice — to the 1964 birth (p.35) and the 1947 fire (pp.36–37) — and both bends deliver the cause of the act just witnessed. A memory piece earns its reversals by rationing them; a shuffled timeline spends the same currency on every cut and has none left for the climax.

7. **Let a line finish itself across the cut.** "You'd better be worth all of this" (p.24) → "Well, you're not" (p.25) puts six years inside one sentence. The elision is simultaneously the joke, the wound and the exposition — three functions, one device, no dialogue spent explaining the gap.

8. **Store minor characters as job titles to model the protagonist's attention.** `A NICE, OFFICIAL LOOKING WOMAN` (p.36), `ORDERLY`, `GIRL #1`, `HOUSE STAFF`. Naming policy is free characterisation of the POV, it works on the page before any visual department touches it, and it scales directly into a visual device (the aired episode's scribbles). Conversely, overwriting a *known* person with another known person (BoJack → Henrietta, p.2) reads as guilt, not forgetting — choose which of the two you want.

9. **A spotlight episode should answer a question the season already asked.** Hollyhock is set up in 4x01 and paid at 4x11 in a single action line ("because it is," p.35). Placement is earned by the plot the character is standing on, not by how interesting the character is. The corollary: build the season-long question *before* you build the spotlight episode, or the episode will play as a detour.

10. **Generate sympathy by showing the source code, not by softening the behaviour.** Beatrice's worst act (pp.34–35) is staged in full, in tears, and is not excused; one page later her exact phrase — "for your own good" — is heard in her father's mouth (p.36). Show the quotation before the original and the audience does the forgiving without being asked.

11. **Hand the device to another character in the final beat.** For 37 pages Beatrice's failing memory owns the form. On pp.38–39 BoJack seizes it and authors a memory for her ("Can you taste the ice cream, Mom?"). The last move of a formal conceit should belong to someone other than its owner — that is how a structural gimmick becomes a character's decision.
