# Genre Contracts

A genre is not a marketing label. It is **a set of obligatory scenes plus a required emotional delivery** — a promise the audience formed before page one, which the structure must keep. Two failure modes:

- **Breach.** A promised scene is missing. The audience feels cheated and usually cannot say why. They report the script as "flat," "unsatisfying," "it just ended."
- **Blind breach.** The writer broke the contract without knowing it existed. This is the one to hunt. A knowing subversion is a craft move; an unknowing one is an absence.

Use this file to (a) name the contract, (b) audit the draft against it, (c) populate the **Genre contract** table in the structure spec. Sources: the 42 beat maps in `../../../structure-library/`, their cross-corpus findings as compiled in `measured-evidence.md`, and the genre canon. Claims tagged **[mapped]** are verified against a beat map; **[catalog]** means the script is in the library but not beat-mapped; untagged claims are craft-canon.

## 1. Save the Cat's ten story-DNA genres

Snyder's genres name a *structure*, not a shelf. Getting the genre wrong is a structural error that no amount of scene-level quality repairs — a Monster in the House paced like a Golden Fleece fails as a whole.

| Genre | Required elements | Structural consequence | Library example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Monster in the House** | A monster · a "house" (confined space/group) · a **sin** that summoned it · the "half man" who warns | Confinement is the act architecture; escalation must track the sin, not just the body count | The Babadook (sin = suppressed grief), The Menu (house = restaurant) **[catalog]** |
| **Golden Fleece** | A road · a team · a prize | Episodic by design — but each "road apple" must **change the hero**, or it's a travelogue | Raiders (relay of set-pieces; the Ark changes hands 9× **[mapped]**), Dune **[mapped]** |
| **Out of the Bottle** | A wish · a spell **with rules** · a lesson (do it without the magic) | Rules must be stated early and never broken ("no double mumbo jumbo"); the finale is the rules withdrawn | EEAAO **[mapped]** — 11 of 15 STC beats within 3 points; E.T. **[mapped]** |
| **Dude with a Problem** | An innocent hero · a sudden event · life-or-death | Needs an "eye of the storm" respite beat or the escalation flatlines | All Quiet, Tenet **[catalog]** |
| **Rites of Passage** | A life problem · a wrong way of attacking it · acceptance of a hard truth | The arc *is* the plot; resolution is acceptance, not victory | Lady Bird, Aftersun **[mapped]**; Mean Girls **[mapped]**; Legally Blonde pp.1–25 only **[mapped]** |
| **Buddy Love** | An incomplete hero · a counterpart who completes them · a complication keeping them apart | Two-hander scene geometry; the "chase to the airport" finale | When Harry Met Sally **[mapped]**; BEEF as dark inversion **[mapped]** |
| **Whydunit** | A detective · a secret · a dark turn (the detective breaks their own rules) | Information design replaces goal-and-obstacle; "case within a case" | Glass Onion, Mare of Easttown, Only Murders **[mapped]** |
| **Fool Triumphant** | A fool · an establishment · a transmutation (often a new name/identity) · a jealous insider | The Fool must **not** change — the institution does | Legally Blonde — the library's canonical case **[mapped]** |
| **Institutionalized** | A group · a choice · a sacrifice (join / burn it down / be destroyed) · a naïf who explains the rules | The group is the antagonist; the choice is the climax | The Bear, Severance **[mapped]** |
| **Superhero** | A special power · a nemesis who mirrors the hero · the curse of being extraordinary | Nemesis must be a self-made mirror, not a bigger obstacle | The Matrix **[mapped]**; Erin Brockovich **[mapped]** — no capes required |

**Use the STC genre as a premise-drift check.** Name the genre from the *logline*, then name it again from the *midpoint* and from the *finale*. If the three answers differ, the script has drifted and you have found the fault before writing a note about pacing. Drift is legitimate only when it is staged and dated: Legally Blonde runs Rites of Passage for 25 pages (heartbreak → mourning → grief-driven plan) and switches to Fool Triumphant at the exact frame the Boxster hits Harvard Yard (p.25, 21%) — one visible seam, never revisited. Compare the diagnostic power: the Legally Blonde map disqualifies Rites of Passage on a single test — *the heroine does not change; the institution does* — which is what forces the correct reading and explains why the back half inverts Snyder. Run that test on your own draft: **who changed, the hero or the room?**

## 2. The obligatory-scenes doctrine

Story Grid's principle (Coyne), which matters more than his exact taxonomy: **every content genre carries conventions (the world/roles/objects it requires) and obligatory scenes (events the audience contract demands).** Miss them and the reader contract breaks, whatever else is working.

Three operating rules:

1. **Deliver the genre at least once every reel** (Velikovsky) — roughly one genre set-piece every ~10 pages. Apatow's comedy spacing (a set-piece every 10–15 minutes) generalises out of comedy.
2. **Genre fixes the protagonist's structural goal** (Dallas Jones): Crime = catch a criminal · Detective = find the truth · Horror = defeat a monster · Love = find love · Myth = a journey to oneself. Identify genre *first*; it sets the goal and the expectations you will fulfil or knowingly break.
3. **Hybrids are the norm, but hybrid tone still needs ONE structural genre as the spine.** Pick the spine; the other genre supplies texture and set-pieces.

| Content genre | Obligatory scenes (miss = cheated) | Conventions (world / roles / objects) | Core emotional delivery |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Action** | Hero at the mercy of the villain · the escalating set-piece chain · the final one-on-one · the sacrifice/cost beat | A MacGuff or hostage · a ticking device · escalating arenas · a specialist skill | Exhilaration; competence under mortal load |
| **Thriller** | Hero at the villain's mercy · discovery of the villain's plan · the midpoint polarity flip (hunted → hunter) · the false-defeat before the confrontation | A villain with a stated plan · a clock introduced mid-story · a false ally | Dread; the pleasure of being hunted |
| **Horror** | The rule-establishment scene (what kills it / what invites it) · the "half man" warning · the false defeat of the monster · the final confrontation · the sting | A confined "house" · a summoning sin · a monster with a metaphor attached | Fear released and rebuilt higher |
| **Mystery / crime** | Discovery of the body/crime · the false solution · the gathering/accusation scene · exposure of the criminal | A closed suspect pool · a detective with a private rule · clues planted before the reveal | The click of a fair solution |
| **Heist / caper** | The assembly · **the plan as shown** · the execution · the victory lap · the thing that goes wrong · the reveal of the real plan | A mark worth taking · one specialist per obstacle · a rehearsal · a clock · an incumbent who must be displaced | Competence as pleasure; the click of a machine working |
| **Romance / rom-com** | Lovers meet · reasons-this-can't-work · proof of love · the breakup · the grand gesture · the declaration/commitment | A mirror couple · a confidant · a running gag that becomes evidence | Yearning, then earned union |
| **Comedy** | The premise demonstrated, not explained · a set-piece every reel in which the flaw is executed at scale · the escalation the protagonist can no longer stop · the public humiliation · the restoration that prices the joke | A flaw that generates the plot rather than obstructing it · a straight man with a position of their own · a rule-set the comedy can break · a running gag that becomes evidence | Release; recognition arriving faster than judgement |
| **Coming-of-age** | The humiliation that names the false self · the betrayal of the old world · the refused retreat · the act performed as the new self | A natural clock (school year / summer / pregnancy) · a dual-valence antagonist (loved *and* opposing) | Recognition; the ache of a door closing |
| **Drama** (character / social) | The value installed as ordinary life · the pressure that cannot be refused · the confrontation deferred and deferred · the irreversible choice · the reckoning — which is very often a *withdrawal* rather than an event | A want the protagonist cannot say aloud · an antagonist who is also loved, or a system in place of a person · a place the story keeps returning to · a metered clock where there is no plot | Reappraisal: the audience revalues something it thought it already understood |
| **Biopic / true story** | The origin of the gift · the public triumph · the private cost · the reckoning/verdict | A frame or hearing · archival texture · a thesis that selects the events | "So that's what it cost" |
| **War** | The cost-made-personal scene · the order that cannot be obeyed cleanly · the return home (or its pointed denial) | Unit-as-family · a command strand braided with a front-line strand | Grief inside scale |
| **Sports / performance** | Discovery of the gift · training/mentor sequence · early win (false ceiling) · the setback that exposes the flaw · the big event | A calendar of contests · a mentor with a lock-in beat · an opponent who mirrors | Vicarious mastery |
| **Satire / dark comedy** | The premise made literal · the escalation nobody stops · the target's self-justification scene · the refusal (or granting) of catharsis | A social system as antagonist · chapter/course/act-title structure | Recognition that curdles into complicity |
| **Musical** | The "I Want" song (~first 15 min) · production numbers as fun-and-games · villain song · dark reprise at All Is Lost · finale reprise as proof-of-change | Song slots = beat slots · a diegetic excuse or a full presentational contract | Feeling arriving faster than plot |
| **Family / animation** | The wish stated aloud · the mentor/sidekick relief rhythm · the parent-truth scene · the transformation performed in public | Dual-address comedy (child/adult layers) · a clear rule-set world | Safety, then awe |

**Audit method.** List your genre's obligatory scenes. For each, write the page number where it lands, or write `ABSENT`. Then ask of each `ABSENT`: *is this a deliberate substitution with something else delivered in its place, or did I never notice it was owed?* Only the first is a subversion (§5).

## 3. Deep playbooks

The nine genres a user is most likely to arrive with. Percentages are of total script length. **Measured** = taken from a beat map. **Prescribed** = framework convention, unverified against the library. §§3.1–3.6 are the genres with the strongest structural signatures; §§3.7–3.9 are here because they are the largest categories of real work, not because their contracts are the tightest — and §3.7 says plainly why drama's is thinner than everything above it.

### 3.1 Rom-com / love story — evidence-backed from When Harry Met Sally [mapped, 122 pp] and Juno [mapped, 101 pp]

| Beat | Target % | WHMS (measured) | Juno (measured) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meet (often hostile) | 1–5% | pp.2–4 (2–3%) — and *repeated* at 15–21% and 30–33% | none — consummation precedes p.1, doled out in flashback (pp.1, 15, 62) |
| Thesis / reasons-this-can't-work | 10–15% | pp.14–16 (12–13%) — "men and women can't be friends" | p.7 (7%) — "It's extremely complicated" |
| Commitment (Act II break) | 20–36% | pp.41–44 (34–36%) — a **friendship pact**, not a kiss | pp.21–24 (21–24%) — the adoption decision (A-plot) |
| Deepening / fun-and-games of the couple | 37–50% | pp.45–59 | pp.60–63 (59–62%) — displaced to B-plot |
| Midpoint intimacy spike | 47–55% | pp.60–64 (49–52%) — NYE dance, dip, the promise | p.68 (67%) — A-plot false victory, not an intimacy beat |
| Complication: intimacy breaks the equilibrium | 70–80% | pp.85–91 (70–75%) — comfort sex; post-coital two-shot carries the whole crisis | pp.71–76 (70–75%) — the locker fight |
| Breakup / dark night | 85–93% | pp.104–113 (85–93%) | p.86 (85%) |
| Epiphany | 87–96% | pp.114–117 (93–96%) — built from the film's own reprised images | pp.88–90 (87–89%) — the father states the love theme |
| **The run** (grand gesture) | 90–97% | p.118 (97%) — pure motion, no dialogue | p.91 (90%) — 100 boxes of Tic Tacs, zero dialogue |
| Declaration → union | 91–99% | pp.119–121 (97–99%) — the list speech re-prices every mocked trait | pp.92–94 (91–93%) |
| Coda | 99–100% | pp.121–122 | pp.100–101 |

**What the two maps prove.**
- The meet-cute can be **tripled** and hostile (WHMS: three meetings across 12 years, real catalyst at 30%); the "cute" is retro-fitted by the closing V.O.
- The Act II break can be an **anti-romance pact**, which converts every subsequent scene into dramatic irony.
- No rival and no misunderstanding required: **the false union itself is the crisis trigger.**
- The whole beat set can run as a compressed B-plot — Juno spends ~4 pages on gesture/declaration/union and lands at full weight because the A-plot loaded the theme for 80 pages. **Beat placement matters less than thematic charge.**
- A **natural clock** (pregnancy, four seasons written into action lines) beats a manufactured one; WHMS's calendar rhyme (NYE at 52% → NYE at 98%) supplies deadline, escalation and payoff for free.
- Running gags are **emotional escrow**: WHMS's list speech cashes "high maintenance," "on the side," the dip and the paprikash as evidence of love.

**Mistakes.** External obstacle standing in for internal blockage (the couple has no thesis to disprove) · a midpoint kiss with no consequence · a breakup caused by a withheld secret rather than by the intimacy · a declaration that lists virtues instead of re-pricing the flaws the script already mocked · a grand gesture with dialogue in it.

### 3.2 Horror — **no beat map in the library; this playbook is theory-sourced.** Treat as prescribed, not measured.

**Chain:** sin (established, often deniable) → summoning → confinement established → escalating incursions → **rule-establishment scene** (what kills it / what invites it) → the "half man" warning → false defeat of the monster → final confrontation → **sting** (door left open — the genre's signature final image).

**Prescribed placements:** first incursion by ~12%; rules stated by ~35–45%; false defeat ~70–75%; sting on the final page. Scare set-piece every reel (~10 pp).

**Load-bearing rules.** The monster's escalation curve must track the **protagonist's flaw** — the monster-as-metaphor design (The Babadook maps haunting beats to a grief arc **[catalog]**). Tension is built on build → release → **rebuild higher** cycles, and alternates dramatic irony (audience sees the monster first) with mystery (audience sees less than the hero). Gore is not tension.

**Mistakes.** Rules invented at the climax · a monster that escalates in scale rather than in relevance · skipping the false defeat (the audience needs to have believed it was over) · a sting that contradicts the theme rather than restating it · a "house" the characters could plausibly leave, unaddressed.

### 3.3 Thriller — anchored on No Country for Old Men [mapped, 117 pp] and Black Mirror 1x01 [mapped, 69 pp]

**Chain:** innocent-entangled (Dude with a Problem) *or* investigator-in-too-deep (Whydunit dark turn) → the villain's plan installed → hero at the villain's mercy → **midpoint polarity flip** (hunted becomes hunter) → clock introduced and shortened → false defeat → confrontation.

**Measured anchors.** No Country: midpoint pp.57–59 (49–50%) is Moss finding the transponder — *the pursuit machine breaks*, which is the polarity rule at its purest; the obligatory confrontation is **explicitly promised by telephone** at 68–77%; the protagonist is killed off-screen at ~p.102 (87%). Black Mirror: the demand lands p.5 (7%) and executes p.57 (88%) — everything between is the interval between statement and enforcement; the reversal is carried by a single inverted number (86% against at p.30 → 86% for at p.43), and the protagonist makes exactly one decision (p.44), which fails.

**Rules.** Plot the antagonist's story first, then intersect the hero (Siegel: the villain's need to execute his plan drives the story). **Fate favours the antagonist** — coincidence may hurt the hero, never save them (Rossio). Clocks are introduced mid-story and shortened toward the climax, not set in Act One and left running.

**Mistakes.** A villain with capability but no *plan* (nothing to discover) · the polarity flip never happening, so the hero is passive for two acts · a clock that never shortens · a rescue by coincidence · promising the confrontation and delivering it early.

### 3.4 Mystery / whodunit / courtroom — Glass Onion [mapped, 134 pp], Poker Face [mapped, 65 pp], Mare of Easttown [mapped, 80 pp], Only Murders [mapped, 37 pp]

| Architecture | Engine | Measured demonstration |
|---|---|---|
| **Fair-play whodunit** | Every solution element planted before the reveal; a false solution before the true one; the gathering-of-suspects scene | Glass Onion: two-pass structure hinged at p.73 (54.5%) on a mirrored slug line (`PAINTED BLACK` → `PAINTED WHITE`) — the trick is structural, requiring no on-page device |
| **Howcatchem / inverted** | Crime first; pleasure = watching the detective close in; audience ahead of the detective | Poker Face: crime pp.1–9, unlabelled rewind at 14%, detective learns a crime happened at p.32 (49%), gotcha 86–94%. A withheld identity ("an unseen woman," p.9) converts a 22-page replay from repetition into revelation |
| **Community-web limited series** | Suspects hidden inside an inventory of an ordinary town; the case is the delivery mechanism for a grief spine | Mare: 43 named characters, none introduced as a suspect; the **body arrives at ~98%**; the episode's real turn is emotional and sits at the midpoint (p.30, 48%) |
| **Closed-world serial comedy-mystery** | Bounded suspect pool converted into a season engine | Only Murders: body found at act-out p.16 (43%), case *closed* as suicide at p.23 (62%), reopened, and the final page reveals the protagonist knew the victim |

**Obligatory and non-negotiable:** the clue-plant audit (list every fact the solution requires and its plant page — any unplanted fact is a cheat), the false solution, the exposure. **Courtroom variant:** the trial *is* the act structure — witnesses = sequences, objections/verdict swings = act-outs, the verdict = climax, with testimony-triggered flashbacks as the time machine.

**Mistakes.** Withholding information from the audience that the detective has (that is not mystery, it is concealment) · a solution that requires a fact introduced after 90% · the gathering scene as pure exposition with no reversal inside it · in TV, an engine that solves the show (Poker Face deliberately cripples the lie-detection gift so it cannot).

### 3.5 Coming-of-age — Lady Bird [mapped, 110 pp], Aftersun [mapped, 100 pp], Mean Girls [mapped, 117 pp], Juno [mapped, 101 pp]

**Skeleton:** Rites of Passage + **a natural clock**. School year (Lady Bird, Mean Girls) · one holiday (Aftersun) · a pregnancy (Juno).

**Measured anchors.** Lady Bird: break into Two p.14 (13%) — the only choice with consequences; midpoint pp.50–52 (45–47%) is a card (`SECOND SEMESTER`) plus a tribe change — its map calls this "the exact centre," but 45–47% is the corpus's second-lowest midpoint, just under the 47% floor; break into Three pp.72–75 (65–68%); **crisis at 90–91% is a withdrawal, not an event** (the mother stops speaking). Aftersun: calendar, not causality — each holiday day opens on a morning slug and closes on a night, and the memory frame occupies the seams *between* days, where a memory has no footage. Mean Girls: the school calendar does the act work invisibly, and the finale (Spring Fling) is named at p.10 (9%) by the character who dismisses it. Juno: season markers written into action lines (p.1 fall / p.43 winter / p.69 thaw / p.88 crocus / p.100 summer); the due date is stated at 33% and arrives on schedule.

**Rules.** Episodic-vignette construction is tolerated here more than anywhere — but only because the clock and one relationship spine hold underneath (Lady Bird: 124 scenes averaging 0.91 pp, with length bought exactly where the character cannot escape — pp.70–80 runs 6 scenes at 2.08 pp). The antagonist is usually a system or a parent who is **also loved**. The arc is the plot. **Teen-comedy infiltration variant** (Mean Girls, Clueless): infiltration → corruption → exposure → re-integration.

**Mistakes.** Vignettes without a clock (beads with no string) · a purely hateable antagonist (kills the dual valence) · the identity change announced rather than performed · placing the crisis on an event when the genre's crisis is usually a silence.

### 3.6 Biopic / based-on-truth — Oppenheimer [mapped, 197 pp], Erin Brockovich [mapped], Chernobyl 1x01 [mapped, 54 pp]

**Pick ONE shape. Drifting between them is the classic biopic failure.**

| Shape | Requirement | Measured demonstration |
|---|---|---|
| **Cradle-to-grave compression** | A frame or narrator to *select*, plus a thesis to unify. Highest sprawl risk | Elvis, Respect **[catalog]** |
| **The slice** (modern default) | One revealing episode stands for the life; structurally a normal feature wearing a true story | Erin Brockovich: PPI at 33–36%, midpoint 50.4%, PPII 79–81%; act-break-scale turns manufactured from three renewable resources — numbers that get bigger, documents that change hands, and a recurring negotiation ritual |
| **Non-linear thesis biopic** | The *argument* selects the timeline order | Oppenheimer: two hearings braided, Fission (colour, first person) ~82% of pages in long blocks, Fusion (B&W, third person) ~18% in short stabs used as a **cutting mechanism**; Trinity at 67% is a physical, not structural, climax; the reveal at 79–80%; twin verdicts at 98% |

**Known-outcome tension.** Since the audience knows the ending, tension migrates to **how** and to **the private cost** — dramatic irony becomes the engine. Chernobyl states its argument in the first line of dialogue ("What is the cost of lies?"), delivers the season's endpoint before its beginning (Legasov's suicide, pp.1–4), and opens an irony gap at p.9 that does not close inside the episode. Rise-and-fall also lives here — the one commercial home of Freytag's shape.

**Mistakes.** Shape drift (a slice that keeps reaching for the whole life) · a frame device that decorates rather than selects · reverence in place of an argument · a climax that is the famous event rather than the private reckoning (Oppenheimer puts the bomb at 67% precisely to avoid this).

### 3.7 Drama — Erin Brockovich [mapped, 135 pp], Lady Bird [mapped, 110 pp], Aftersun [mapped, 100 pp], The Father [mapped, 87 pp], Parasite [mapped, 141 pp], No Country [mapped, 117 pp], Chernobyl 1x01 [mapped, 54 pp], Mare of Easttown 1x01 [mapped, 80 pp]

**Say this to the writer before anything else: drama's contract is thinner than any other in this file, and it is thin by nature rather than by neglect.** Action owes set-pieces, horror owes a rule scene, rom-com owes a run. Drama owes almost no specific *events*. What it owes is a **value** — something the audience is made to care about, put under pressure, and priced. That is why McKee's apparatus is the working tool here (`frameworks.md` §3.6): value charges, not mileposts. The audit question is therefore not "is the gathering scene present?" but **"what is at stake, who says so, and what does it cost?"** A drama with no answer to that is broken however many scenes it has, and a drama with a good answer can dispense with almost every convention above.

**The chain, such as it is:** the value installed as ordinary life → pressure the protagonist cannot refuse → a confrontation deferred and deferred → an irreversible choice → the reckoning.

**What the eight maps actually show.** Seven findings, each measured:

1. **The crisis is frequently a silence.** Lady Bird's all is lost is a mother who stops speaking (pp.99–100, 90–91%), and its map states the rule outright — *make the "all is lost" a withdrawal of speech* — which works only because 99 pages have established argument as this family's love language. The same map records that the film has **no classical climax at all**: no confrontation, no decision under pressure, no final test.
2. **The value can be carried by a prop, a number or a ritual instead of a speech.** Erin Brockovich manufactures act-break-scale turns from three renewable resources — numbers that get bigger, documents that change hands, and a recurring negotiation ritual — in a true story with no action engine (PPI 33–36%, midpoint 50.4%, PPII 79–81%, climax sequence 88–97%).
3. **The complications are about status, not information.** That same map catalogues fifteen progressive complications in which the gap between expectation and result is never about what a character *knows*; it is about what a character is worth to the room.
4. **Drama survives without a spine only if something else is metered.** Aftersun has no plot and substitutes a **borrowed clock** (return date, school term, a birthday two days off) plus **one quarantined conventional arc** — the rave, about 4.8 pages total across pp.1, 21, 48, 69, 84 and 97, advancing exactly one variable. Lady Bird has no goal-obstacle spine and runs on a calendar, a relationship curve and a repeating four-beat pattern across 124 scenes averaging 0.91 pp — buying length precisely where the character cannot escape (pp.70–80: 6 scenes at 2.08 pp, against pp.40–50's 24 scenes at 0.44).
5. **A drama that disclaims act structure still keeps a middle.** The Father opens "the script has no act structure in the conventional sense" and then names both turns outright (pp.9–10 / 10–11%; pp.44–46 / 51–53%), landing its emotional climax at 98–99% ("I want my mummy"). Aftersun declares both turns absent from the holiday body and relocates them into its 4.8-page memory frame (p.21 / 21%, p.48 / 48%). The Father's formal apparatus is also **ordered rather than random** — the reality-violations escalate by category and never de-escalate, ending on the location reveal at p.79 (91%). Randomised confusion reads as sloppiness; a ratchet reads as an accelerating story.
6. **A drama may state its value as a question and spend the script itemising the answer.** Chernobyl asks "What is the cost of lies?" in the first line of the series, converting 53 pages from narrative into demonstration and licensing a structure with no protagonist and no goal-obstacle spine; its most naked statement is an action line at p.44 (81%) — "Applause for delusion. Applause for death." The counter-thesis is given its best advocate (Zharkov, the only character who wins his scene), which is what makes the proposition arguable rather than a slogan.
7. **Where drama has an obligatory scene, it is the one the genre is permitted to refuse — after building it.** No Country promises the Moss/Chigurh confrontation out loud by telephone at 68–77% and never pays it: the two men never meet in 117 pages, the apparent protagonist is killed off-screen at 87%, and the last 15% converts archplot into miniplot. Parasite refuses identically — the Kims and the Parks never have one scene of open conflict in 141 pages, and the collision is displaced onto Kun-Sae and Mun-Kwang, the Kims' own class one floor down. In both the refusal *is* the argument. Run §5's four tests before attempting it.

**The one structural obligation drama does carry: the value must change state.** Mare of Easttown is the cleanest demonstration because its map separates and measures the two spines — the case spine is intermittent, unresolved and largely inherited, while **the grief spine is continuous and is the only thing that changes state.** Its turn at ~48% is not a murder but the ex-husband's engagement (p.30), and the episode's entire grief content is five pieces of evidence: an action line on p.1, "One. A daughter" on p.20, the word "disaster" on p.30, the turtle named Kevin on pp.62–63, and the apparition on p.65. **Five beats, correctly placed, carried an hour.** Do not confuse a thin dose with a thin design.

**Mistakes.** A topic in place of a value ("a film about grief" is a subject; "grief that is never spoken becomes inheritance" is a value under pressure) · the cost stated in dialogue rather than charged to a character · a confrontation the script never built being withheld and called restraint · vignettes with no clock, relationship curve or operational deadline underneath them · a reckoning staged as an argument when the material has been building toward a silence · and the specific trap of the value-free midpoint: a drama's second act sags exactly when the *price* stops rising, whatever else is happening on the page.

### 3.8 Heist / caper — Parasite [mapped, 141 pp], and prescription for the rest

**The library holds one heist structure and it is half a film.** Parasite's first half is a con-caper and its map analyses that structure directly — assembly, plan, rehearsal, named cue, victory lap — before the genre hinges at p.71 (50.4%). Everything tagged **[mapped]** below is from that map. The rest is craft-canon: hand it over as prescription, not evidence.

| Obligatory scene | What it owes | Parasite's version |
|---|---|---|
| **The assembly** | Each member installed with the one skill the plan needs, and a reason to be in | "The family becomes a crew in one scene" (pp.21–24, 15–17%) **[mapped]** — Ki-Woo invents "Jessica," the salon briefing supplies the mark's weakness ("she's a 'believer' — she tends to trust people rather easily"), and the Jessica jingle sung at the gate is **the caper genre declaring itself** |
| **The plan as shown** | A plan the audience can hold in its head, so it can watch the plan break | A V.O. briefing over images (pp.42–44, 30–31%) **[mapped]** that arms three payoffs at once: the peach allergy, the hot sauce, and the fact that the target "has been in the house the longest — longer than the family" |
| **The execution** | The plan performed, the pleasure sitting in competence | Four progressive infiltrations (pp.13–56, 9–40%) **[mapped]**, welded by a **named music cue** — "The Belt of Trust," explicitly spanning pp.40–49 across five scenes — and the technical showpiece at pp.44–49, where **rehearsal and performance are intercut** so the audience watches the con and its making at once |
| **The victory lap** | The crew believes it has won: the genre's false summit | A single Steadicam through the whole house (pp.56–58, 40–41%) **[mapped]**, interrupted mid-flourish — "Wait. Not yet--" — by "Same smell. They smell exactly the same!" **Victory and its refutation share one camera move** |
| **The thing that goes wrong** | Caused by the crew, never by bad luck | A doorbell at **p.71 (50.4%)**, and the person at the door is the housekeeper they got fired at p.51 **[mapped]**. The map's test for any script attempting this: **name the page in the first half where the protagonist caused the second half** |
| **The reveal of the real plan** | The retroactive re-read: what the audience watched was not what was happening | **ABSENT, and deliberately — treat this beat as theory-sourced.** Parasite has no second plan; it has the *end* of planning ("Do you want to know how you make a foolproof plan? Don't plan at all… If you don't have a plan, you can't fail," p.111, 79%). No map in the library executes a real-plan reveal. The nearest measured analogue is not a heist: `glass-onion`'s two-pass rewind at p.73 (54.5%), where 55 pages of Pass One compress to 24, sixteen pages of *entirely new* material precede the first repeated scene, and the hinge is carried by a `HARD CUT TO:` and one mirrored slug line — the mechanism a real-plan reveal would use **[mapped]** |

**Two rules from the Parasite map that generalise across the genre [mapped].** (1) **Rehearse the climax as comedy.** Ki-Woo swings the viewing stone at a drunk and is disarmed (p.59); Chung-Sook's shot-put photos hang in scene 5 (p.6); peach fuzz is established as a weapon at p.44. By the finale every capability has already been seen, so the violence reads as arithmetic rather than escalation. (2) **The caper's engine is replacement, not greed** — each new job requires the destruction of an incumbent (Min-Hyuk's absence, Yun's dismissal, Mun-Kwang's exile), which is precisely what makes the crew's own bill collectible in the second half.

**Mistakes.** A plan the audience cannot hold, so its collapse means nothing · the thing that goes wrong arriving as coincidence rather than as the crew's own bill · a real-plan reveal that requires a fact never shown — the whodunit's cheat rule (§3.4) applies exactly · a crew of specialists whose specialisms the plan never uses · no clock at the hinge, so revelation stalls into explanation (Parasite installs one sixteen pages after the doorbell — "Eight minutes, according to navigation," p.87 — to convert a static blackmail standoff into farce-suspense) · and the genre's signature failure, a victory lap the script means sincerely.

### 3.9 Comedy — the library's largest holding, and the contract with the fewest obligatory scenes

Comedy is the biggest category of maps here and the hardest to write a contract for, because its obligatory content is a **rate** rather than a set of scenes. The one hard rule is already stated in §2: deliver the genre at least once every reel — a set-piece roughly every 10 pages, Apatow's 10–15 minutes generalised out of comedy. What the maps add is where the beats land and what they are made of.

| The question | The measured answer |
|---|---|
| What does a comedy act-out button on? | **Humiliation, not jeopardy.** Abbott Elementary prints hard act-outs at 35% and 71% and both are *public arguments lost*; it then deliberately refuses to act-out on its own biggest reversal (Ava's sign, p.22 / 65%), spending two further pages converting it into Janine's own mistake so the act buttons on self-inflicted humiliation **[mapped]**. Desperate Housewives's Act Two contains zero mystery content and still buttons cleanly on one line at a family dinner table (p.31) **[mapped]** |
| Where does the engine go? | Later than instinct says — and it must be a *machine*, not a premise. The Office puts its entire series engine in a **TAG at 97%** and delegates exposition to talking heads that are 27% of scenes but only 11.5% of words **[mapped]**. Ted Lasso prints no acts at all and turns roughly every five pages, **always on an information release**, with the sabotage reveal at p.21 (54%) placed seven pages *before* the BELIEVE sign at p.28 (72%) — reversed, the sign is a slogan **[mapped]** |
| How is repetition kept funny? | Hold the runtime, change the contents. Russian Doll's loops do **not** shorten; the fixed sequence inside them compresses 8.7 → 5.4 → 3.0 pp, and the audience learns the rule at p.13 while the protagonist learns it at p.20 **[mapped]** |
| How much can a comedy page carry? | Measurably. Big Bang Theory runs 99 words/page at ≈2.1–2.4 pp per screen minute, with a **13-page cold open carrying the entire inciting incident**; Seinfeld is two acts of exactly 22 pp with no cold open and no tag; Mom builds its two 17-page acts in opposite directions — Act One breadth (7 scenes @ 2.4 pp), Act Two depth (3 scenes @ 5.7 pp) **[mapped]** |
| Can comedy carry real pain? | Only with stated rules. Mom sets explicit hold-and-release rules for tonal switches inside multi-cam joke rhythm, with measurable hold-lengths; Barry runs two climaxes back to back (acting pp.31–33, killing p.34) so the second answers the first; BEEF splits 17.26 / 17.23 pp between two mirrored protagonists with a 5.51-page convergence, carrying comedy and dread without alternating at all **[mapped]**. See `frameworks.md` §3.18: the shift must be *planted*, must land at a structural joint, and the deeper question must survive it |
| Does the comic hero change? | Often not — and that is the diagnostic. Legally Blonde is disqualified from Rites of Passage on a single test: **the heroine does not change, the institution does**, which forces the correct STC genre and explains why the back half inverts Snyder **[mapped]**. Mean Girls runs the inverse, a descending corruption arc inside an ascending template, and pays two structural taxes for it — a 2½-page Debate at 15–16% and a Break into Three at 83–84% **[mapped]** |

**So the comedy contract is three things, not a scene list.** A **flaw that generates the plot** rather than obstructing it — this is what makes set-pieces feel inevitable instead of inserted. A **rate** the script never drops below. And a **cost that is eventually charged**: the humiliation the protagonist cannot talk their way out of, which is why the measured act-outs land where they do.

**Mistakes.** Set-pieces that are funny and cost nothing — the reel rule met, the story static · a straight man with no position of their own, which flattens every scene into one voice · a running gag that never becomes evidence (WHMS's list speech, §3.1, is the model: it cashes "high maintenance," the dip and the paprikash as proof of love) · comedy act-outs written as cliffhangers, which the maps show is the wrong instrument · pain introduced with no hold-and-release rule, so the tone drifts instead of shifting · and, in TV, an engine that solves the show — the fault Poker Face answers by deliberately crippling its own lie-detection gift (§3.4).

## 4. Genre × framework fit

| Genre | First-choice frameworks | Fights it | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Action / adventure | Sequences · STC · 4-act | McKee-only theme work | Set-pieces need sequence-level architecture; Raiders is the canonical 8-sequence case **[mapped]** |
| Thriller | Sequences · McKee · Siegel 9-act (villain-first) | Harmon circle | The villain's plan must be plotted first; polarity midpoint |
| Horror | STC Monster in the House · Story Grid obligatory scenes | Hero's Journey | Tension shape and rule-set, not transformation stages |
| Mystery | Truby reveals · Aristotle anagnorisis · fair-play clue audit | STC 15 beats | Information design, not beat placement |
| Heist / caper | Sequences · Siegel 9-act (villain-first, inverted onto the crew) · STC Golden Fleece | Miniplot · Harmon circle | The plan *is* the sequence architecture; a team and a prize, where the "road" is the plan (§3.8) |
| Comedy | Harmon circle · STC (name the genre from the logline) · Story Grid for the rate audit | Truby's 22 steps · Murdock | Speed, and a flaw that generates plot — an over-engineered outline kills it (§3.9) |
| Rom-com | Hauge (identity→essence) · STC Buddy Love · Virgin's Promise | Vogler | The romance *is* the inner journey externalised (§3.1) |
| Drama (character) | McKee · Mazin · Truby · Weiland arcs | Sequence method | Theme-first |
| Indie / slice-of-life | Miniplot · kishōtenketsu · Harmon circle | Field 3-act | The three-act pass mislabels the real hinge |
| Tragedy / rise-fall | Freytag · negative arc (Weiland) | STC | The one commercial home of Freytag's shape |
| Biopic | The three shapes (§3.6) + frame devices | Any single 15-beat template | The argument selects the order |
| Coming-of-age | Rites of Passage · Virgin's Promise · natural clock | Vogler | Growth by internal awakening in place, not by quest |
| Sports / performance | Performance chain · mentor lock-in template | Miniplot | The competition calendar is the act structure |
| War | Golden Fleece (mission) or de-glorification arc · parallel command | Single-protagonist arc models | The cost-made-personal scene is the spine |
| Satire | Comedy engine + drama stakes · chapters/courses | Redemptive arcs | The argument must resolve even when catharsis is denied |
| Musical | Song-slot map over 3-act | Sequence method | Song spotting *is* beat spotting; a number that could be cut is a red flag |
| Family / animation | HJ · STC · Pixar spine + iteration | Miniplot | Rule-set clarity and dual-address comedy |
| Franchise / superhero | STC Superhero · flat-arc option · Eco scheme | Weiland arcs | The flat-arc hero (Bond) is a legitimate luxury; nemesis = mirror |

**Two measured cautions from the maps (see `measured-evidence.md` §7).** (1) **Framework choice is diagnostic, not decorative.** Mulan run against Vogler, Murdock and Hudson simultaneously: Hudson's Virgin's Promise fits 13 beats for 13, in order, while Vogler maps only *Ping's* journey and breaks three ways. The wrong lens does not fit less well — it describes a different character. (2) **On hard scripts, run two lenses and surface the disagreement rather than resolving it.** On Parasite, kishōtenketsu describes the macro-shape (p.71 is a genuine *ten*: it voids the goal rather than escalating it) while a pure three-act pass mislabels p.71 a midpoint and hides the protagonist handoff entirely.

## 5. Subversion rules

**The rule of exchange: you may break any convention on purpose, but you must deliver something in its place, and the contract must be established first.** Subversion of a contract the audience never formed is just absence.

**The earned-subversion test.** A subversion passes only if all four hold:

1. **The contract was installed.** The audience was taught to expect the scene, on the page, before it was denied. No Country withholds the Moss/Chigurh confrontation — and *explicitly promises it by telephone* at 68–77% first. It also withholds structurally, not just at the climax: the two men never meet in 117 pages.
2. **Something is delivered in its place, and it is bigger.** No Country replaces the confrontation with a thematic argument and a handoff: the protagonist is killed off-screen at 87% and the last 15 pages convert archplot into miniplot. That ending is a **return, not a swerve** — Bell's voice owns pp.1–3 before he owns a single scene.
3. **The script names the objection.** If the audience will call it a cheat, put the accusation in a character's mouth.
4. **The emotional delivery still lands.** You may swap the *scene*; you may not skip the *feeling*. If the genre owes exhilaration, dread, or earned union and none arrives, it is a breach whatever the intention.

### Worked case A — Legally Blonde: the romance the heroine chases is not the one she gets [mapped, 117 pp]

| Contract element | How it is subverted | What is delivered instead |
|---|---|---|
| The rom-com's Act Three reconciliation | Warner delivers the canonical speech at p.113 (97%) — "I'm an idiot… I want to be with you, Elle. Forever." The script has her **melt on the page**, lets her say the line back, then converts it into a wrist-lock: "In court. On opposing sides" | A refusal that only lands because the beat was played completely straight for four lines first |
| The love interest's beat set | Emmett gets no meet-cute, no obstacle, no jealousy, no gesture — a bench, an argument, and 1.5 pages of courtship at 98% | **The rival gets the full romance**: Sarah runs meet-hostile → escalation → respect → olive branch → betrayal misread → apology → union → transformation (newly blonde in the final image). Beat-sheeted blind, Sarah reads as the love interest |
| The union beat | The romantic climax is a reaction two-shot over a murder confession (p.112) — "Oh, my God!" / "Oh, my God!!" | The real love object is her competence, and the staging proves it: 5 pages of cross-examination against 1.5 pages of romance |
| "Bad Guys Close In" as descent | 54–83% is a **winning streak** | Pressure is substituted: nobody in authority credits the wins. Then the All Is Lost at 85% **poisons the achievement rather than removing it** — one gesture retroactively re-prices 40 pages |

**Why it is earned:** the goal-swap is staged and never announced (p.51: "You're about to see just how valuable Elle Woods can be") and never argued about again; the audience revises the goal automatically. **Transferable rule:** when the goal changes, don't announce it — and if you intend to refuse the genre's promised union, play the promised scene straight before you dismantle it.

### Worked case B — Mean Girls: a corruption arc inside a triumph-shaped framework [mapped, 117 pp]

The 15-beat template describes an ascending shape with one deep trough. This is a **descending** shape: the protagonist gets everything she set out to get by p.71 (61%), and the getting is the damage. Eight of fifteen beats still land within two points of Snyder, including the four hardest.

| Where the template strains | The fix on the page |
|---|---|
| A fall needs a hero who says **yes** — so the Debate collapses to 2½ pages (15–16%) | Act Two is started **twice**: social threshold p.19 (17%), moral threshold p.29 (25%), mission threshold p.39 (33%). Put the *moral* gate on the 25% mark — small, irreversible, deniable — and let the mission arrive later |
| "Fun and Games" and "Bad Guys Close In" cannot be sequential | They are **the same footage**, guaranteed by adjacency: cross "older boyfriend" off the chalkboard (p.63) then cut to the victim sobbing; cross off "hot body" (p.71) then give her the only defenceless line she has |
| A fall's hero must be publicly stripped, which costs pages — Break into Three slides to 83–84% | A 17-page third act carrying seven movements. The cost is real; budget for it |
| The win condition must not be answerable | The bus at p.98 destroys it. It survives as a payoff, not a cheat, because it is planted twice (physical p.5, metaphorical p.13), the **moral turn precedes the plot turn by one page**, and the antagonist names the objection 13 pages later: "Are you a superhero? Do you control the buses?" |
| A corruption arc that ends in a won fight is a triumph arc in costume | End by **re-aiming the competence, not restoring the innocence**: "How would Regina handle this? She would 'attack' and 'confuse'" — then two words of V.O., "Thank you, Regina." She keeps the toolkit, keeps the boyfriend, and keeps a fragment of the crown |

**Transferable rule:** when you run a descending arc through an ascending template, do not fight the beats — **double them**, and pay the two structural taxes the fall imposes (a nearly absent Debate, a late Break into Three).

## 6. Wiring this into the structure spec

Populate the spec's **Genre contract** table (`| Obligatory scene/element | Required by | Present? | Where |`) directly from this file:

- **Obligatory scene/element** — one row per item from the §2 row for your content genre, plus the three required elements from the §1 row for your STC genre. One row per item; do not merge.
- **Required by** — cite the source so a later pass can tell a convention from a finding: `STC:Fool Triumphant`, `SG:Thriller`, `§3.1 rom-com chain`, or `measured: WHMS 97%`.
- **Present?** — use the spec's status vocabulary only: `planned` / `drafted` / `working` / `broken`. An obligatory scene you have decided to subvert is **not** `broken` — record it as `working` and log the exchange (what replaces it) as a numbered **Decision**.
- **Where** — page and %; blank is a finding, not an omission.

Then run the three checks: **genre named from logline, midpoint and finale — do they agree?** (§1) · **every obligatory scene placed or explicitly exchanged** (§2) · **every subversion passes all four tests** (§5). Set the beat-placement tolerances from measured evidence, not prescription: flag a midpoint outside **47–55%** loudly, and a first-act break outside **10–38%** only gently.
