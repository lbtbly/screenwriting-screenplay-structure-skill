# Formats — choosing one, and its structural specification

Two jobs. §1 determines which format the story wants; §2 supplies the exact spec once it is chosen. §3 covers
pilots, §4 act-outs, §5 maps all of it onto the `structure.md` contract. **Authority of numbers:** every figure
is tagged. **[MEASURED]** = counted off a script in the beat-map library (42 maps; sample sizes given).
**[CONVENTION]** = what craft sources prescribe. Where they disagree, measured wins and the conflict is stated.
Never present a convention as a finding.

## 1. The format decision funnel

Ask in order. The first YES that survives its test is the format. Do not skip ahead — a story that
passes Q1 is a feature even if it has series-sized world.

| # | Question | The test it must pass | If it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **One dramatic question, answerable in a single sitting?** | Compresses to one logline with one climax; every subplot serves one spine; you can name the yes/no the ending answers | → Q2 |
| 2 | **Is there a repeatable ENGINE — a situation that generates story weekly without depleting?** | Answer "what's your show about?" and check whether a story-generator is embedded in the sentence. "Cops in a small town where everyone has secrets" = yes. "A heist goes wrong" = no, that's a feature or a limited | → Q3 |
| 3 | **One big story with a designed ending, too big for two hours — 3 to 8 hours of necessary story?** | The subplots/POVs are the point (a novel's braid), or the process is the drama (a real event), or the community web needs room to hide something | → Q4 |
| 4 | **One situation, one turn, under 40 minutes?** | One character, one situation, one emotional core; the ending carries the weight | → Q5 |
| 5 | **A serialized hook machine for phones?** | Conflict premise is structural (enemies-to-lovers, forced proximity, secret identity) and survives being cut into ~80 cliffhangers | Reconsider Q1 — most failures here are underdeveloped features |

**Tiebreaks.**

| Ambiguity | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Feature vs limited** | If removing subplots and POVs to fit 120 pp would remove what makes it worth telling → **limited**. If the six-hour version would repeat its middle beats → **feature**. |
| **Ongoing vs limited** | Ongoing needs a future-season engine and characters who resist change; limited needs a designed ending and may spend its arcs completely. If you can't name what episode 50 is about → limited. |
| **Feature vs ongoing** | Film is often about characters who change; TV about characters who do not or cannot. Premise-driven comedies must prove episodes 5 and 50 exist. |
| **Limited vs season one** | Ask whether the ending is designed or deferred. A deferred ending in a limited reads as a broken feature. |
| **Short vs feature** | ~40 min reads as a broken feature. If the story needs a second turn, it is not a short. |

The format decision is made for the new work on its own terms, never dictated by source material.
Pitch-side consequence: a feature pitch is story-driven and includes the ending; a series pitch is
character/theme-driven — pilot inciting incident, season one, the engine — and never the whole series.

## 2. Format specifications

### 2.1 Feature

| Item | Spec |
|---|---|
| Length | **87–197 pp [MEASURED, 18 scripts]** — the tails are real (The Father 87, Oppenheimer 197). **90–120 pp [CONVENTION]** holds for the centre and is still the right target for a spec. |
| Act architecture | Act One break **10–38% [MEASURED, all 18 scripts; only 10 of 18 sit in 19–25%]** · Midpoint **47–55%, 14 of 17 locatable ones [MEASURED, 17 scripts; Oppenheimer names none; full range 45–67%]** · PPII/All Is Lost ~75% · climax 90–99% [CONVENTION] |
| Alternative grid | 8 sequences × 12–15 pp; ~60 scenes × ~2 pp [CONVENTION]. **Printed act breaks: never** — acts are invisible in features. |
| Opening pages must | Make a promise the story keeps; plant what the ending pays. E.T. plants "I'll be right here" on p.31 and returns it on p.112; Oppenheimer cuts away from its own ending on p.8 and delivers it on p.195. Treat the opening image as a deferred payment, not a mood-setter. |
| Closing pages must | Answer the dramatic question and mirror the opening. Design the final image before writing. |

**The one check that matters.** If you can verify a single placement, verify the midpoint. A midpoint at 50%
with a first-act break anywhere in 10–38% describes almost every measured script; a break at exactly 25% with
a midpoint at 40% or 60% describes none of them. Flag a midpoint outside 47–55% loudly, a first-act break
outside 10–38% only gently. **Paying for a late break:** the four latest — Glass Onion 37–38%, Erin
Brockovich 33–36%, When Harry Met Sally ~36%, Dune 29–30% — each run *complete small stories* before it (an
entire fake detective story then killed; a full job-hunt with its own crisis; three meetings; the gom jabbar
and departure as a closed movement). A long first act is affordable only when it is not merely setup. **The
early break is the mirror:** the four earliest — The Father and No Country 10–11%, Parasite ~11%, Lady Bird
13% — buy a long, undirected second act in which the audience does not yet know what kind of story it is.

| Measured grid B | **Desperate Housewives: teaser 4 pp + 17 / 10 / 12 / 16** [MEASURED] |
| Measured grid C | **This Is Us: prints nothing, but the inferred acts run 8.5 / 9 / 10 / 9 / 8.3 / 8.1 pp — more regular than several scripts that do print them** [MEASURED]. Teaser runs 2–10 (~8.5 pp). |

| Opening pages must | Hook with a question, a shock, or the case-of-week's crime. Tease tone, world, concept, conflict — without being Act One. |
| Closing pages must | Resolve the episode's case and leave the engine loaded. The Rookie ends on a new call, not on a resolution. |

### 2.3 Cable hour (AMC/FX model)

| Item | Spec |
|---|---|
| Length | **57 pp [MEASURED: Breaking Bad]** · 55–80 [CONVENTION] |
| Act architecture | **Teaser + four acts.** Measured Breaking Bad: teaser 3 pp (1–3) + **14 / 14 / 12 / 14** (4–17, 18–31, 32–43, 44–57) [MEASURED] |
| Act functions | Act 1 ordinary world → catalyst · Act 2 new situation, allies/antagonists · Act 3 commitment to the central action · Act 4 sets up what propels future episodes [CONVENTION] |
| Printed act breaks | **Yes** |
| Storyline beats | A-heavy; serialized cable tolerates a single dominant thread plus one relationship line |
| Opening pages must | Buy patience. A flash-forward teaser is a loan: Breaking Bad's three pages of gas mask and corpses purchase fourteen pages of stair-steppers and car-wash humiliation. Budget it consciously — the more inert your Act One, the more extreme the cold open must be. |
| Closing pages must | Withhold causation, not outcome. Breaking Bad's teaser shows two dead men and never says who killed them; that single omission keeps Act Four suspenseful. |

### 2.4 Premium / streaming hour

| Item | Spec |
|---|---|
| Length | **53–80 pp [MEASURED, 6 scripts: Severance 53, Chernobyl 54, Black Mirror 64, Poker Face 65, Succession 72, Mare 80]** — a 27-page spread inside one format. 55–80 [CONVENTION]; streaming dramas often quoted at 70–80. |
| Act architecture | Usually unprinted, always present. Succession's central turn lands at **58–60%**, exactly where a four-act hour puts its third act-out [MEASURED]. |
| Measured grid A | **Severance prints four acts + prologue + tag on a show with no commercials: 4 / 7 / 16 / 12 / 13 / 1 pp** [MEASURED]. Network architecture used as drafting discipline. |
| Measured grid B | **Black Mirror prints four UK "PARTS": 8 / 24 / 18 / 14 pp** [MEASURED] |
| Printed act breaks | Optional, and **not predictive**. Printing acts is a drafting tool that survives independently of whether anything interrupts the episode; scripts that omit the labels still turn on the same schedule. |
| Storyline beats | Movements rather than A/B/C letters. Succession's writers' room explicitly refuses A/B-story language. |
| Opening pages must | Announce the grammar. Succession opens on Logan disoriented in the dark; the first spoken line begins mid-sentence — the dialogue rule is declared on page 2. |
| Closing pages must | Hook forward without resolving. Succession ends on a contenders montage captioned in the action line. |

### 2.5 Limited series

| Item | Spec |
|---|---|
| Length | 3–8 hours total; **order size serves story** — Frank expanded Netflix's six Queen's Gambit episodes to seven because episode balance demanded it |
| Per-episode | **54–80 pp [MEASURED: Chernobyl 54, Mare 80]** |
| Act architecture | Episode-shaped **movements**, not acts. Chernobyl ep.1 runs five: 4 / 7 / 16 / 17 / 10 pp [MEASURED]. Mare ep.1 runs seven across 80 physical pages [MEASURED]. **Printed act breaks: no** — neither prints any. |
| Season design | Treat the season as one five-part story sliced at episode boundaries that each (a) carry a complete movement, (b) end on a valence shift, (c) rotate emphasis among braided threads |
| The three doctrines | **Mazin:** compression forces authorship; withhold the *why* until the finale; give each episode a distinct identity. **Frank:** end while they still miss it. **Ingelsby:** layered revelation — "give a little here, then surprise you with something that deepens what you've seen before"; tension is in the gaps. |
| Difference from ongoing | No future-season engine required; the ending is designed from the start; arcs may be fully spent; per-episode act-outs are replaced by episode-level layered revelation. |
| Opening ep. must | Install the community/process dense enough to hold the whole story. Mare withholds the body until ~98% of episode one and spends the hour building the town instead. |
| Closing ep. must | Deliver the withheld master reveal. Design the final image before writing episode one. |

### 2.6 Multi-cam half-hour

| Item | Spec |
|---|---|
| Length | **44–51 double-spaced pp [MEASURED: Big Bang Theory 51, Mom 47, Seinfeld 44]** · 42–58 [CONVENTION], norm ~45–50. Draft variance is real: a different BBT production draft runs 42 pp. |
| Page-to-minute | Seinfeld ≈1.9 pp/min; BBT ≈2.1–2.4 pp/min [MEASURED] — stable enough to estimate runtime from page count |
| Act architecture | Cold open + 2 acts + tag (classic); modern 3-act and 4-act variants exist as ad loads grew |
| Measured grids | **BBT:** cold opening 1–13 (2 scenes) / Act One 14–27 — one continuous 14-page scene auditing all five principals in one room / Act Two 28–49 (8 lettered scenes) / tag 50–51. **Mom:** nine-page cold open (19%) / Act One curtain p.26 (55%) / Act Two 27–43 / tag 44–47. **Seinfeld:** two acts, no cold open; the tag exists only as `(MONOLOGUE TO COME)` on p.44. [all MEASURED] |
| Printed act breaks | **Yes** — scenes lettered ("SCENE B") with character lists beneath the heading; END OF ACT ONE centered |
| Scene math | 8–18 scenes (preferred 11–16); scenes play long and in real time [CONVENTION] |
| Storyline beats | A ≈ 8–10 (≈1 scene each) · B ≈ 5–6 · C = 1–2 runner scenes [CONVENTION] |
| Joke rhythm | ~3 jokes/page; **all but one or two scenes end on a joke** ("punchline out"); exceptions are emotional climaxes and setups paid at the top of the next scene |
| Opening pages must | Deliver one self-contained joke and the engine. Mom spends nine pages dumping full backstory disguised as a public breakdown. |
| Closing pages must | Install the new status quo and withhold the last concession. Mom's tag asks for an apology and refuses it. |

### 2.7 Single-cam half-hour

| Item | Spec |
|---|---|
| Length | **30–39 pp [MEASURED, 9 scripts: Ted Lasso 39, The Bear 37, Only Murders 37, The Office 37, Barry 35, Weird City 35, Abbott 34, BEEF 34, Russian Doll 30]** |
| **The correction** | **The widely quoted 25–34 pp [CONVENTION] is about five pages low. Only two of nine measured scripts fall inside it. Use 30–39. State this whenever a writer targets 25–30.** |
| Act architecture | Cold open + 3–4 acts + tag, or no printed acts |
| Measured grids | **Abbott Elementary:** cold open 3 / Act One 9 / Act Two 12 / Act Three 9 / tag 1 pp. **Only Murders:** unlabelled teaser 1–2 / Act One 2–16 (5–43%) / Act Two 16–23 (43–62%) / Act Three 23–37 (62–100%). **Weird City:** three printed acts, no teaser and no tag — the breaks serve engine changes, not commercials. **The Office:** prints nothing; inferred Act One 2–13 with the act-out on the leak montage at 35%; the tag (pp.36–37) delivers the series engine wordlessly. [all MEASURED] |
| Printed act breaks | Split. Network single-cam prints them (Abbott keeps 22-minute act discipline despite the documentary style); streaming usually does not. The turns persist either way. |
| Storyline beats | Braid of 3–4 threads. Modern Family's extreme: **4 storylines × 3 mini-acts each**, as little as one scene per mini-act, converging in a thematic wrap. |
| Cold open | 1–2 min (max 3–4), **often unrelated to the plot** (The Office's detachable joke). Abbott inverts this: its cold-open button *is* the inciting incident. |
| Opening pages must | State the engine as a joke. Abbott's broken toilets create the want-object on p.3, which frees Act One to be entirely character. |
| Closing pages must | Restore status quo with a whiff of change, then prove the world outranks the plot. Abbott's tag contains zero A-story. |

### 2.8 Half-hour drama / dramedy

| Item | Spec |
|---|---|
| Length | **34–37 pp [MEASURED: The Bear 37, Barry 35, BEEF 34]** · 28–36 [CONVENTION] — slightly low; use 34–37 |
| Act architecture | **One or two major plot movements**, not 4–6 act turns. Barry runs six movements; BEEF three. |
| Measured turns | **Barry: 31% / 49% / 71% / 86% — feature architecture at half-hour scale** [MEASURED] |
| Printed act breaks | **No.** None of the three prints acts. |
| Storylines | Single A-story or an equally weighted duo/ensemble — not A/B/C/D braids. Average scene ≈1.8 pp. |
| Tone rule | Genre is determined by tone, not runtime. Adding a meaty B and C to a half-hour drama converts it into an hour. |
| Opening pages must | Plant the thing the last page discards. The Bear opens on "FIELDS of TOMATO CANS" (p.1) and ends with a tomato can in the garbage (p.37) — the season's hidden money planted before the audience can know what it is. |
| Closing pages must | Land a single image, not a resolution. |
| Pacing device | Buy length where the character cannot escape. The Bear runs pp.8–18 as one unbroken CONTINUOUS chain — ~30% of the episode in one real-time walk through six rooms. |

### 2.9 Animated TV

| Item | Spec |
|---|---|
| Length | **39 pp [MEASURED: BoJack "Time's Arrow"]** · Simpsons-family scripts run 45–60+ pp for 22 min [CONVENTION] — animation scripts run long and visual; parentheticals are encouraged because actors record without visuals |
| Act architecture | 3 acts (Simpsons, Futurama, Family Guy) · 4 (Bob's Burgers) · 2 (Central Park) · cold open + 3 (BoJack, Archer, Harley Quinn) · none printed (Big Mouth) |
| Measured grid | **BoJack: cold open 1–3 / Act One 4–22 (19 pp, 49%) / Act Two 23–31 (9) / Act Three 32–39 (8)** [MEASURED]. Deliberately lopsided — **act length tracks novelty, not duration.** |
| Simpsons model | Act 1 ≈5 min trouble is coming · Act 2 ≈10 min trouble is here · Act 3 ≈7 min climax and reset. Signature: the **first-act tangent** — the opening premise swerves into the real story at the act break. |
| 11-minute | ~14–18 pp; **no secondary plots** — every second on one story or idea; one character facet per episode |
| Printed act breaks | Usually yes. Lead times force whole-season breaking before production, which favours novelistic season shapes. |
| Opening pages must | Hand over the POV if the episode is a formal experiment. BoJack's cold open starts in the protagonist's POV, spends one page there, then defects to another character for the rest. |
| Closing pages must | Deliver the withheld memory the structure was built to defer. |

### 2.10 Short film (1–40 min)

| Item | Spec |
|---|---|
| Length | ≤40 min = short (Academy). **Under 10 min is the sweet spot** (8–12 viable; docs to ~20) — programmers fill 90-minute blocks. Near 40 reads as a broken feature. |
| Bands | <5 min: one idea/one joke, sketch logic, button ending · 5–15 min: compressed 3-act, one protagonist, one question, ~15 scenes max · 15–40 min: needs feature-grade justification (proof-of-concept, thesis) |

### 2.11 Vertical micro-drama

| Item | Spec |
|---|---|
| Length | 60–120 s per episode; **1–2 pp per episode**; 50–100 episodes; 90–150 min total ≈ a feature cut into ~80 cliffhangers |
| Per-episode grid | **Hook (0–15 s** — open on conflict, zero setup) → **Friction (15–60 s)** → **Spike (60–90 s** — revelation/shift) → **Button (final 5–10 s — cut BEFORE the answer)**. Freeze-frame out at ~second 55 of 60. No printed acts — the episode *is* the act. |

| Closing must | Never fully resolve. "The cliffhanger isn't optional — it's the product." |

## 3. Pilots

### 3.1 Pilot types

| Type | Definition | Use when | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Premise** | Dramatizes the founding event; structured to run as episode one (Breaking Bad, Lost) | The origin is the best story you have and the engine is manufactured on screen | Tests well but "can be deceiving and not representative" of the weekly show — the engine, not the origin, is the product |
| **Non-premise / typical episode** | Plays like any episode; suits procedurals | The engine is self-evident and the appeal is the weekly machine | Still needs a "whiff of change" — something must change even here |
| **Soft premise** | A kick-starting event inside an otherwise routine episode (Friends: Rachel arrives mid-engine) | You want both the change and the demonstration | Hardest to balance; the event can swamp the routine |
| **Backdoor** | An episode of an existing series introducing spin-off characters | You have an existing show | Serves two masters |

*Put pilot* is a contractual commitment, not a structural type.

### 3.2 The pilot audit

Run every row. Cite the page each is established on and judge the efficiency. Missing rows are the finding.

| Must establish | Test | Worked example (Breaking Bad, 57 pp) |
|---|---|---|
| **Protagonist** | Established by action before dialogue explains him | pp.1–3 as an image, p.4 as a man — two deliberately contradictory introductions |
| **Want (stated/external)** | Can be a decoy, but must be spoken | p.39 — "Money, mainly." |
| **Need (true/internal)** | Must be proved dramatically, not asserted | p.40 "I am… awake," proved p.43 and p.57 |
| **World** | Delivered through action, not tour | pp.4–14 domestic, pp.24–26 the drug world via a DEA raid |
| **Tone** | Locked early and never broken | p.1, confirmed p.20 and p.44 |
| **Engine** | A *process* that can be shown compressible into a montage | pp.29–31 partnership + pp.44–47 the cook |
| **Central relationship** | Must be renegotiable in every scene, or it can't run for years | pp.28–47, a teacher/pupil relationship renegotiated continuously |
| **Antagonist** | Layered is stronger than singular | Hank p.11 (as family), the market pp.48–51 (as danger), Walt p.43 (as himself) |
| **Series promise** | What the show is promising to keep doing | teaser pp.1–3, p.14, pp.56–57 |
| **The "why now"** | Why this week and not any previous week | p.20 (diagnosis), reinforced p.23 ("Two years") |
| **Stakes / clock** | A named limit | p.8, p.20, p.38, p.57 |
| **Ensemble** | Enough speaking bench to survive without the lead | seven characters by p.28 |

Two calibrations. **The engine may arrive late if it is felt early** — Severance pitches its premise at
79–81% and works, because the audience has lived inside it since page one. **The strongest pilots sometimes
unsettle a row deliberately**: Succession installs a *question* where a protagonist should be (asked p.9,
formalised p.42, answered no p.43, voided p.69); Ted Lasso's hero never states a want, and the tension runs
entirely on the audience knowing the antagonist wants him to fail. Neither is an error — both substitute a
different engine for the missing one. Say which.

### 3.3 Series-engine tests

| Test | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| **Embedded generator** | The one-sentence answer to "what's your show about?" contains a story-generator, not an incident |
| **Episodes 5 and 50** | You can name both. Premise-driven and high-concept comedies fail here most often |
| **The montage test** | The engine can be *shown running* inside the pilot and compressed into a montage — the strongest available proof it runs weekly |
| **Depletion** | Running the engine ten times does not exhaust the situation or force a cast change |
| **Renegotiation** | The central relationship changes terms every episode rather than repeating a dynamic |
| **Out-of-order** | Procedural: episodes work in any order. Serialized: they do not, and the act-outs must therefore advance arc turns |

Engine types: procedural · serialized · hybrid (monster-of-week + mythology) · ensemble/character ·
fish-out-of-water · anthology (episodic reset vs seasonal reset; needs a unifying non-plot engine —
thematic, formal, locational, or moral).

**Pilot hook vs series loyalty.** The pilot must hook *attention* on page one. Loyalty is a multi-episode
purchase — the Netflix study across 25+ shows found no one hooked on a pilot; loyalty formed at eps 2–8.
Both are true at different granularities; do not let one excuse a slow pilot or an unplanned episode two.

## 4. Act-out craft

An act-out is a cliffhanger or emotional whammy placed immediately before the break. The cardinal sin is
resolving the issue before the break. If the act-out is forgettable, the script is forgettable.

### 4.1 Button types

| Type | What the audience returns for | Register |
|---|---|---|
| Major reversal · jaw-dropping revelation | The new configuration; what it means | Any |
| Character dilemma with no easy answer | To watch a choice | Drama |
| Escalated stakes — new threat or deadline | Survival | Drama |
| Emotional complication / betrayal | Relationship damage | Any |
| **Transgression** | To find out who the protagonist now is | Drama |
| **Stillness** | Dread | Premium |
| **Consequence / tragedy** | To see the cost paid | Network |
| **Argument loss, joked against the protagonist** | To see whether a *position* can be recovered | Comedy |
| **Punishment scheduled** | To watch a promised excruciating scene | Comedy |
| **Planted-object reversal** | Curtain, not break-holder; restates the theme | Comedy |
| **Detached runner** | Proves the world outranks the plot | Comedy tag |

The Rookie's five buttons run consequence/tragedy → innocent in jeopardy + ticking clock → revelation →
jeopardy cliffhanger + guilt → series-engine button [MEASURED]. Abbott's five run sight-gag-that-is-the-
inciting-incident → argument loss → punishment scheduled → planted-object reversal → detached runner
[MEASURED]. Comedy act-outs trade anxiety for curiosity and social dread; that is the format difference,
not a lower standard.

### 4.2 What makes an act-out survive a break

- **Withhold the crucial detail, not the outcome.** Withheld causation preserves suspense even when the audience already knows the protagonist survives.
- **Reverse the expectation set inside the act.** Abbott buttons Act One by having Janine's own talking-head claim ("we're passionate and capable") refuted by a kicked child four lines later.
- **Anchor in sensory or physical detail** — an image, not a summary.
- **Do not resolve.** The purest comedy act-out tells the audience exactly what horror is coming and makes them return to watch it.
- **A tag is not an act-out.** Its job is to tie a loose end or untie one; 1–2 pp.

### 4.3 The finding: strong act-outs measure appetite, not jeopardy

**Breaking Bad — three of four act-outs are transgressions rather than jeopardy** [MEASURED]: Walt raises
a pistol at approaching police lights (p.3), blackmails a former student — "Either that, or I turn you in"
(p.31), and drops a teenager with a kick to the back of the knee, standing on his ankle "realizing more
and more that he likes it" (p.43). Only Act One ends on something happening *to* him (the collapse, p.17).
Act Three contains no danger at all — twelve pages of shopping — and escalates appetite instead. The
buttons ask "how far will he go?", not "will he survive?"

**Abbott Elementary converts its own reversal into self-inflicted humiliation** [MEASURED]. The episode's
biggest reversal — Ava spending the rug money on a plastic sign — lands on p.22 (65%) and is *deliberately
not the act-out*. Brunson spends the next two pages turning the antagonist's move into the protagonist's
mistake: Janine escalates, emails the superintendent, and the email boomerangs back to Ava, who schedules
a mandatory lunchtime "trust workshop… It's going to be FUN." The act buttons there, on p.24 (71%).

**Severance: three of its five act-outs are moments of stillness rather than jeopardy** [MEASURED].

The generalization: **choose the act-out for what it reveals about character, not for what it leaves
unresolved.** In a premise pilot especially, make the buttons measure the protagonist's movement — the
reader should close each act asking "who is he now?", which is the only question a premise pilot answers.

## 5. Writing this into `structure.md`

The contract's `Format:` field takes exactly one of `feature | tv-hour-network | tv-hour-premium |
tv-half-single | tv-half-multi | limited | short | vertical`. Do not add values. Map the formats above:

| This file's block | Contract value | Record the distinction in |
|---|---|---|
| Cable hour (2.3) | `tv-hour-premium` | Framework line + Act grid (printed acts) |
| Half-hour drama (2.8) | `tv-half-single` | Genre line (drama) + single A-story in Storylines |
| Animated TV (2.9) | `tv-half-single` | Framework line |

Then: put the measured band in **Target length** and the format's act architecture in the **Act grid**
(TV only), one row per act with its `Button type` drawn from §4.1. Every page figure carries its source —
tag it `[MEASURED]` or `[CONVENTION]` so a later pass can tell a finding from a convention. Log the format
choice and its rejected alternative as **Decision 1**; that is what stops the next pass relitigating it.
