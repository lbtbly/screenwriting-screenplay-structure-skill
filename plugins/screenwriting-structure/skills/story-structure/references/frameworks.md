# Frameworks — the working catalogue

Load this to **choose** a structural framework and then **apply** one. Output goes into the `Framework(s):` line and the `Beat sheet` of a `structure.md` spec: one PRIMARY, at most one SECONDARY.

**Attribution notation — carry it into every beat sheet you write.** `[P]` = prescription (what the framework says a beat should land at; a convention). `[M]` = measured (what the 42 beat maps in `../../../structure-library/` actually show). Never present a `[P]` figure as evidence. In the spec, `Target %` is `[P]`; `Tolerance` is `[M]`.

The two `[M]` numbers that govern everything below:

- **Midpoint lands 47–55%** in 14 of the 17 measured features that have a locatable one (82%); the full range is 45–67%, and Oppenheimer names no midpoint at all. Flag deviations loudly.
- **Act One break ranges 10–38%** across all 18 measured features, and only 10 of 18 sit in the conventional 19–25% zone — a spread wider than the midpoint's. Flag deviations gently, and never merely for missing 25%. This inverts the emphasis of every page-mapped framework here, all of which anchor on the first-act break.

## 2. The five-beat crosswalk

Every spec carries the same five load-bearing beats. This is how each framework names them, so you can fill that table from any lens.

| Load-bearing beat | Field | Save the Cat | Sequences | Vogler | Harmon | Hauge | Yorke | Mazin |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Disturbance** | Inciting incident | Catalyst | end Seq 1 | Call to Adventure | 2 Need | TP1 Opportunity | Inciting call | Ironic disruption |
| **Commitment** | Plot Point I | Break into Two | end Seq 2 | Crossing the Threshold | 3 Go | TP2 Change of Plans | Acceptance | Anti-theme clung to |
| **Midpoint reversal** | Midpoint | Midpoint | end Seq 4 | Ordeal | 5 Find | TP3 Point of No Return | The "big gift" | Glimpse of the theme |
| **Collapse** | Plot Point II | All Is Lost | end Seq 6 | Road Back crisis | 6 Take | TP4 Major Setback | Worst point | Both worldviews lost |
| **Proof** | Climax | Break into Three → Finale | Seq 7–8 | Resurrection | 8 Change | TP5 Climax | Mastery | Defining moment |
| `[P]` target | 10/25/50/75/90% | pp.12/25/55/75/85 | pp.12/30/60/90/100 | 10/25/50/75/90% | — | 10/25/50/75/90% | centred | — |

`[M]` tolerance: Commitment **10–38%** (n=18), Midpoint **47–55%** (n=17). **Collapse and Proof have no policing band** — the maps that name them cluster at 66–85% and 87–100%, which is description, not tolerance. Use §2.1 to place them at design time and `measured-evidence.md` §6 to decide whether to flag them in a draft. (An earlier version of this line printed "Collapse 66–85%, Proof 85–98%" tagged `[M]` as though both were audit bands. They are not, and the Proof figure understated the top: five of the corpus's climaxes run past 98%.)

Preserved disagreements — do not resolve them silently: Vogler puts the Ordeal at the midpoint, most mashups slide it to the 75% All-Is-Lost slot; Snyder's raw pages are not linear percentages (his p.75 = 68%); Yorke's midpoint is an act *centre*, not a hinge between halves.

### 2.1 Design-time targets — what to put in the spec before there are pages

Workflow 1 places these five before anything else, and only the midpoint has a measured band you can design against. That is not an oversight in `measured-evidence.md`: it is built to **audit a draft that exists**, so it leaves Disturbance, Collapse and Proof with no band and severity `note`, on the correct principle that position alone is not a finding. That rule is right for a diagnosis and useless in front of a blank page.

**So: at design time a prescription is a legitimate starting point. The obligation is not to avoid it — it is to mark it.** Put the figure in `Target %` tagged `[P]`, put whatever measured support exists in `Tolerance` tagged `[M]`, and never let the two columns blur. A spec that silently prints 75% as though the corpus had measured it is the exact failure this notation exists to prevent.

| Beat | Design-time target | Status | Measured support from the library |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Disturbance** | **10–12%** | `[P]` — Field / Vogler / Hauge convention. The corpus measures no band | The maps run wide: Juno p.4 (4%), Legally Blonde p.8 (7%), Parasite pp.10–12 (7–9%), Erin Brockovich ~10% (the situation turns) and ~14% (the problem arrives), E.T. p.13 (12%), EEAAO p.16 (13%), Mean Girls p.17 (15%). Design at 10–12%; treat anything inside 4–15% as precedented, and a deliberate split — situation, then problem, then question — as a legitimate third option |
| **Commitment** | **19–25%** | `[P]` — the measured band exists but is 28 points wide and cannot be designed against | Only 10 of 18 features sit in 19–25%. Start there, then move it *on purpose*: later than 25% must buy a **complete small story** before the break, earlier than 19% must buy an undirected second act (`measured-evidence.md` §3). If you cannot name the purchase, put the break back |
| **Midpoint reversal** | **47–55%** | `[M]` — 14 of 17, 82% | The one beat whose design target and audit tolerance are the same number. Design to 50%; use the band as the check |
| **Collapse** | **75%**, with **66–85%** as working room | `[P]` 75% (Field PPII, Hauge TP4, Weiland's third plot point; Snyder's p.75 = 68%), with real measured clustering behind it | Eleven maps name a collapse beat outright: EEAAO 66%, The Matrix 67–71%, Mean Girls 68–69%, E.T. 73–76%, Dune 75%, Parasite 76–79%, Juno 79–81%, Erin Brockovich 79–81%, Legally Blonde 84–85%, WHMS 89–93%, Lady Bird 90–91%. **Nine of the eleven land 66–85%** — the prescription sits inside the cluster rather than against it. Two structures recur and are worth designing on purpose: the **split collapse** (Dune's external collapse at 63–65% and emotional collapse at 75–78%; The Matrix's all-is-lost at 67–71% and PPII at 75–77%) and the **poisoned achievement** (Legally Blonde at 85%, where nothing is removed and only the belief that she earned it is destroyed) |
| **Proof** | **90–99%** | `[P]` 90% (Field, Vogler, Hauge), with unusually strong measured agreement behind it | Seventeen features have a locatable climax and **fifteen of them begin at 90% or later**: Raiders 90%, The Matrix 90–98%, Mean Girls 90–94%, Mulan 91–92%, Parasite 91.5%, Legally Blonde 91–95%, Juno 93–97%, E.T. 96%, Glass Onion 96–98%, Aftersun 97–98%, Dune 97–99%, WHMS 97–99%, The Father 98–99%, No Country 98–100%, Oppenheimer 99–100%. The two earlier: EEAAO's finale opens at 87–89% and Erin Brockovich's campaign at 88%. **Lady Bird's map states there is no classical climax at all** — no confrontation, no decision under pressure, no final test — which is a real reading, not a gap. Two scripts carry an early *physical* climax and a late structural one, and the distance between them is the finding: Oppenheimer 67% and 99–100%, Mulan 65–67% and 91–92% |

**Three rules that follow.**

1. **Design forward from the midpoint, not from the quarter mark.** It is the only anchor with `[M]` authority. Place it first, then hang Collapse at ~75% and Proof at ~90–99% off it, then negotiate Commitment and Disturbance last — they are the two you will actually move.
2. **A `[P]` target is a hypothesis with a page number on it.** It earns its place by being falsifiable once pages exist; it does not earn the right to be flagged. Nothing in this section is a severity. When the draft arrives, `measured-evidence.md` §6 governs: outside the midpoint band is an **error**, outside the Commitment band is a gentle **warn**, and Disturbance, Collapse and Proof are `note` only — check that the beat is *present and doing its job*, never that it hit a number from this table.
3. **Mark the status in the spec or the number is a lie.** `Target %: 75% [P]` and `Tolerance: 66–85% [M], n=11, descriptive` are two different claims about the world, and a writer who cannot tell them apart will defend a convention as if it were evidence.

## 3. The frameworks

### 3.1 Field — the three-act paradigm (Syd Field, 1979)

The industry's shared vocabulary. Machinery: a protagonist's **dramatic need** meets escalating obstacles; a **plot point** is any event that "spins the action around in another direction."

| Beat `[P]` | Act I Set-Up | Inciting inc. | **Plot Point I** | Pinch I | **Midpoint** | Pinch II | **Plot Point II** | Act III |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| pp. (110) | 1–27 | ~10–13 | 23–25 | ~41 | ~55 | ~69 | 78–82 | 82–110 |
| % | 0–25 | ~10 | ~25 | ~37.5 | 50 | ~62.5 | ~75 | 75–100 |

Fix the four anchors in this order before writing: **ending → beginning → PPI → PPII.** Keep Field's distinction between the *inciting incident* (starts the story) and the *key incident* (the premise executed; usually = PPI). Resolution means solution, not final scene.

**Use when:** first screenplays · plot-driven genre features · you must communicate with a reader who literally checks pp.25–30 · diagnosing where a draft loses shape.
**Avoid when:** ensemble or non-linear projects · the arc, not the plot, is the story · an Act Two that is accumulation rather than duel.
**Criticism:** Film Crit Hulk's charge that the model never defines what an "act" *is*; Truby calls it "mechanical and simplistic"; the undivided 60-page Act II is the hardest act in the canon to write.
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/the-matrix.md` — PPI 23–24%, midpoint 48–50%, PPII 75%, textbook `[M]`. The instructive finding is what Field has no slot for: the beat at dead centre (Cypher's steak deal, pp.60–61) turns the *audience's knowledge*, and the protagonist is neither in the scene nor ever learns of it; his own reversal — the Oracle revoking his identity — sits at 58%. Split the midpoint: irony at centre, reversal at 58%. Compare `../../../structure-library/erin-brockovich.md`, where Act One hinges twice (situation p.13/10%, problem p.19/14%, question p.48/36%) and the all-is-lost sequence sits *before* PPI, so the act break is a rescue rather than a decision.

### 3.2 Save the Cat — Snyder's 15 beats (2005)

The most precise and most fought-over page-level system. Logic underneath: Act 1 thesis → Act 2 antithesis → Act 3 synthesis.

| # | Beat | `[P]` p.(110) | # | Beat | `[P]` p. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opening Image | 1 | 9 | Midpoint (false win/loss) | 55 |
| 2 | Theme Stated | 5 | 10 | Bad Guys Close In | 55–75 |
| 3 | Set-Up | 1–10 | 11 | All Is Lost | 75 |
| 4 | Catalyst | 12 | 12 | Dark Night of the Soul | 75–85 |
| 5 | Debate | 12–25 | 13 | Break into Three | 85 |
| 6 | Break into Two | 25 | 14 | Finale (five-point) | 85–110 |
| 7 | B Story | 30 | 15 | Final Image | 110 |
| 8 | Fun and Games | 30–55 | | | |

Brody's percentage re-issue circulates alongside (Catalyst 10%, Break into Two 20%, All Is Lost 75%, Break into Three 80%); both keep Midpoint = 50%. Always name the STC genre too — the ten story-DNA families carry required elements that stop premise drift.

**Use when:** high-concept comedy, action, thriller, animation · specs aimed at the mainstream market · any rewrite diagnosis · you need a shared page vocabulary with a producer.
**Avoid when:** miniplot character studies · theme-first projects (use Mazin) · anything whose engine is texture rather than premise.
**Criticism:** Peter Suderman's "Save the Movie!" (Slate, 2013) — the sheet became a de facto studio mandate that homogenised blockbusters; readers who recognise it sometimes stop reading.
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/everything-everywhere-all-at-once.md` — eleven of fifteen beats within three points of Snyder and three exact `[M]`: Break into Two p.29/23%, Midpoint pp.63–64/50–51%, Final Image p.125/100%. The famous formlessness is entirely in the *content* of the beats (the All Is Lost is a literal death plus an end card at 66%; the Dark Night is two rocks in a field; the climax is a hug), not their placement. Radical content, orthodox skeleton. Counter-case: `../../../structure-library/legally-blonde.md`, where Bad Guys Close In (54–83%) is a *winning streak* and All Is Lost lands at 85%, seventeen points late — survivable because the crisis is single-cause and **poisons the achievement rather than removing it**.

### 3.3 The eight-sequence method (Frank Daniel → Gulino)

A feature is 3 acts made of **8 sequences**, each ~12–15 pp `[P]`, each a short film with its own protagonist-goal and partly-resolved climax whose residue propels the next.

| Seq | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `[P]` pp | 1–12 | 12–30 | 30–45 | 45–60 | 60–75 | 75–90 | 90–100 | 100–110 |
| Function | Hook, status quo → inciting incident | Failed restoration → main tension locks (Act I out) | Easiest attempt fails; subplots launch; Pinch I | Bigger attempt → midpoint / first culmination | Consequences; want-vs-need; Pinch II | Easy options exhausted → 2nd culmination (Act II out) | Apparent resolution proves premature; new tension | All tensions resolve; imagery mirrors the opening |

Gulino's audience tools, ascending in power: telegraphing → dangling cause → dramatic irony → dramatic tension. Elasticity documented by Gulino himself: *The Graduate* 7 sequences, *North by Northwest* 9, *Lawrence of Arabia* 16.

**Use when:** action, adventure, thriller, road movie — any long-middle genre · outlining from scratch · rescuing a middle · you are intimidated by 110 blank pages.
**Avoid when:** contained real-time or single-situation films where tensions cannot hand off · theme-first work (the method says almost nothing about theme or arc).
**Criticism:** Matt Bird — equal-weight units flatten rising action into episodes; sequence counts are post-hoc rationalisation.
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/raiders-of-the-lost-ark.md` — measured sequences run **9–19 pp, not 15** `[M]`; they are tension units, not page units, and arithmetic application misplaces the midpoint (p.52/50%, inside sequence 5, not at the end of 4). The instructive finding: sequences 1, 3, 4, 6 and 7 all end with the hero holding *less* than when he started, and every sequence ends on a **physical handover** — an object changing hands, a door sealing, a vehicle leaving — never on a decision. That grab/lose/re-grab pulse, not accumulating wins, is what makes the form read as a serial. See also `../../../structure-library/et-the-extra-terrestrial.md`: sequence 1 asks "can he get back to the ship?" and answers **no**, and the unresolved need powers the remaining 106 pages.

### 3.4 Vogler — the Hero's Journey, 12 stages (1992)

| Stage `[P]` | 1 Ordinary World | 2 Call | 3 Refusal | 4 Mentor | 5 Threshold | 6 Tests/Allies | 7 Approach | 8 Ordeal | 9 Reward | 10 Road Back | 11 Resurrection | 12 Elixir |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| % | 1–10 pp | ~10 | 10–20 | 15–20 | ~25 | 25–50 | 40–50 | ~50 | 55–60 | ~75 | ~90 | end |

Archetypes are **functions**, not people: Hero, Mentor, Threshold Guardian, Herald, Shapeshifter, Shadow, Ally, Trickster. One character can carry several; several can carry one.

**Use when:** fantasy, sci-fi epic, adventure, sports, war · coming-of-age with a literal journey · animation · the protagonist must be physically removed from home to be changed.
**Avoid when:** static protagonists · chamber drama · network narratives · heroes who refuse change · stories whose obstacle is a community rather than a threshold.
**Criticism:** unfalsifiable when force-fit — *Chinatown* has no annihilation/rebirth and the model will manufacture one; male-coded in origin (Campbell's reported "Women don't need to make the journey"); post-*Star Wars* Hollywood converted a description into a prescription.
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/mulan.md` — every external stage is present and well-placed `[M]` (Call p.18, Threshold p.21/21%, Ordeal pp.63–72, Reward p.72, Road Back p.80, Resurrection pp.86–94) and the lens still fails in three specific ways: the mentor arrives *after* the threshold and is a self-interested fraud; the Reward ("you have my trust," p.73) is confiscated four pages later; the elixir is refused on the page, set aside unlooked-at by the father at p.98. The diagnosis is not "poor fit" but **two protagonists in one body** — Vogler describes Ping, who is exposed and effectively killed off at p.76. Also `../../../structure-library/dune.md`, which completes the Journey by *refusing* it: Jessica offers escape offworld (p.109) and Paul says "No." An omitted stage reads as a defect; a declined one reads as characterisation.

### 3.5 Harmon — the Story Circle

**1 You** (comfort) → **2 Need** → **3 Go** (threshold) → **4 Search** → **5 Find** (get what they wanted; ≈ midpoint) → **6 Take** (heavy price) → **7 Return** → **8 Change.** Feature mapping `[P]`: 1–2 ≈ Act I, 3 ≈ 25%, 4 ≈ 25–50%, 5 ≈ 50%, 6 ≈ 50–75%, 7 ≈ 75–90%, 8 ≈ climax. Fractal — runs per scene, per plotline, per season.

**Use when:** contained character stories · comedy · animation · shorts · the first outline of anything, in any format · you need a spine before lunch.
**Avoid when:** sprawling ensembles · information-design mysteries · anti-change protagonists · pilots, which must refuse closure.
**Criticism:** reductive — no B-story, no theme machinery, no antagonist apparatus; sitcom-calibrated "change" is often cosmetic.
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/tv-ted-lasso-pilot.md` — steps 1–4 map scene for scene `[M]` (Go at 23%, Search occupying 46% of the running time), and steps 5–8 have to be argued for, because **the circle is a desire engine and this protagonist states no want in 39 pages.** Find degrades to "acquire allies you didn't ask for," Take sits terminally at 92–100%, Return is absent, and the visible Change belongs to the supporting cast. The honest reading is a half-circle for the hero welded to a full circle for the antagonist, joined at the midpoint reveal (p.21/54%). Corollary for pilots: expect the ring to break at Return, and read the break as the series promise. See also `../../../structure-library/tv-russian-doll-pilot.md` — a loop gets step 7 free, so step 8 must carry all the meaning; that script's answer is to make the change epistemic rather than moral.

### 3.6 McKee — Story (1997)

Five-part and fractal (same shape at scene, sequence, act, story): **Inciting Incident** (on screen, no later than 25%) → **Progressive Complications** (widening across inner, personal and extra-personal levels; each action closes the gap between expectation and result, forcing riskier action; no repeated beats) → **Crisis** as *dilemma* (irreconcilable goods, or the lesser of two evils) → **Climax** (the choice enacted; irreversible value change) → **Resolution**. No page numbers, on principle.

Doctrine you should actually use: scenes turn on **value charges** (+/−); the **Controlling Idea** is value + cause in one sentence; **negation of the negation** (the deepest antagonism is the value's perverse double — not hate but self-hate); the **obligatory scene** the inciting incident makes the audience anticipate. Story Triangle: archplot / miniplot / antiplot.

**Use when:** drama, crime, thriller — any archplot · diagnosing a dead draft · finding why a scene is inert (usually: no value charge, or a repeated beat) · designing a climax as a dilemma.
**Avoid when:** you need a prescriptive scaffold with mileposts · outlining under deadline · the writer needs page targets to finish.
**Criticism:** descriptive rather than generative, and the seminar dogmatism satirised in *Adaptation*.
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/no-country-for-old-men.md` — the obligatory scene is **promised out loud** (Chigurh takes Wells's phone and offers Moss terms at ~72–73% `[M]`) and never paid: the two men never meet in 117 pages, the apparent protagonist dies off-screen at 87%, and the POV transfers to Bell for a fifteen-page inquest. The transferable verdict: *you can only withhold a climax you have actually built.* Pages 1–101 are a conventional pursuit thriller executed at the highest level — that is the credit balance the refusal is drawn against. See also `../../../structure-library/erin-brockovich.md` for fifteen catalogued progressive complications in which the gap is never about information; it is about status.

### 3.7 Truby — The Anatomy of Story (2007)

The loudest institutional critic of three-act ("a mechanical device superimposed on the story"). The replacement is **organic**: a designing principle, a **moral argument**, and a **character web** in which hero and opponents are competing answers to one moral question.

**7 key steps:** Weakness & Need (psychological *and* moral) → Desire → Opponent (wants the same thing; attacks the weakness) → Plan → Battle → Self-Revelation → New Equilibrium. The 22-step expansion adds the reveal sequence — first revelation and decision, opponent's counterattack, drive, attack by ally, **apparent defeat**, obsessive drive, audience revelation, gate/gauntlet/visit to death, battle, self-revelation, **moral decision**. Deliberately ordinal, never page-mapped: "an accordion."

**Use when:** character-driven drama, crime, morally complex prestige material · the antagonist is underwritten (Truby is best-in-class here) · a mystery or thriller whose reveals need sequencing · heavy plotters who want the outline to *be* the argument.
**Avoid when:** light high-concept comedy that needs speed · discovery writers (the 22 steps produce over-engineered outlines) · you need pacing guidance, which Truby refuses to supply.
**Criticism:** overwhelming for beginners, and — despite the anti-act rhetoric — his steps still cluster into the familiar movements he says do not exist.
**Worked example:** no map in the library runs Truby's 22 steps as a formal pass; the closest demonstration of his machinery is `../../../structure-library/mean-girls.md`. Opponent and hero want the same thing (social supremacy), so the antagonist is a mirror rather than an obstacle, and the moral argument is the spine. The finding: the threshold is crossed in **four gates** `[M]` — invitation p.17/15%, social p.19/17%, **moral p.29/25%**, mission p.39/33% — because a character who deliberates at a single door cannot be corrupted convincingly. Put the small, deniable, irreversible compromise on the 25% mark. The self-revelation (the apology, p.97) is the only major beat with no V.O.: the device is withdrawn, and the silence is the arc.

### 3.8 Hauge — Six Stages / Five Turning Points

| Stage `[P]` | I Setup 0–10% | II New Situation 10–25% | III Progress 25–50% | IV Complications 50–75% | V Final Push 75–90% | VI Aftermath |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Turning point | **TP1 Opportunity 10%** | **TP2 Change of Plans 25%** — desire becomes a specific, visible goal | **TP3 Point of No Return 50%** — bridges burned | **TP4 Major Setback 75%** | **TP5 Climax 90–99%** | — |

Twin engine: the **outer journey** (visible goal, clear finish line) runs in parallel with the **inner journey from identity to essence** — identity being the protective persona built on a wound. The climax forces the choice of essence over identity; the love interest is the character who sees the essence.

**Use when:** romance, rom-com, inspirational drama, commercial star vehicles · you need arc and plot welded explicitly rather than tracked in separate columns · pitching to a romance-literate audience.
**Avoid when:** ensembles · non-linear · goal-less protagonists · tragedy · interior dramas, where the "visible goal" requirement forces a false external want.
**Criticism:** rigid symmetry — the maximalist claim that all successful Hollywood films share one basic structure does not survive measurement; real classics drift.
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/erin-brockovich.md`, one of Hauge's own published analyses. Measured against his percentages `[M]`: TP2 lands at 33–36% (not 25%), TP3 at **50.4%** to the page ("if, and only if, you find me the evidence" — the terms of the climax stated aloud 62 pages early), TP4 at 79–81%, climax at 96–97%. The centre is exact; the first turn is eleven points late. The script pays for the delay by running a *complete small story* — a full job-hunt with its own crisis and its own bottom — in the space before the turn. A long first act is affordable when it is not only setup.

### 3.9 Yorke — Into the Woods, five fractal acts (2013)

Five acts symmetrical around the midpoint: home world + call → doubt → **Act 3 (centre): experiment; MIDPOINT = the "big gift," truth glimpsed at dead centre** → the price: doubt, regression, worst point → reawakening, climax, mastery. The **Roadmap of Change** tracks knowledge: No Knowledge → Growing → Awakening → Doubt → Acceptance → Experiment → **midpoint revelation** → Doubt → Regression → Reawakening → Total Mastery.
**Use when:** you want to understand why beats exist · long-form and TV-adjacent features · change-driven drama · reconciling two frameworks that disagree. **Avoid when:** you need a drafting checklist — Yorke supplies almost no page-level guidance.
**Criticism:** post-hoc pattern-matching risk; "less a hands-on guide than a light read."
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/tv-barry-pilot.md` — turns measured at 31% / 49% / 71% / 86% `[M]`, feature architecture at half-hour scale, in a script that prints no act breaks at all. Yorke's doctrine proved by inversion: **the midpoint is an invitation, not a reversal.** At p.17/49% the acting teacher demands a monologue and Sally invites him to Residuals — the exact centre is where the wrong life offers the protagonist a future. Everything before is Barry being pulled; everything after is Barry choosing, and paying.

### 3.10 Mazin — the central dramatic argument (Scriptnotes 403, 2019)

"Structure is a symptom of a character's relationship with a central dramatic argument. Structure isn't the dog. It's the tail." Start from a **debatable** proposition, never a topic word. Then: stasis in anti-theme → ironic inciting disruption tailored to this character's fear → anti-theme reinforcement → doubt, and a tentative try punished by cruel reversal, with **midpoint = a glimpse of the theme** → low point = losing both worldviews → transformation → a final temptation to regress, rejected by free and costly choice. Denouement mirrors the opening, inverted.
**Use when:** character-driven drama in any genre · prestige film/TV · ensembles with no protagonist · writers allergic to beat sheets · the "second-act problem" is really a theme problem. **Avoid when:** assembly-line rewrites against studio beat notes · writers who need mileposts to finish · you do not yet know the argument.
**Criticism:** no pacing scaffold — beginners drown; Mazin himself concedes templates are fine for post-hoc diagnosis.
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/tv-chernobyl-ep1.md` — the argument is stated as a **question in the first line of the series** ("What is the cost of lies?"), which converts 53 pages from narrative into demonstration and licenses a structure with no protagonist and no goal-obstacle spine. Its most naked statement is not dialogue but an **action line** at p.44/81% `[M]`: "Applause for delusion. Applause for death." An ensemble with no protagonist has nowhere else to put a verdict. Note also that the counter-thesis is given its best advocate (Zharkov, who argues that believing the State is a virtue independent of facts) and is the only character who wins his scene — that is what makes the proposition arguable rather than a slogan.

### 3.11 Four-act / Thompson (Field IIa-IIb; Kristin Thompson, 1999)

Four roughly **equal 25% parts** `[P]`: Setup → Complicating Action (the goal is recast) → Development (struggle, delay, little net progress) → Climax (action streamlines to resolution) + epilogue. Naming the midpoint an act break kills the sag by giving the middle two distinct jobs.
**Use when:** TV hour of any kind · features whose Act Two is inert · teaching or diagnosing in quarters · network/cable drafting discipline. **Avoid when:** you expect it to add machinery — it adds a label, not a method.
**Criticism:** functionally a relabelled three-act; Thompson's counts are descriptive statistics, not instructions.
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/tv-severance-pilot.md` prints four acts, a prologue and a tag for a platform with no commercial breaks, and three of its five act-outs are moments of stillness rather than jeopardy. `../../../structure-library/tv-this-is-us-pilot.md` prints nothing, yet its inferred act lengths run 8.5 / 9 / 10 / 9 / 8.3 / 8.1 pp `[M]` — more regular than several scripts that do print them. **Act labels are a drafting tool that survives independently of whether anything is scheduled to interrupt the episode.**

### 3.12 Murdock — The Heroine's Journey (1990)

Ten circular stages: Separation from the Feminine → Identification with the Masculine → Road of Trials → **Illusory Boon of Success** → Awakening to Spiritual Aridity → Descent to the Goddess → Yearning to Reconnect → Healing the Mother/Daughter Split → Healing the Wounded Masculine → Integration.
**Use when:** burnout and identity arcs · a protagonist who wins and finds the win hollow · stories Campbell cannot describe because the cost of success *is* the story. **Avoid when:** external life-or-death quests · you need placements, which it barely supplies (it is a therapy framework).
**Criticism:** its terminal stages assume a maternal reconciliation many stories simply do not contain.
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/mulan.md` — Murdock is **the most useful diagnostic and the least accurate map**. "Illusory Boon of Success" names pp.72–75 exactly (a triumph awarded to a person who does not exist) where Vogler's "Seizing the Sword" cannot. But her Descent is to a wisecracking male dragon, and the mother/daughter split has no referent: Fa Li has no dialogue after p.22 and no scene with Mulan after p.15. The split that heals is father/daughter. Borrow her vocabulary; do not run her as the spine.

### 3.13 Hudson — The Virgin's Promise (2010)

Growth by internal awakening **in place**, not by external quest. Thirteen beats: Dependent World → Price of Conformity → Opportunity to Shine → Dresses the Part → Secret World → No Longer Fits Her World → Caught Shining → Gives Up What Kept Her Stuck → Kingdom in Chaos → Wanders in the Wilderness → Chooses Her Light → Re-ordering (Rescue) → The Kingdom is Brighter. Gender-neutral in application.
**Use when:** coming-of-age, coming-out, artist and "secret talent" stories, romance · the antagonist is a community rather than a person · the hero's problem is conformity. **Avoid when:** external life-or-death quests where the protagonist must physically leave and return.
**Criticism:** narrow by design — outside its story class it has no dragon and no war beat, so plot events sit outside the structure.
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/mulan.md` — thirteen beats for thirteen, in order, at the right percentages `[M]`, including literal name matches: **Dresses the Part** is the haircut (p.21/21%); **Caught Shining** is the exposure (p.76/**75%**), Hudson's definition being that the secret is discovered at the moment of greatest success, which is exactly what a wound taken while saving the captain produces. Run Hudson and Vogler together and the page where they separate is the finding.

### 3.14 Weiland — the lie/truth arc

Hook → Inciting Event ~12% → First Plot Point 25% → First Pinch ~37% → Midpoint "Moment of Truth" 50% → Second Pinch ~62% → Third Plot Point 75% → Climax ~90% `[P]`. Signature: the **Lie the Character Believes vs the Truth**, mapped beat-for-beat onto those percentages, in positive-change, flat (the protagonist holds the Truth and the world changes) and negative variants.
**Use when:** you have the plot and not the arc, or vice versa, and need them on one grid · flat-arc protagonists (procedurals, westerns, many series leads) · corruption stories. **Avoid when:** ensembles · protagonists whose belief system is not the subject.
**Criticism:** the percentages are inherited convention rather than measurement; the machinery is Field's with an arc column bolted on.
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/the-matrix.md` — the Lie is external and the script **revokes it at 58%** `[M]`: the Oracle tells Neo he is not the One, and he enters Act Three saying so aloud ("I'm not the One, Trinity… Because I believe in something"). Stripping the protagonist of every external justification is what makes the Truth a choice instead of a prophecy. Negative-arc case: `../../../structure-library/mean-girls.md`, where the Lie becomes the face at p.71/61%, and Fun and Games and Bad Guys Close In are literally the same footage read twice.

### 3.15 Story Grid (Shawn Coyne)

McKee systematised for editing. The **Five Commandments** must be present in every unit — beat, scene, sequence, act, subplot, global: Inciting Incident → Progressive Complication ending in a Turning Point → Crisis (binary: best bad choice / irreconcilable goods) → Climax → Resolution. Plus content genres with **obligatory scenes and conventions** (miss them and the contract breaks) and per-scene value tracking.
**Use when:** revision, not drafting · auditing genre delivery · the note is "it isn't landing" and you need to find which unit has no turn. **Avoid when:** first drafts · discovery writing · anywhere the jargon overhead costs more than it returns.
**Criticism:** heavy jargon; post-draft orientation by design.
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/tv-mare-of-easttown-ep1.md` — the genre's obligatory inciting scene, the body, arrives at **~98% of an 80-page episode** `[M]`, because the true A-story is the grief spine and the case is only its delivery mechanism. The rule the map states: earn every reveal; the shocking reveal you have not earned is worthless. Use Story Grid to check the obligatory scenes exist at all, then argue about where they sit.

### 3.16 Kishōtenketsu (起承転結)

Four parts, no mandatory conflict. **Ki** (introduction, 0–25%) → **Shō** (development without major change, 25–50%) → **Ten** (the twist, 50–75%, often striking 60–70%; an unexpected, seemingly unrelated turn — the structural centre of gravity) → **Ketsu** (conclusion, 75–100%; meaning emerges from juxtaposition). Momentum comes from contrast and recontextualisation, not protagonist-vs-obstacle.
**Use when:** slice-of-life, family drama, mood pieces, anthology segments, animation · the payoff is a recontextualising turn rather than a defeated antagonist. **Avoid when:** the genre contract demands escalating opposition (thriller, action, mystery).
**Criticism:** audiences trained on causality read *shō* as "nothing happening," and the form is hard to pitch in goal-oriented terms.
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/parasite.md` — p.71/**50.4%** is a genuine *ten*, on four testable grounds `[M]`: it does not escalate the existing goal, it voids it; it is introduced by a character with no prior function in the plan; it works by re-description rather than conflict; and the fourth movement reconciles rather than resolves. A pure three-act pass mislabels p.71 "the midpoint," which is technically true and analytically useless — it hides the fact that the second half has a different protagonist, genre, causal logic and question.

### 3.17 Miniplot / anti-plot (McKee's triangle)

**Miniplot:** internal conflict, passive or multiple protagonists, open endings, quieter causality. **Anti-plot:** coincidence over causality, non-linear time, inconsistent realities. Two rules: master classical form before deviating (both are *reactions* to archplot and depend on audience literacy); and every step from the classical corner shrinks the audience — a budget decision, not only an aesthetic one.
**Use when:** the subject is a state rather than a want · memory pieces · you can name precisely what replaces the spine. **Avoid when:** you cannot name that replacement · commercial specs · anyone's first script.
**Criticism:** McKee's own — most miniplot scripts are archplot scripts with the causality removed by accident rather than by design.
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/aftersun.md` — there is no plot; there is a holiday of fixed length wrapped in a tape being played. Two mechanisms replace the spine `[M]`: a **borrowed clock** (return date, school term, a birthday two days off, a tournament bracket) so the reader feels structure where there is only calendar; and **one quarantined conventional arc** — the rave, about 4.8 pages total across pp.1, 21, 48, 69, 84 and 97, advancing exactly one variable, how close she can get. Feed the audience's need for direction on a metronome and they will tolerate ninety pages of a family on holiday.

### 3.18 Tonal-shift architecture

Acts defined by genre/tone gear-changes rather than only by plot turns. Three rules: the shift must be **planted** (the new genre's seeds visible in retrospect); it must land at a **structural joint** (midpoint or act break), never mid-scene; and the deeper story question must **survive** the shift. Distinguish from unplanned tonal drift — architecture is designed, drift is leakage.
**Use when:** you want genre itself to evolve · satire escalating into horror or tragedy · half-hour drama built on register collision. **Avoid when:** the shift is a rescue for a stalled middle · the second genre has no cause in the first.
**Criticism:** almost always misapplied — writers plant the *tone* and forget to plant the *agent* of the change.
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/parasite.md` — the hinge is a doorbell at p.71/50.4%, and the person at the door is the housekeeper the protagonists got fired at p.51 `[M]`. That is the test for any script attempting this: **name the page in the first half where the protagonist caused the second half.** Supporting technique from the same map: rehearse the climax as comedy (the stone swung at a drunk, p.59; the shot-put photos on the wall, p.6), so the violence at pp.124–129 reads as arithmetic rather than escalation.

### 3.19 Non-linear patterns

Taxonomy: bookend/frame · flashback-driven · reverse chronology · parallel timelines · hyperlink/network · circular or loop · Rashomon/multiple POV · puzzle film · real-time · anthology. Three tests before committing: **the removal test** (if the story survives being re-cut chronologically, it is a gimmick); **form = psychology or theme**; and the **experienced-order law** — the revelations must themselves escalate and still land the five load-bearing beats in the order the audience receives them. Standard practice: write the chronology first, then design the telling order.
**Use when:** two eras genuinely explain each other · a mind's distortion is the subject · withheld information is the suspense engine. **Avoid when:** the reordering is decoration, or the underlying causality has not been written.
**Criticism:** the reliable failure is fragmentation — audience shrinkage plus pacing collapse.
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/oppenheimer.md` — make the two strands **structurally unequal** `[M]`: Fission holds 82% of pages in blocks averaging 6.2 pp, Fusion 18% in blocks averaging 1.4 pp, so the minor strand is punctuation and interrogation rather than a co-equal narrative, which is why three hours of non-linear material stays legible. Vary the cutting rate as a curve, not a pattern: one switch per 33 pages through Trinity, one per 1.8 pages after the reveal, one per page in the last fourteen. See also `../../../structure-library/the-father.md`: order the reality-violations by category and never de-escalate (fact → space → identity → eyewitness → causal order → narrator → repeated scene → migrating props → mid-scene substitution → violence → the sane character's endorsement → location, p.79/91%). Randomised confusion reads as sloppiness; a ratchet reads as an accelerating story.

### 3.20 Mystery architectures — whodunit vs howcatchem

Decide which half of the knowledge triad you live in. **Fair-play whodunit:** hide the crime; the audience could theoretically solve it; obligatory scenes are the gathering, the accusation and the reconstruction. **Inverted mystery / howcatchem:** show the crime first; tension is the detective closing the gap; the pleasure is irony, not mystery. Serialised variants: layered revelation (earn every reveal) and puzzle-box, where mystery is the engine and the structural risk is *debt* — answers owed compounding faster than they are paid.
**Use when:** who-knows-what is the plot · you can build a clue ledger before you draft. **Avoid when:** you have not decided what the audience knows at every page.
**Criticism:** the whodunit's failure mode is a solution the audience could not have reached; the howcatchem's is a replay that is recap rather than revelation.
**Worked example:** `../../../structure-library/glass-onion.md` (whodunit) — the hinge at p.73/54% is carried by **no device at all**: a `HARD CUT TO:` and a mirrored slug line, `A FRONT DOOR, PAINTED BLACK` → `PAINTED WHITE` `[M]`. Sixteen pages of *entirely new* material precede the first repeated scene, and 55 pages of Pass One compress to 24 — never open a second pass with repetition. `../../../structure-library/tv-poker-face-pilot.md` (howcatchem) — crime 9 pp, detection 56 pp, and the crime half conceals exactly one thing: who the unseen woman across the table is (p.9). One withheld *identity* converts a 22-page replay from repetition into revelation. If you must cheat in a howcatchem, cheat on whose eyes we are behind, never on what happened.

### 3.21 Adams — the Story Spine

**Once upon a time…** (the stable world) → **Every day…** (the rule it runs on) → **But one day…** (the disturbance) → **Because of that…** ×3 (the causal chain; three is customary, the number is elastic) → **Until finally…** (the climax) → **Ever since then…** (the new world). Eight sentences. No page numbers, no percentages, and none implied — **the Spine contains no proportion at all.** Crosswalk: "But one day" = Disturbance, the first "Because of that" = Commitment, the last = Collapse, "Until finally" = Proof. **There is no midpoint slot**, which is the single thing you must supply yourself before this becomes a spec.

**It is a generative tool, not an analytic one — say so out loud when you hand it over.** Kenn Adams built it for improvised long-form theatre, where the job is to keep a scene producing consequence in real time, and it reached screenwriting through Pixar as a pitch shape (Emma Coats's story rules, 2011). It will produce a well-formed causal chain from almost any premise, including premises with nothing at stake. So it can tell you whether your story has *consequence*; it can tell you nothing about whether it is any good. Never run it as a diagnosis, and never write a beat sheet in its vocabulary — convert to the primary framework's terms first.

**Use when:** Workflow 2 has routed a discovery writer here · the writer has a premise, an image or a character and no shape · you need to convert an "and then" outline into a "because of that" one in one pass · the opening ten minutes of any session where the story does not yet exist.
**Avoid when:** anything is being audited — the draft exists, so use McKee or Story Grid · you need placement, proportion, theme, an antagonist or an arc, none of which the Spine models · the writer will mistake a completed Spine for a completed structure, which is its most common failure in the room.
**Criticism:** it is an improv exercise wearing a screenwriting hat. The "Because of that" chain guarantees causality and nothing else; the "Ever since then" clause invites a tidy moral that most serious films refuse; and because it fits everything, it discriminates nothing.
**Worked example:** **none, and honestly so.** No map in the library runs the Spine as a formal pass, because the library is 42 autopsies and the Spine is a drafting instrument — there is nothing in it to measure. Every claim in this entry is `[P]`. If the writer wants a fast spine that *does* have a centre and a price, hand them Harmon (§3.5) instead and keep the Spine for the first twenty minutes.

### 3.22 Braided / network narrative structures (ensemble · hyperlink · parallel-protagonist)

Not a framework but a family, and the only entry here whose beats belong to *threads* rather than to a story. Three sub-types: **ensemble** — one world, several co-leads with separate A-stories (Succession, Desperate Housewives); **hyperlink / network** — strangers whose connection is itself the reveal (the term "hyperlink cinema" is Alissa Quart's, *Film Comment* 2005, popularised by Ebert on *Syriana*; film studies calls it the network narrative, after Bordwell's "Mutual Friends and Chronologies of Chance," *Poetics of Cinema*, 2008); and **parallel-protagonist** — two or more complete arcs run side by side and welded late (This Is Us, BEEF).

There are no page targets and there will not be any. What the library measures instead are four dials, and they are the whole craft `[M]`:

| Dial | What the maps show |
|---|---|
| **Installation rate** | Succession installs 8 principals inside 39% and orders them **by distance from the deal, not by rank in the family** — the eldest child arrives *last*, at 39%, inverting birth order, and the outsiders (Lawrence, Alessandro, Frank, all p.2) arrive before three of the four heirs. Desperate Housewives installs 4 co-leads in 9 pages (pp.5–13) at a single wake, using one repeated sentence shape — "*X, who lives Y, brought Z*" — with the dish as the diagnosis. Mare of Easttown puts 43 named characters on screen (54 named in all) in 80 pages, one new name every page and a half, and survives it because **almost nobody is introduced to the detective**: Mare meets exactly one new person in the episode, so the *town* does the introducing. This Is Us gives four leads an age, a job, a room and a want in six pages |
| **Thread page-share** | Never equal, on purpose. Desperate Housewives: Susan ~31%, Bree ~19%, Lynette ~17%, Gabrielle ~17%, Edie ~8% — its map's verdict is that "a 'true' four-way split would leave no one to follow." This Is Us: the LA twins 46.6%, Jack 24.7%, Randall 24.6%, Kate lightest at 15.4% solo — and compensated by being handed the pilot's most exposed emotional beat and the reveal's delivery line. Mare runs two spines rather than four threads, and the case spine is the *smaller* one |
| **Cut rate** | This Is Us measures it directly: 23 blocks, 22 inter-block cuts, average 2.27 pp, and the braid **tightens into the reveal** — 2.23 pp through the introduction rotation, slowing by 42% to 3.17 pp for the long middle scenes, back to 2.85, then 1.15 pp across the last 15% (7 blocks in 8 pages). Its two longest blocks are its two confrontations (4.68 and 4.16 pp), placed back to back: **when a braid needs to stop braiding and just play a scene, it does, for nine consecutive pages** |
| **Convergence** | Desperate Housewives converges on one Friday night via a single overheard line in a Starbucks queue (p.44) and an arson that puts four women on one lawn. This Is Us converges by wrapping a 1979 scene *inside* a 2015 sentence, so the reveal is an interruption of one line of dialogue rather than a jump between stories. Succession converges physically — four cities in the first quarter, one apartment for the middle third, one field and two helicopters at the end: **as the space contracts, the number of simultaneous negotiations rises** |

**The rule all four maps agree on: substitute a question for the protagonist, then service it with absolute discipline.** Succession's is asked by a junior banker at 13%, formalised into a legal instrument at 58%, answered *no* at 60%, tested by five separate private negotiations between 74% and 92%, and made permanently unanswerable at 96% `[M]`. Its map states the transferable version: **an ensemble may defer the protagonist for as long as it likes, provided it never defers the question, and provided each candidate gets at least one scene in which they are unambiguously the POV.**

**Two supporting laws, both measured.** (1) **Four A-stories stay A-stories only if each runs a different genre against a different antagonist who is physically present in the world** — Desperate Housewives runs rom-com, domestic farce, marital drama and adultery caper, and no storyline borrows its stakes from another; the moment one does, it is a B-story. (2) **Ensemble scenes are rare and load-bearing** — four in 59 pages (~12%), each doing three jobs at once: refresh the premise, let each lead comment on another's plot, and advance or reset the serialized question.

**Use when:** the subject is a group, a family, a town or a system · Workflow 2 has routed an ensemble here · an ensemble pilot or limited series · two eras or two strands that genuinely explain each other · there is no protagonist and you can name the question that replaces one.
**Avoid when:** one character's want actually drives the story and the "ensemble" is really a cast list · you cannot name each thread's own antagonist and own stakes · a feature under 100 pages, where installation cost eats the second act.
**Criticism:** the reliable failure is a braid of B-stories — several threads, none with stakes of its own, converging because the writer needs them to; and the network variant's standing charge is that coincidence does the work causality should, manufacturing significance out of proximity. The library supplies its own honest caution: Desperate Housewives's ensemble load is uneven enough that **Bree has no scene of her own between p.11 and p.29**, an 18-page gap patched by a single image and a V.O. montage — a script without a narrator would have to pay for those re-entries with scenes.
**Worked example:** four, and read the one that matches your problem. `../../../structure-library/tv-succession-pilot.md` — installation order and the deferred protagonist. `../../../structure-library/tv-desperate-housewives-pilot.md` — four A-stories, and the narrator as connective tissue: because a narrator can move between houses, the script never needs a scene whose only job is to get from one woman to another, which is the cost that normally forces ensembles into A/B/C hierarchy. `../../../structure-library/tv-this-is-us-pilot.md` — the measured cut-rate curve, and 19 thematic bridges used *in place of* plot connections, because no cut in it can hand off on plot. `../../../structure-library/tv-mare-of-easttown-ep1.md` — a community web dense enough to hide a killer, built entirely before a case exists.

### 3.23 Siegel — the nine-act structure (David Siegel)

Nine acts numbered **0 to 8**, and the numbering is the doctrine: the story is already running before page one.

| Act `[P]` | 0 Backstory | 1 Opening image | 2 Inciting incident | 3 Hero emerges | 4 Point of no return | 5 **The wrong goal** | 6 Revelation | 7 Final push | 8 Resolution |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Function | forces already in motion; the disaster has roots | place, period, tone | "something bad happens" — Siegel wants it inside the first four minutes | the full cast installed, protagonist and antagonist each with allies; the goal becomes visible | the hero commits, willingly or under pressure | **the longest act.** The hero pursues the *wrong* goal on incomplete information; complications mount; it ends at the villain's peak | the right goal arrives, at the low point, with the missing information | correct goal, ticking clock — and the win has a price | the problem solved, the loose ends, the intended feeling |

Two signatures, and they are why `genre-contracts.md` §4 makes this the thriller's first choice. **First, the dual goal.** Acts 5 and 6 split the middle into a wrong objective and a right one, which is a different machine from a midpoint reversal: it is not that the price of what the hero wants goes up, it is that the hero has been solving the wrong problem — and the audience often knows it first, which converts the whole middle into dramatic irony rather than suspense. **Second, villain-first plotting**: the antagonist's need to execute his plan drives the story, so you plot his story to completion and then intersect the hero with it. That rule is already carried in `genre-contracts.md` §3.3, alongside Rossio's corollary that fate may favour the antagonist but coincidence may never save the hero.

The model is **not page-mapped** beyond that early inciting incident, and Siegel published no percentages. Treat every placement above as ordinal.

**Use when:** thriller, action, hostage or heist material · TV movies, where nine acts map cleanly onto commercial breaks · the antagonist has a plan and the hero spends the middle chasing the wrong thing · a "second-act problem" that is really a *goal* problem rather than a theme problem.
**Avoid when:** the antagonist is a condition rather than a person — grief, a system, weather · character drama with no plan to execute · anything in which the hero's goal is correct throughout, which is most romance and most coming-of-age.
**Criticism:** it is Field's paradigm cut into finer slices — nine labels over the same skeleton, with no new mechanism except the wrong-goal/right-goal split, which Truby's "apparent defeat" and Weiland's lie/truth arc both already carry. The published material is thin and dated, and the model's real currency in a room is the maxim ("plot the villain first"), not the nine acts.
**Worked example:** **none — no map in the library runs Siegel's nine acts as a formal pass, so treat the table above as `[P]` throughout.** The closest measured demonstrations of the machinery are two: `../../../structure-library/no-country-for-old-men.md`, where the antagonist's plan is the engine and the obligatory confrontation is *promised out loud* at 68–77% `[M]` and then never paid; and `../../../structure-library/tv-black-mirror-national-anthem.md`, where one unfalsifiable demand is issued at p.5 (7%) and executed at p.57 (88%) `[M]` — everything between is the interval between statement and enforcement, which is Act 5 in the purest form the library holds. Note what that second case also shows: the protagonist makes exactly one decision (p.44), and it fails. A villain-first structure has to be watched for exactly that, or Act 5 becomes 40 pages of a hero being moved around.

## 4. Combining frameworks

**Pairs that genuinely complement** — different jobs, no overlap:

| Pair | Why it works | Set it up as |
|---|---|---|
| Field + eight sequences | Field supplies four tentpoles; sequences fill the 60-page hole between them | PRIMARY Field · SECONDARY sequences |
| Save the Cat + genre contract (STC genres or Story Grid) | Beats give placement; the contract gives obligatory content | PRIMARY STC · SECONDARY genre audit |
| Vogler + Hudson | Diagnostic pair for identity stories: run both, read the page where they separate | Whichever wins on the evidence is PRIMARY |
| Mazin + any page-mapped sheet | Mazin supplies the argument; the sheet supplies the mileposts he refuses to give | PRIMARY Mazin · SECONDARY Field/STC |
| Kishōtenketsu + McKee | Four-part for the macro shape; McKee for the tragic engine inside the *ten* | PRIMARY kishō · SECONDARY McKee |
| Yorke + Harmon | Yorke explains why the midpoint is load-bearing; Harmon gets a spine onto the page fast | PRIMARY Harmon (drafting) · SECONDARY Yorke (diagnosis) |
| Truby + Story Grid | Truby builds the moral argument; Story Grid audits whether each unit turns | PRIMARY Truby · SECONDARY Story Grid |
| Braided/network + Mazin | The braid supplies thread architecture and convergence; Mazin supplies the one argument that lets a protagonist-less ensemble cohere | PRIMARY braided · SECONDARY Mazin |
| Story Spine + any page-mapped sheet | The Spine gets causality onto a page in ten minutes; the sheet then supplies the proportion the Spine has none of | PRIMARY Spine (first session only) · SECONDARY Field/STC, promoted to PRIMARY once beats exist |

**Pairs that are redundant** — running both buys nothing and doubles the beat sheet: Campbell + Vogler (Vogler *is* Campbell for screen) · Harmon + Vogler (Harmon is Campbell compressed) · McKee + Story Grid as co-primaries (Story Grid is McKee's five-part with a jargon layer) · Field + Hauge + Weiland (one skeleton at three granularities — Hauge is Field with percentages, Weiland is Hauge with a lie column) · Four-act/Thompson + Field (Thompson promotes the midpoint to an act break; a label, not machinery) · Save the Cat + Soth's Mini-Movie Method or Edson's Hero Goal Sequences (STC's 15 beats already carry the sequence pulse) · Story Spine + Harmon's circle (both are compressed causal engines for a first pass, and the circle strictly dominates because it at least has a centre and a price) · Siegel Nine-Act + Field or eight sequences (nine labels over one skeleton; Siegel adds the wrong-goal split and nothing else, so take the split and keep the sequences).

**The rule, from the cross-script synthesis.** On the hardest scripts **no single framework is sufficient, and the informative move is to run two and locate the disagreement** rather than forcing one to fit. The disagreement is the finding, not a failure of the analysis.

- *Mulan* — Vogler and Hudson disagree at p.76, because there are two protagonists in one body: the Hero's Journey's climax lands at 67%, the Virgin's Promise's at 85–93%.
- *Parasite* — kishōtenketsu and three-act disagree about what p.71 is, because the second half has a different protagonist and a different question.
- *Ted Lasso* — the Story Circle fits the first half and fails the second, because the hero has no want and the antagonist owns the plot.

**How to record it in the spec.** Put both lenses on the `Framework(s):` line (PRIMARY / SECONDARY), write the beat sheet in the PRIMARY's vocabulary, and log the disagreement as a numbered, dated **Decision** — the choice, the alternative rejected, the reason. Never delete a Decision; supersede it. Then run the two `[M]` tolerances over the result: **a midpoint outside 47–55% is a loud flag; a first-act break anywhere in 10–38% is not a problem.** That is the inverse of how most beat-sheet tools behave, and it is what the evidence supports.
