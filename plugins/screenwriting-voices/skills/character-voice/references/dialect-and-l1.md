# Dialect & L1 — Writing Origin Into Voice

How to make a character's English carry another language or a nonstandard variety without turning
the character into the deviation. This file defines the intensity dial that `axes.md` (Group E —
Origin) points to, and supplies the feature kits a voice.md draws from. Features appear here the
way a voice.md renders them: named mechanism plus a worked line in a character's mouth.

## The Method

**Carry origin in grammar, idiom, and rhythm — never in respelled pronunciation.** Word order,
tense and aspect choices, article behavior, calqued idioms, sentence shape: these are what actually
differ between a Lyon-born engineer's English and her colleague's, and the reader's inner ear
rebuilds the accent from them automatically. A Russian L1 speaker drops articles because Russian
has none; a French L1 speaker asks by intonation because French has no auxiliary *do*. Structure is
the fingerprint. Spelling only announces that you noticed the character sounds different.

**No eye-dialect, and know why.** Respellings like "zis," "don'," "wuz" put the deviation, not the
person, at the forefront of every line. The device also carries specific historical weight:
American minstrel writing used nonstandard respelling to mark Black speakers as lesser, and the
technique still reads as status-coding no matter who it targets. Worse, much eye-dialect respells
pronunciations everyone uses — "wuz" is how every English speaker says *was* — so the only
information it transmits is "this character is beneath the standard."

**One respelling family, maximum — usually zero.** If a variety genuinely licenses a written
marker (Scots "cannae/dinnae" has centuries of print tradition behind it), keep exactly one such
family and apply it with total consistency. Apostrophe litter ("goin'," "an'") counts against this
budget, because dropped-g spelling is eye-dialect wearing a trench coat.

**Apply the necessity test.** Write visibly non-fluent English only when proficiency itself does
story work — an arc of improvement, a real communication barrier, a scene that turns on being
misunderstood. If the character simply *is* foreign, mark the voice lightly through idiom and
rhythm, or establish the language situation in an action line and write clean dialogue. Marking
speech just to signal "foreign" others the character. And the word is "non-fluent," never
"broken": fluency tracks dominant language and exposure, not birthplace.

**Circumlocution beats error.** A real L2 speaker who lacks a word talks around it: "the machine
for the dishes," "the — how do you say — the roof of the mouth." This is the most realistic marker
available, and it characterizes intelligence and effort where a grammar error only catalogs
deficiency. When in doubt, replace an error with a workaround.

**Code-switching needs a trigger.** Legitimate switch points: another speaker of the L1 enters; an
emotion spike; automatic language (counting, praying, pain); a word with no English equivalent.
Decorative confetti — "watch it, *arschloch*!" mid-fluent-sentence — fails because real bilinguals
switch for reasons, and readers feel the difference between motivated switching and sprinkled
seasoning. The same logic governs native-variety speakers moving between dialect and standard:
the shift happens at audience boundaries, and it is competence, not inconsistency.

**Proficiency is a dial, not a constant.** Stress, fatigue, anger, and alcohol reduce
self-monitoring, so L1 transfer thickens; rehearsed domains — the job, the practiced speech, the
anecdote told fifty times — run near-native; new topics, phone calls, and high stakes degrade.
Write the same character at two or three settings keyed to scene pressure. An error rate that
never moves is cardboard, and the movement itself is free characterization.

**Establish once, then trust the reader.** "Her thick Marseille accent" in one action line
outperforms fifty apostrophes, because after the establishing beat the reader's ear supplies the
sound in every unmarked line. So mix marked and unmarked lines deliberately — each marked line
taxes the reader a little, and a voice that pays that tax on every clause exhausts its welcome.
Keep the established feature set stable; deviate only for motivated code-switching or written
growth. Rhythm and lexicon do heavy lifting here: a drawl can live entirely in long looping
sentences, clipped city speech in short ones, and "y'all," "mate," "I reckon," kinship terms, and
calqued idioms all signal origin without deforming a single spelling.

**Never intelligence-code.** The dialect must not be the joke or the stupidity marker. A century
of dialect writing devolved into caricature exactly this way, and the pattern is why many readers
flinch at marked speech at all. The character's mind shows in what they say; the origin layer only
shapes how. Finally, have features checked by native speakers of the L1 or variety — they know
what is real transfer and what is stereotype, and outsiders writing a community need cultural
readers regardless of technique.

## The Intensity Dial

`axes.md` gives every active origin layer a dial: light / medium / heavy. These are the feature
budgets it refers to. Proficiency (survival / functional / fluent-marked / near-native, from Group
E) is the in-world fact and sets the ceiling; the dial is the rendering budget — what the page
shows in a given scene.

**Light** — the default: a high-proficiency L2 speaker, or a dialect speaker in formal register.
Rhythm and lexicon only: one signature idiom or calque per scene, an occasional article slip or
adverb placement, discourse tags ("isn't it?", "no?", "so it is"). Zero respelling. The reader
forgets the accent for lines at a time — that is the setting working, not failing.

**Medium** — an established L2 voice, or a dialect speaker at home register. Two or three
grammatical features applied with total consistency, one false-friend or calque event per page,
circumlocution when vocabulary runs out, code-switching only at trigger points. Still zero
respelling, or the single licensed family.

**Heavy** — low proficiency *is* the plot; use briefly. Add dropped copulas and auxiliaries,
simplified tense anchored by a time adverb ("Yesterday I see him"), heavier circumlocution,
retreat to the L1 when another bilingual is present. Hard floor: never incomprehensible, never
every sentence marked. The comprehension cost must buy plot value, or it is just friction.

**Movement:** stress, fatigue, or fury pushes the dial one notch heavier; a rehearsed professional
domain pulls it one notch lighter. Spec both directions in the voice.md Under Pressure section.

The same line at three settings — French L1, a woman confronting the friend who left her waiting:

> **Light:** "I've been waiting since eight o'clock. Two hours. Why didn't you call me? ...Bon.
> Forget it — it's not grave."
>
> **Medium:** "I am waiting since eight o'clock — two hours. Why you didn't call me? Honestly, I
> am very deceived."
>
> **Heavy:** "Since eight o'clock I wait. Two hours! Why you don't call? I have not the — the
> *word* for this. Tu sais quoi — forget it. You have deceived me tonight."

What moved: light spends its whole budget on one calque ("it's not grave") and a tic. Medium runs
three consistent grammar features — present-for-perfect with *since*, question without do-support,
the false friend *deceived*. Heavy adds fronted time, do-less negation, a vocabulary stall, and
one motivated L1 ejecta at the emotional peak. All three stay fully comprehensible, and heavy is
one scene's spike, not the character's constant.

## The Auteur Exception

The grammar-first default has one honest exception class: writers rendering their own community
with total phonetic commitment. Katori Hall writes Mercedes in *P-Valley* as "Had Henney, a blunt
and a nut. I'on need nuthin' else"; August Wilson scored Black American speech across a ten-play
cycle the same way. This is an auteur register, and it works for reasons that do not transfer: the
writer is transcribing music they grew up inside, with insider authority, and the commitment is
absolute — every character, every page, no standard-spelled "normal" voice standing beside the
marked one as a silent baseline.

That last condition is load-bearing. One respelled character in a standard-spelled cast reads as
mockery, because the respelling becomes a costume only one person has to wear. So these skills
default to grammar, syntax, and idiom; full phonetic rendering happens only when the writer
explicitly opts in, owns or has deeply researched the community, and commits everywhere. If any
condition fails, render the same voice through the AAVE grammar kit below — the grammar carries
everything the spelling did.

## French L1 — Deep Kit

The flagship kit, fully worked so the anatomy is visible; the quick kits compress the same
structure. French transfer clusters predictably: a compound past tense (passé composé) bleeding
into English perfects, no do-support anywhere, an article-hungry grammar, and a large stock of
near-English words that mean something else. Any one character uses a personal subset — see the
note at the end.

**Tense & aspect**
1. Present for present-perfect-continuous — "I am cooking since early this morning." (French uses
   present + *depuis*.)
2. Passé-composé bleed — "I have seen him yesterday." (*J'ai vu* covers both simple past and
   perfect.)
3. Perfect for past-perfect — "She have gone home before I arrive."
4. Tense collapse under pressure — "I am sleeping all day yesterday."
5. Future after when/if — "When I will arrive, I call you." (French requires future after *quand*.)

**Questions, negation, auxiliaries**
6. Intonation questions, no do-support — "You want to go to the movies?"
7. Inversion without *do* — "How often go you there?" (*Vas-tu* pattern.)
8. Do-less negation — "I have not the time." "She comes not." (*Ne...pas* wraps the verb directly.)
9. Universal tag "isn't it?" — "You come tomorrow, isn't it?" (*N'est-ce pas* never varies.)
10. "Si!" to contradict a negative — "You don't like it?" "Si! I mean — yes, I like it!" (French
    needs a third word to reverse a negative question; it escapes under emotion.)

**Word order**
11. Adverb between verb and object — "I eat always at that restaurant." "I go often to Paris."
12. Adjective after noun, as a fatigue slip — "a car red."
13. Adverbial stacking — "I will explain you tomorrow the problem."

**Articles, possessives, pronouns**
14. Articles on abstracts and generics — "The life is beautiful." "I love the nature."
15. Article + possessive pile-up — "This is the Karen's dog."
16. Possessive agrees with the thing owned — "Chad and her wife like board games." (*Son/sa* track
    the noun's gender, not the owner's.)
17. Gendered pronouns for objects — "The car? She is broken again." (*La voiture*, feminine.)
18. Dative calques — "Explain me the problem." "I telephoned to him."

**Calques** (idiom carried word for word — the highest-value French flavor: fluent-sounding,
unmistakably French)
19. *Avoir* states — "I have 30 years." "I have hunger." "I have fear."
20. Cleft habit — "It's me who is right." "It's her who said it." (French clefts constantly.)
21. Hedging formula — "We can say that it was a disaster." (*On peut dire que.*)
22. "How do you call this?" — for "what do you call this."
23. "I am agree." — (*Être d'accord* drags in *am*.)
24. Do/make swaps from *faire* — "Make attention!"
25. Preposition transfer — "I profit of the occasion." "It depends of you."

**False friends** (the character says one thing and means the French thing — misunderstanding as a
scene engine)
26. *actually* = currently — "Actually I live in Lyon."
27. *library* = bookstore — "I bought it at the library."
28. *deceive* = disappoint — "You have deceived me."
29. *assist* = attend — "I assisted to the meeting."
30. *attend* = wait for — "I am attending my sister."
31. *coin* = corner — "the coin of the street."
32. *demand* = ask — "He demanded me my name." (Accidental rudeness — gold in scenes.)
33. *sensible* = sensitive — "She is very sensible, she cries easily."
34. *eventually* = possibly — "Eventually we could take the train?"
35. *pass an exam* = take it — "I passed the exam, but I failed it."
36. *rest* = stay — "I rested at home all day."
37. *sympathetic* = nice — "Your friend is very sympathetic."

**Countability & discourse tics**
38. Mass nouns pluralize — "informations," "advices," "furnitures." (*Des informations* is plural.)
39. Light-setting tics — "voilà," "bon," "hein?", "no?", "how to say..." — cheap, respectful,
    effective.

**Picking a personal subset.** No real speaker produces all 39. Give each character 3–5 signature
features (the voice.md cap) plus one false friend held in reserve for a scene where the
misunderstanding matters. Choose by proficiency and personality: a professor keeps the calques and
clefts but never loses an article; an exhausted line cook shows tense collapse and word searches.
The subset is the character. The kit is only the menu.

## Quick L1 Kits

Same anatomy as the French kit, compressed. The kits differ on purpose: each L1 leaves its own
fingerprint, and giving a Spanish speaker Russian-style article deletion is a detectable error.

**Spanish L1**
1. Intonation questions — "You are interested in learning English?"
2. "I am agree" — (*estar de acuerdo*.)
3. Adjective after noun — "a puppy cute."
4. Preposition transfer — "They will meet in Wednesday." (*En* covers both in and on.)
5. Introduction calque — "Let me present you my friend." (*Presentar* = introduce.)
6. Formal fullness, missing contractions — "I do not like juice," said casually.
7. Pro-drop subjects — "Is raining." "Is important to arrive early." (No dummy *it* exists.)
8. "The people is angry" — (*la gente* is singular.)
9. *Tener* states — "She has 20 years." "I have hunger."
10. Double negative — "I didn't see nothing." (*No vi nada* — concord is obligatory in Spanish.)
11. False friends — *embarrassed/embarazada* (pregnant), *actually* = currently, *exit/éxito*
    (success), *carpet/carpeta* (folder), *sensible* = sensitive.

**Russian L1**
1. Articles vanish — "I am good person." (Russian has none; expect drops and misplacements both.)
2. Missing infinitive *to* — "I want learn English."
3. Future inside if-clauses — "If I will see him, I will tell him."
4. "Too" for "either" — "I don't like this movie too."
5. Preposition transfer — "I called to John."
6. "I feel myself bad" — (the Russian verb is reflexive.)
7. "The reason because" — "That's the reason because I left."
8. Copula drop — "He doctor. She very smart." (No present-tense *to be* — the strongest single
   Russian marker at medium.)
9. "I very like this" — (adverb placement from *очень*.)
10. Mass plurals — "advices," "moneys."
11. "How to say..." — vocabulary-search filler; pairs naturally with circumlocution.

**German L1**
1. Since/for — "She lives since three years in London."
2. Perfect for narrative past — "I have seen the film last week."
3. Present for future — "I tell her when I see her."
4. Verb–object separation (V2 order) — "He plays very often football."
5. Time before place — "I drink every morning a tea." "She came with the bus to school."
6. False friends — "Where do I become a ticket?" (*bekommen* = get); "He will go home" meaning
   wants to (*wollen*).
7. Article slips both ways — "He wants to be teacher," but "The life is hard."
8. Adjective as adverb — "She dances very good."
9. "What" for "which" — "He refused to help, what surprised me." (German *was*.)
10. Gendered pronoun slips — "Jane took the spoon and threw him on the floor." (*Der Löffel*,
    masculine.)
11. Invariant tag "or?" — "You are coming, or?" (*Oder?* — a cheap light-setting marker.)

**Mandarin L1**
1. No plural inflection — "There are many dog."
2. No third-person -s — "He like books."
3. No tense inflection; time from context — "She run yesterday." "I finish already." (*Already*
   doing the work of 了.)
4. He/she interchange in speech — "I met a new friend. His name is Mary." (他/她 are both *tā*.)
5. Articles dropped or misapplied — "I am in supermarket."
6. Yes/no answers track agreement, not polarity — "You don't like him, do you?" "Yes, I don't."
7. Topic-comment frames — "This restaurant, very delicious."
8. Verb-mapping calques — "Open the light." "I'll carry you to the airport." (开 and 带 each cover
   several English verbs.)
9. Paired conjunctions — "Although he's not well, but he insists." "Because he's tired, so he
   skips."
10. Containment prepositions — "Alice is in the bus."

**Arabic L1**
1. Copula drop — "He happy." "The report ready." (No present-tense *to be*.)
2. Definite article overuse — "The life is beautiful." (*Al-* attaches far more widely.)
3. No indefinite article — "I bought car."
4. Stative progressive — "I am knowing him for years."
5. Since/for — "I live here since 2 years."
6. Age with have — "I have 20 years."
7. Pronoun gender slips — "My sister, he is smart."
8. Emphasis-first order — "Very I like football."
9. Verb calques — "Open the TV." "Make homework." (No do/make split.)
10. Resumptive pronouns in relatives — "The man who I saw him yesterday is here."
11. "And...and" chaining — *wa-* coordination strings clauses long in excited speech.

## Indian English

Not an interference kit. Indian English is a legitimate, systematic, institutionalized variety — a
national standard with its own press, courts, and literature — so write it as a dialect with
internal rules, exactly like AAVE or Scots, never as an error list. Its speakers command a range
from street to high-formal, and the formality is register, not error.

1. Progressive with statives — "I am understanding it." "She is knowing the answer."
2. Invariant tags — "You're going, isn't it?" "He's here, no?"
3. Focus particles *only* and *itself* — "I was in Toronto only." "Can we meet tomorrow itself?"
   (Emphasis patterns from Hindi *hī/bhī*.)
4. Wh-questions without inversion — "What you would like to eat?"
5. Reduplication for intensity or distribution — "small small things." "Come come! Sit sit!"
6. Agreement answers — "You didn't come on the bus?" "Yes, I didn't."
7. Preposition patterns — "pay attention on," "discuss about."
8. *Since* with present — "I am working here since 2019."
9. Lexicon — *prepone* (move earlier), *do the needful*, *out of station*, *good name* ("What is
   your good name?"), *pass out* (graduate), *revert* (reply), *cousin-brother*.
10. Formality floor — "kindly," "respected sir": sounds bookish to US ears and is neither error
    nor affectation.

## Native-Variety Kits

Every kit below is a rule-governed grammar with internal constraints — a fully productive system,
not an incomplete copy of the standard. That cuts both ways: the varieties deserve the respect of
accuracy, and the caricature is not using the dialect but getting its rules wrong. In-group
readers clock a violated constraint instantly.

**AAVE (African American Vernacular English)**
Register note: no speaker uses every feature all the time — write the job-interview voice and the
family-kitchen voice as one competence shifting by audience.
1. Habitual *be* — "He be working" = works habitually, regularly. A right-now event is "He
   working." Using *be* for right-now writes the stereotype instead of the grammar.
2. Copula absence — "She at home." "He talking about it." Deletable only where Standard English
   can contract ('s/'re): never first-person *am*, never past tense, never sentence-final.
3. Completive *done* — "I done cooked the turkey" = have already, have just cooked.
4. Remote stressed *BIN* — "She BIN married" = married long ago and still is. (Outsiders misread
   it as "was married once.")
5. Negative concord and negative inversion — "Nobody can't see nothing." "Can't nobody beat 'em."
   (The auxiliary fronts over the subject.)
6. Existential *it* — "It's a lot of people out there" = there are.
7. Third-person -s absence — "He walk." "She have a new car." "He don't."
8. Aspect suite — *finna* (imminent: "He finna go"), *steady* (persistent-intense: "steady
   steppin"), indignant *come* ("He come walking in here like he own the place"), *be done*
   (future perfect: "She be done had her baby").
9. *Ain't* covers be-negation, haven't, and didn't — "I ain't believe you that day" = didn't.
10. Possession by juxtaposition — "the boy hat," alternating freely with -'s.

**Southern US**
Register note: the deposition voice and the porch voice belong to the same speaker; the shift is
skill, not slippage.
1. *Y'all* — a real second-person-plural paradigm: possessive "y'all's," distributive "all y'all."
2. *Fixin' to* — "I'm fixin' to wash the dishes" = about to, with preparatory lead time; takes
   inanimates and weather: "That chair's fixin' to break." "There's fixin' to be trouble."
3. Double modals — "We might could get a bus." "You might should call her." "I useta could."
   (Hedged possibility, politeness softening.)
4. Perfective *done* — "I done told you twice."
5. *Liketa* — "I liketa died laughing" = nearly.
6. Personal dative — "I got me a new truck." "She bought her a house."
7. Was-leveling — "We was there all night."
8. Negative concord with *ain't* — "Ain't nobody got time for that."
9. Lexicon — "over yonder," "I reckon," "bless your heart" (weaponizable); a-prefixing for older
   rural voices ("a-huntin'" — an apostrophe that counts against the respelling budget).

**New York City**
Register note: mostly a phonology-and-attitude dialect — carry it in lexicon, rhythm, and
discourse, and put the sound in narration ("that Queens honk"); the "Joizey/goil" respelling
tradition is the caricature to avoid.
1. "Stand on line," not in line — the cleanest NYC grammatical shibboleth.
2. "On accident" for "by accident" (some speakers).
3. Indirect questions keep question order — "He wanted to know when will he come."
4. *Youse / youse guys* — older, outer-borough, working-class coding.
5. Yiddish-influenced discourse — fronting ("A genius, he's not."), "Enough already," "Go figure,"
   "So sue me."
6. Lexicon — stoop, bodega, hero, schlep, schmear, egg cream, "the City" meaning Manhattan.
7. Rhythm — fast and overlapping; interruption as engagement, not rudeness; questions answered
   with questions.

**London: Cockney → MLE**
Register note: one continuum, two generations — place the character by age and scene, and let
them shift by company.
1. Invariant *innit* — "He's late, innit" — extends far beyond isn't-it contexts; also "you get
   me."
2. Pronoun *man* — "Man's been waiting here for an hour." (Young MLE; generic or first person.)
3. New quotative — "This is me: 'I'm not paying that.'"
4. Negation set — *ain't*; third-person *don't* ("She don't care"); negative concord; preterite
   *never* ("I never took it" = didn't take it, that one time).
5. Intensifiers — *bare* ("bare people" = lots of), *proper* ("proper annoyed").
6. *Them* as demonstrative — "them things"; was-leveling ("we was") — the Cockney continuity
   running under MLE.
7. Cockney classics for older voices — *me* for *my* ("me mum"), rhyming slang ("dog and bone" =
   phone): deploy one, as a wink, or it turns costume.
8. Caribbean-substrate lexicon — *ends* (neighborhood), *mandem*, *wasteman*, *peak*, *allow it*
   (let it go). Dates fast — see Failure Modes.

**Irish English (Hiberno-English)**
Register note: Irish-language machinery running under English — it thickens at home and thins in
formal rooms, and both settings are the same competence.
1. After-perfect (hot news) — "I'm after losing me wallet" = I have *just* lost it. "He was after
   insulting me." (Calque of Irish *tar éis*; outsiders misread it as pursuit.)
2. Habitual *do be* — "He does be coding every day." "They do be talking for hours."
3. Echo answers instead of yes/no — "Are you finished?" "I am." "Is it charged?" "It is." (Irish
   has no words for yes and no.)
4. Clefts and unbound reflexives — "'Tis herself that's coming now." *Himself/herself* alone = the
   boss, the person of the house.
5. *Amn't* — "I'm making a mistake, amn't I?"
6. Plural you — *ye*, *yous/yiz* — "Did ye all go?"
7. Medial-object perfect — "I have the computer rebooted." "I've it done." (A result state, not an
   error.)
8. Affirming tag — "This box is banjaxed, so it is."
9. Discourse set — *sure* ("Sure you know yourself"), clause-final *so* ("I will, so"), *your man*
   (that guy over there), *give out to* (scold), *grand*, *craic*.

**Scottish English / Scots**
Register note: Scots and Scottish Standard English sit on a continuum — pick a point per
character, hold it, and move only for audience and formality.
1. Clitic negation *-nae/-na* — "Ye cannae say that." "I canna mind." "I couldna." (The one
   licensed respelling family.)
2. *No/nae* after contractions and in questions — "She's no guid." "Can ye no see it?"
3. Double modals — "We micht can get a bus." "You used to could go through the estate."
4. Northern Subject Rule — plural subjects take -s unless the subject is an adjacent pronoun:
   "Thae bairns is awfie quiet." "My feet's gey sair." (But: "they are.")
5. Three-way demonstratives — this / that / *thon*; plural *thae* — "thon yin ower there."
6. Definite article where English omits — "the day" (today), "the morn," "the cauld," "the rugby."
7. "Needs washed" — "The car needs washed." "That needs seen to."
8. Lexicon — *yous* (urban west), "How no?" (why not), *outwith*, *wee* as default diminutive,
   *ken* (know), "dinnae fash" (don't worry).
9. Scots preterites at medium and above — *telt* (told), *kent* (knew), *bocht* (bought).

## Dosage & Failure Modes

The algorithm, for any origin layer at any dial setting:

1. **Establish hard in the character's first two or three speeches** — two grammar features plus
   one lexical item. First impressions set the reader's inner ear; everything after is
   maintenance.
2. **Taper to a maintenance dose** — roughly one marked element per three or four lines, rhythm
   carrying the rest. The ear, once set, supplies the accent in unmarked lines for free.
3. **Re-spike at emotional peaks and code-switch boundaries** — the call home, the compatriot at
   the door, rage, exhaustion. The spike is characterization: the reader watches self-monitoring
   fail in real time.
4. **One respelling family maximum, or none.** Apostrophe litter counts.
5. **Audit every ten pages:** read the character's lines alone, in sequence. All marked — cut a
   third. None marked — the voice has evaporated; re-seed the next scene.

Failure modes, named so a review pass can catch them:

- **Eye-dialect gravity** — drifting into "wuz," "sez," "goin'" under the illusion of texture.
  Respelling standard pronunciations transmits nothing but status.
- **Error overload** — density past comprehension; the reader starts skipping the character's
  lines, which is death.
- **Feature salad** — the whole kit at once. Real speakers run a personal subset; 3–5 signatures
  per character.
- **Rule-violation caricature** — habitual *be* on a right-now event, the after-perfect written as
  pursuit, "innit" after every sentence. Wrong dialect grammar reads as fraud to every in-group
  reader at once.
- **Flat dial** — identical marker density in the job interview and the bar fight. No movement
  means no code-switching means cardboard.
- **Comedy coding** — the accent as the punchline or the IQ signal. The oldest failure, and the
  one this whole method exists to prevent.
- **L1 confetti** — unmotivated foreign-word sprinkles ("¡Dios mío!" as seasoning). Switching
  without a trigger.
- **Universal-donor mistakes** — generic "foreign" errors pasted onto any nationality, like a
  French character dropping articles Russian-style. Each L1 has a fingerprint; the kits exist to
  keep them distinct.
- **Costume lexicon** — "bodega and stoop" or "wee and bonnie" draped over fully standard syntax.
  Vocabulary without grammar reads as tourism; grammar features are the load-bearing wall.
  Related: slang fossilizes fast (MLE and AAVE lexicon especially) — grammar ages better than
  vocabulary.
